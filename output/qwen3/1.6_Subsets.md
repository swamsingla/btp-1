## Introduction  
Understanding subsets is fundamental to working with sets, as it helps us describe relationships between different collections of elements. This section explores the concept of subsets, including how to determine if one set is a subset of another, the distinction between proper and improper subsets, and special cases like the empty set. By the end, you’ll be able to identify subsets, represent them using symbols, and apply these ideas to solve problems.  

---  

## Explanation  

### **Definition of Subset**  
A set **A** is a **subset** of set **B** (written as $ A \subset B $) if **every element of A is also an element of B**. This means:  
> **If $ a \in A $, then $ a \in B $.**  

This is equivalent to saying:  
> **A is a subset of B if and only if all elements of A belong to B.**  

### **Symbols and Notation**  
- $ \subset $: "is a subset of" or "is contained in".  
- $ \not\subset $: "is not a subset of".  
- $ \subseteq $: Sometimes used interchangeably with $ \subset $, but strictly speaking, $ \subseteq $ includes the possibility of equality (i.e., $ A \subseteq B $ means $ A \subset B $ or $ A = B $).  

### **Special Cases**  
1. **Empty Set**: The empty set $ \phi $ is a subset of **every set**, including itself.  
2. **Equality of Sets**: If $ A \subset B $ and $ B \subset A $, then $ A = B $.  
3. **Proper Subset**: If $ A \subset B $ and $ A \neq B $, then $ A $ is a **proper subset** of $ B $.  

### **Examples from the Text**  
- $ \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R} $: Rational numbers are a subset of real numbers.  
- Let $ A = \{1, 3, 5\} $ and $ B = \{x : x \text{ is an odd natural number less than } 6\} $. Then $ A \subset B $ and $ B \subset A $, so $ A = B $.  
- If $ A = \{a, e, i, o, u\} $ and $ B = \{a, b, c, d\} $, neither $ A \subset B $ nor $ B \subset A $.

## Solved Examples  

### **Example 1**  
Determine whether $ \{1, 2\} \subset \{1, 2, 3\} $.  
**Step 1**: Check if all elements of $ \{1, 2\} $ are in $ \{1, 2, 3\} $.  
- $ 1 \in \{1, 2, 3\} $, and $ 2 \in \{1, 2, 3\} $.  
**Conclusion**: Yes, $ \{1, 2\} \subset \{1, 2, 3\} $.  

### **Example 2**  
Is $ \{2, 4\} \subset \{2, 3, 4\} $?  
**Step 1**: Verify elements of $ \{2, 4\} $ in $ \{2, 3, 4\} $.  
- $ 2 \in \{2, 3, 4\} $, and $ 4 \in \{2, 3, 4\} $.  
**Conclusion**: Yes, $ \{2, 4\} \subset \{2, 3, 4\} $.  

### **Example 3**  
Check if $ \{a\} \subset \{a, b\} $.  
**Step 1**: Confirm $ a \in \{a, b\} $.  
**Conclusion**: Yes, $ \{a\} \subset \{a, b\} $.

## Practice Problems  

1. **🟢 Easy**: Is $ \{1\} \subset \{1, 2\} $?  
2. **🟡 Medium**: Let $ A = \{2, 4, 6\} $ and $ B = \{1, 2, 3, 4\} $. Determine if $ A \subset B $.  
3. **🔴 Hard**: Prove that $ \phi \subset A $ for any set $ A $.  

**Answers**:  
1. Yes.  
2. No, since $ 6 \notin B $.  
3. Since $ \phi $ has no elements, the condition $ a \in \phi \Rightarrow a \in A $ is vacuously true.  

---  

## Summary  
- **Subset Definition**: $ A \subset B $ iff every element of $ A $ belongs to $ B $.  
- **Proper Subset**: $ A \subset B $ and $ A \neq B $.  
- **Empty Set Property**: $ \phi \subset A $ for any set $ A $.  
- **Equality Condition**: $ A = B $ iff $ A \subset B $ and $ B \subset A $.  
- **Symbol Usage**: Use $ \subset $ for subset, $ \not\subset $ for non-subset.

## Student Corner  

### **Common Mistakes**  
- Confusing **subset** with **proper subset**: Remember, $ A \subset B $ includes the case where $ A = B $.  
- Forgetting that the **empty set** is a subset of all sets—even if it seems counterintuitive.  

### **Exam Tip**  
When verifying $ A \subset B $, always check **all elements** of $ A $ against $ B $. Even one missing element makes the relation false.  

### **Memory Aid**  
**"All elements in A must live in B."** – Mnemonic for recalling the subset definition.
