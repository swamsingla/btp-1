## Introduction  
Real numbers form a vast set that includes various special subsets, such as natural numbers, integers, and rational numbers. This section explores how these subsets relate to each other and their unique properties. By the end of this topic, you’ll be able to identify subsets of real numbers, describe their characteristics, and analyze their hierarchical relationships.  

---

## Explanation  
### **Key Terms Defined**  
- **Natural Numbers (N)**: The set of positive integers used for counting.  
- **Integers (Z)**: The set containing all whole numbers, including negatives, zero, and positives.  
- **Rational Numbers (Q)**: Numbers expressible as a fraction $ \frac{p}{q} $, where $ p, q \in \mathbb{Z} $ and $ q \neq 0 $.  
- **Irrational Numbers (T)**: Real numbers that cannot be expressed as fractions; they are **not** in Q.  

### **Subset Relationships**  
> **The set of natural numbers is a subset of integers**:

$$ N \subset Z

$$  
> **The set of integers is a subset of rational numbers**:

$$ Z \subset Q

$$  
> **All rational numbers are real numbers**:

$$ Q \subset R

$$  
> **All irrational numbers are real numbers**:

$$ T \subset R

$$  
> **There is no overlap between natural numbers and irrational numbers**:

$$ N \not\subset T

$$  

### **Properties of Subsets**  
- A **subset** $ A $ of a set $ B $ means every element of $ A $ is also in $ B $.  
- **Rational numbers** include terminating decimals (e.g., $ 0.5 $) and repeating decimals (e.g., $ 0.\overline{3} $).  
- **Irrational numbers** include non-repeating, non-terminating decimals (e.g., $ \pi $, $ \sqrt{2} $).  

---

## Solved Examples  
**Example 1:** Determine whether $ \sqrt{9} $ is in $ N $, $ Z $, $ Q $, or $ T $.  
**Solution:**  
- $ \sqrt{9} = 3 $, which is a natural number.  
- Thus, $ \sqrt{9} \in N \subset Z \subset Q $.  
- Final Answer: $ \sqrt{9} \in N $, $ Z $, and $ Q $.  

**Example 2:** Classify $ -\frac{2}{3} $ as belonging to $ N $, $ Z $, $ Q $, or $ T $.  
**Solution:**  
- $ -\frac{2}{3} $ is a fraction with integer numerator and denominator.  
- Hence, it belongs to $ Q $.  
- Final Answer: $ -\frac{2}{3} \in Q $.  

**Example 3:** Is $ \pi $ in $ Q $? Justify.  
**Solution:**  
- $ \pi $ is a well-known irrational number.  
- By definition, $ \pi \notin Q $, so it belongs to $ T $.  
- Final Answer: $ \pi \in T $.  

**Example 4:** Verify the relationship $ N \subset Z \subset Q $.  
**Solution:**  
- All natural numbers (e.g., 1, 2, 3) are integers.  
- All integers (e.g., -1, 0, 2) can be written as fractions (e.g., $ -1 = \frac{-1}{1} $, $ 0 = \frac{0}{1} $).  
- Thus, $ N \subset Z \subset Q $ holds true.  
- Final Answer: Verified.

## Practice Problems  
**Problem 1 (🟢 Easy):**  
Which of the following numbers belong to $ N $?  
(a) $ \sqrt{16} $  
(b) $ -3 $  
(c) $ \frac{5}{2} $  
(d) $ \pi $  

**Problem 2 (🟡 Medium):**  
Prove that $ Z \subset Q $.  

**Problem 3 (🔴 Hard):**  
If $ A = \{ x \in \mathbb{R} : x \text{ is irrational} \} $, show that $ A \subset \mathbb{R} $ and $ A \cap Q = \emptyset $.  

---

## Answers  
**Problem 1:**  
(a) $ \sqrt{16} = 4 \in N $  
(b) $ -3 \notin N $  
(c) $ \frac{5}{2} \notin N $  
(d) $ \pi \notin N $  

**Problem 2:**  
**Solution:**  
Every integer $ z \in Z $ can be expressed as $ \frac{z}{1} $, where $ z \in \mathbb{Z} $ and $ 1 \neq 0 $. Therefore, $ Z \subset Q $.  

**Problem 3:**  
**Solution:**  
- By definition, $ A \subset \mathbb{R} $ because all irrational numbers are real numbers.  
- $ A \cap Q = \emptyset $ because $ A $ contains only numbers not in $ Q $.

## Summary  
- **Natural numbers** ($ N $) are positive integers.  
- **Integers** ($ Z $) include negatives, zero, and positives.  
- **Rational numbers** ($ Q $) are fractions with integer numerators/denominators.  
- **Irrational numbers** ($ T $) cannot be expressed as fractions.  
- Subset relationships: $ N \subset Z \subset Q \subset \mathbb{R} $, $ T \subset \mathbb{R} $.  
- Rational numbers include terminating/repeating decimals; irrationals include non-repeating/non-terminating decimals.  

---

## Key Formulas  
| Formula | Description |  
|--------|-------------|  
| $ N \subset Z \subset Q $ | Natural numbers are a subset of integers, which are a subset of rationals. |  
| $ Q \subset \mathbb{R} $ | Rational numbers are a subset of real numbers. |  
| $ T \subset \mathbb{R} $ | Irrational numbers are a subset of real numbers. |  
| $ N \not\subset T $ | Natural numbers are disjoint from irrational numbers. |

## Student Corner  
### **Common Mistakes**  
- Confusing **integers** ($ Z $) with **rational numbers** ($ Q $): Not all integers are rational, but all integers are rational!  
- Assuming $ \pi $ or $ \sqrt{2} $ are rational: These are classic examples of **irrational numbers**.  

### **Memory Aid**  
Use the acronym **NIZQT** to remember the hierarchy:  
**N**atural → **I**nteger → **Z**ational → **Q**uotient → **T**errible (irrationals).
