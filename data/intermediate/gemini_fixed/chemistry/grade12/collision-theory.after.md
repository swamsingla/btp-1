# Collision Theory

Collision theory is a fundamental concept in chemistry that explains how chemical reactions actually occur at the molecular level. You may already know that the rate of a chemical reaction depends on factors like temperature and concentration. However, collision theory, developed by Max Trautz and William Lewis in 1916-1918, provides a greater insight into the energetic and mechanistic aspects of these reactions [S1]. It is based on the kinetic theory of gases, according to which the reactant molecules are assumed to be hard spheres [S1]. When these molecules collide with each other, they might react to form products, but only if certain conditions are met. Understanding these conditions helps us predict how fast a reaction will proceed and why some reactions happen instantly while others take a very long time.

## The Collision Frequency

According to collision theory, reaction is postulated to occur when molecules collide with each other [S1]. The number of collisions per second per unit volume of the reaction mixture is known as the collision frequency, denoted by the symbol $Z$ [S1]. For a bimolecular elementary reaction, such as $A + B \rightarrow$ Products, the rate of reaction can be expressed as $\text{Rate} = Z_{AB} e^{-E_a / RT}$ [S1]. Here, $Z_{AB}$ represents the collision frequency of the reactants A and B, and the exponential term $e^{-E_a / RT}$ represents the fraction of molecules with energies equal to or greater than $E_a$ [S1]. 

Comparing this with the Arrhenius equation, we can say that $A$ is related to the collision frequency [S1]. This means that a higher collision frequency generally leads to a faster reaction rate, assuming all other factors remain constant. However, not every collision results in a chemical reaction. This brings us to the critical concept of activation energy.

## Activation Energy

Activation energy, often denoted as $E_a$, is another factor which affects the rate of chemical reactions [S1]. Even when molecules collide, they will not react unless they possess sufficient kinetic energy, which is referred to as the threshold energy. The threshold energy is defined as the activation energy plus the energy possessed by the reacting species [S1]. 

The exponential term $e^{-E_a / RT}$ in the rate equation represents the fraction of molecules that have energies equal to or greater than the activation energy $E_a$ [S1]. This fraction is highly sensitive to temperature. As the temperature $T$ increases, the value of $e^{-E_a / RT}$ increases, meaning a larger fraction of molecules have energies equal to or greater than $E_a$. This explains why reactions generally proceed faster at higher temperatures. It is important to note that while the Arrhenius equation is applicable under a wide range of circumstances, collision theory provides a greater insight into the energetic and mechanistic aspects of reactions [S1].

## Effective Collisions

For a collision to be successful, it must be an effective collision. The collisions in which molecules collide with sufficient kinetic energy (called threshold energy) and proper orientation, so as to facilitate breaking of bonds between reacting species and formation of new bonds to form products are called as effective collisions [S1]. Proper orientation means that the molecules must be aligned in a specific way to facilitate the breaking of bonds between the reacting species and the formation of new bonds to create products [S1]. 

If the molecules collide with improper orientation, they simply bounce back and no products are formed. For example, the formation of methanol from bromoethane depends upon the orientation of reactant molecules [S1]. Proper orientation leads to bond formation, whereas improper orientation makes them simply bounce back and no products are formed. To account for this, collision theory introduces another factor called the probability or steric factor, denoted by $P$. This factor takes into account the fact that in a collision, molecules must be properly oriented [S1].

## The Role of the Steric Factor

To account for effective collisions, another factor $P$, called the probability or steric factor is introduced [S1]. It takes into account the fact that in a collision, molecules must be properly oriented, i.e., $\text{Rate} = P Z_{AB} e^{-E_a / RT}$ [S1]. Thus, in collision theory, activation energy and proper orientation of the molecules together determine the criteria for an effective collision and hence the rate of a chemical reaction [S1].

The steric factor highlights that chemical reactions are not just about energy; they are also about geometry. Molecules have specific shapes and functional groups, and these must align correctly for a reaction to proceed. For instance, the proper orientation of reactant molecules in the formation of methanol from bromoethane leads to bond formation, whereas improper orientation makes them simply bounce back and no products are formed [S1].

## Limitations of Collision Theory

While collision theory provides valuable insights, it does have certain limitations. It considers atoms and molecules to be hard spheres and ignores their structural aspects [S1]. This simplification works reasonably well for reactions involving atomic species or simple molecules, but for complex molecules, significant deviations are observed [S1]. 

The reason could be that all collisions do not lead to the formation of products [S1]. Therefore, collision theory is most applicable to simple reactions and provides a foundational understanding of chemical kinetics. You will study details about this theory and more on other theories in your higher classes [S1].

## Worked Example

Consider a bimolecular elementary reaction between molecules A and B. The collision frequency $Z_{AB}$ is $10^9 \, \text{M}^{-1} \text{s}^{-1}$. The activation energy $E_a$ is $50 \, \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$, and the temperature $T$ is $300 \, \text{K}$. The steric factor $P$ is $0.1$. 

First, we calculate the fraction of molecules with energies equal to or greater than $E_a$ using the exponential term. The gas constant $R$ is $8.314 \, \text{J mol}^{-1} \text{K}^{-1}$. The exponent is $-E_a / RT = -50000 / (8.314 \times 300) \approx -20.05$. The exponential term $e^{-20.05}$ is approximately $2.0 \times 10^{-9}$.

Next, we calculate the rate of reaction using the formula $\text{Rate} = P Z_{AB} e^{-E_a / RT}$. Substituting the values, we get $\text{Rate} = 0.1 \times 10^9 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-9} = 0.2 \, \text{M s}^{-1}$. 

This example illustrates how the rate of reaction is influenced by the collision frequency, activation energy, and steric factor. A higher activation energy or a lower steric factor would result in a slower reaction rate, while a higher temperature would increase the fraction of molecules with energies equal to or greater than $E_a$, thereby increasing the rate.

## Key points

- Collision theory explains that chemical reactions occur when reactant molecules collide with each other [S1].
- The collision frequency $Z_{AB}$ is the number of collisions per second per unit volume of the reaction mixture [S1].
- Activation energy $E_a$ is another factor which affects the rate of chemical reactions, and the fraction of molecules with energies equal to or greater than $E_a$ is represented by $e^{-E_a / RT}$ [S1].
- Effective collisions require both sufficient kinetic energy (threshold energy) and proper orientation of the molecules [S1].
- The steric factor $P$ accounts for the fact that in a collision, molecules must be properly oriented [S1].
- The rate of reaction is given by $\text{Rate} = P Z_{AB} e^{-E_a / RT}$ [S1].
- Collision theory considers atoms/molecules to be hard spheres and ignores their structural aspect, leading to significant deviations for complex molecules [S1].
