# Coordination Compounds

Coordination compounds are chemical species where a central metal atom or ion is bound to a fixed number of ions or molecules [S1]. Alfred Werner prepared and characterised a large number of coordination compounds and studied their physical and chemical behaviour [S1].

## Werner's Theory

Alfred Werner proposed that in coordination compounds, metals show two types of linkages or valences: primary and secondary [S1]. The primary valences are normally ionisable and are satisfied by negative ions [S1]. In contrast, the secondary valences are non-ionisable and are satisfied by neutral molecules or negative ions [S1].

Werner's theory was supported by experiments with cobalt(III) chloride and ammonia. When he added silver nitrate to these compounds, he observed that some chloride ions precipitated as silver chloride while others remained in the solution [S1]. This led him to conclude that six groups remain bonded to the cobalt ion during the reaction. He termed the number of groups bound directly to the metal ion as the secondary valence, which is equal to the coordination number and is fixed for a metal [S1].

## Ligands

The ions or molecules bound to the central atom/ion in the coordination entity are called ligands [S1]. These may be simple ions such as $\text{Cl}^-$, small molecules such as $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ or $\text{NH}_3$, larger molecules such as $\text{H}_2\text{NCH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{NH}_2$, or even macromolecules, such as proteins [S1]. When a ligand is bound to a metal ion through a single donor atom, as with $\text{Cl}^-$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, or $\text{NH}_3$, the ligand is said to be unidentate [S1].

When a ligand can bind through two donor atoms as in $\text{H}_2\text{NCH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{NH}_2$ (ethane-1,2-diamine) or $\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-}$ (oxalate), the ligand is said to be didentate and when several donor atoms are present in a single ligand as in $\text{N}(\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{NH}_2)_3$, the ligand is said to be polydentate [S1]. Ethylenediaminetetraacetate ion ($\text{EDTA}^{4-}$) is an important hexadentate ligand. It can bind through two nitrogen and four oxygen atoms to a central metal ion [S1].

When a di- or polydentate ligand uses its two or more donor atoms simultaneously to bind a single metal ion, it is said to be a chelate ligand [S1]. Such complexes, called chelate complexes tend to be more stable than similar complexes containing unidentate ligands [S1]. Ligand which has two different donor atoms and either of the two ligetes in the complex is called ambidentate ligand, such as the $\text{NO}_2^-$ and $\text{SCN}^-$ ions [S1].

## Coordination Number and Polyhedra

The coordination number (CN) of a metal ion in a complex can be defined as the number of ligand donor atoms to which the metal is directly bonded [S1]. For example, in the complex ions, $[\text{PtCl}_6]^{2-}$ and $[\text{Ni}(\text{NH}_3)_4]^{2+}$, the coordination number of Pt and Ni are 6 and 4 respectively [S1].

The spatial arrangement of the ligand atoms which are directly attached to the central atom/ion defines a coordination polyhedron about the central atom [S1]. The most common coordination polyhedra are octahedral, square planar and tetrahedral [S1]. For example, $[\text{Co}(\text{NH}_3)_6]^{3+}$ is octahedral, $[\text{Ni}(\text{CO})_4]$ is tetrahedral and $[\text{PtCl}_4]^{2-}$ is square planar [S1].

## Isomerism

Compounds with identical empirical formula but distinct properties are termed as isomers [S1]. For example, in a series of compounds of cobalt(III) chloride with ammonia, two compounds have the identical empirical formula, $\text{CoCl}_3 \cdot 4\text{NH}_3$, but distinct properties (one is green and the other is violet) [S1].

The ions/groups bound by the secondary linkages to the metal have characteristic spatial arrangements corresponding to different coordination numbers [S1].

## Worked Example

Consider the coordination compound $\text{K}_3[\text{Cr}(\text{C}_2\text{O}_4)_3]$. To determine the oxidation number of chromium, we first note that oxalate ($\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-}$) is a didentate ligand. Since there are three oxalate ligands, the total charge from the ligands is $3 \times (-2) = -6$. The overall charge of the complex ion is $-3$. Therefore, the oxidation number of chromium must be +3 to balance the charges, as $x + (-6) = -3$, which gives $x = +3$. The name of this compound is potassium trioxalatochromate(III) [S1].

## Key points

*   Coordination compounds consist of a central metal atom or ion bonded to a fixed number of ions or molecules.
*   Alfred Werner proposed the concepts of primary and secondary valences to explain the structure of these compounds.
*   Ligands are the ions or molecules bound to the central metal atom or ion.
*   The coordination number is the number of ligand donor atoms directly bonded to the metal.
*   The spatial arrangement of ligands around the central metal ion is called a coordination polyhedron.
*   Isomerism occurs when compounds with the same empirical formula have different properties.
