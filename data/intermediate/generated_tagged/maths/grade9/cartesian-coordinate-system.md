# Cartesian Coordinate System

On a number line, one number is enough to describe the position of a point. There are many other situations in which we need to describe a position with reference to more than one line [S2]. This page shows how two perpendicular number lines let us describe the position of any point in a plane.

## Why Two Pieces of Information Are Needed

Suppose you put a dot on a sheet of paper. Saying "the dot is in the upper half of the paper" or "it is near the left edge" does not fix its position precisely [S2]. If you also say that the dot is nearly 5 cm away from the left edge, it gives some idea but still does not fix the position. If you add that the dot is at a distance of 9 cm above the bottom line, we know exactly where the dot is [S2]. We fixed the position by specifying its distances from two fixed lines, the left edge and the bottom line of the paper, so we need two independent pieces of information [S2].

A seating plan works in the same way. The position of a student is described precisely by the column in which she or he sits and the row in which she or he sits [S2]. A desk in the 5th column and 3rd row can be written as $(5, 3)$, with the column number first and then the row number [S2].

In general, the position of any object lying in a plane can be represented with the help of two perpendicular lines [S2]. This idea gave rise to an important branch of mathematics known as Coordinate Geometry [S2]. It was developed by the French philosopher and mathematician René Descartes, and in his honour the system for describing the position of a point in a plane is also known as the Cartesian system [S2].

## The Axes and the Origin

Take two number lines, call them $X'X$ and $Y'Y$. Place $X'X$ horizontally and write the numbers on it just as on the number line, and do the same with $Y'Y$, except that $Y'Y$ is vertical [S1]. Combine the two lines so that they cross each other at their zeroes, or origins [S1].

The horizontal line $X'X$ is called the $x$-axis and the vertical line $Y'Y$ is called the $y$-axis [S1]. The point where $X'X$ and $Y'Y$ cross is called the origin, and it is denoted by $O$ [S1]. Since the positive numbers lie in the directions $OX$ and $OY$, these are called the positive directions of the $x$-axis and the $y$-axis [S1]. Similarly, $OX'$ and $OY'$ are called the negative directions of the $x$-axis and the $y$-axis [S1].

## Quadrants

The axes divide the plane into four parts, called quadrants, numbered I, II, III and IV anticlockwise from $OX$ [S1]. The plane consists of the axes and these quadrants [S1]. We call the plane the Cartesian plane, or the coordinate plane, or the $xy$-plane, and the axes are called the coordinate axes [S1].

## Coordinates of a Point

Take a point $P$ whose perpendicular distance from the $y$-axis, measured along the positive direction of the $x$-axis, is 4 units, and whose perpendicular distance from the $x$-axis, measured along the positive direction of the $y$-axis, is 3 units. Take a point $Q$ whose distance from the $y$-axis, measured along the negative direction of the $x$-axis, is 6 units, and whose distance from the $x$-axis, measured along the negative direction of the $y$-axis, is 2 units [S1]. To describe such points without confusion, we write their coordinates using these conventions [S1]:

*   The $x$-coordinate of a point is its perpendicular distance from the $y$-axis measured along the $x$-axis, positive along the positive direction of the $x$-axis and negative along the negative direction. For $P$ it is $+4$ and for $Q$ it is $-6$. The $x$-coordinate is also called the abscissa.
*   The $y$-coordinate of a point is its perpendicular distance from the $x$-axis measured along the $y$-axis, positive along the positive direction of the $y$-axis and negative along the negative direction. For $P$ it is $+3$ and for $Q$ it is $-2$. The $y$-coordinate is also called the ordinate.
*   The $x$-coordinate comes first, and then the $y$-coordinate, and we place the coordinates in brackets. Hence the coordinates of $P$ are $(4, 3)$ and the coordinates of $Q$ are $(-6, -2)$.

The coordinates describe a point in the plane uniquely, and $(3, 4)$ is not the same as $(4, 3)$ [S1]. The system of writing the abscissa first is only a convention, accepted all over the world, to avoid confusion [S1].

## Points on the Axes

A point at a distance of $+4$ units from the $y$-axis and at distance zero from the $x$-axis has $x$-coordinate 4 and $y$-coordinate 0, so its coordinates are $(4, 0)$ [S1]. Every point lying on the $x$-axis has zero distance from the $x$-axis, so the $y$-coordinate of every point on the $x$-axis is always zero [S1]. Thus the coordinates of any point on the $x$-axis are of the form $(x, 0)$, where $x$ is the distance of the point from the $y$-axis [S1]. Similarly, the coordinates of any point on the $y$-axis are of the form $(0, y)$, where $y$ is the distance of the point from the $x$-axis [S1].

The origin $O$ has zero distance from both the axes, so its abscissa and ordinate are both zero, and the coordinates of the origin are $(0, 0)$ [S1].

## Signs of the Coordinates in the Quadrants

There is a relationship between the signs of the coordinates of a point and the quadrant in which it lies [S1]:

*   In the 1st quadrant the point is of the form $(+, +)$, since this quadrant is enclosed by the positive $x$-axis and the positive $y$-axis.
*   In the 2nd quadrant the point is of the form $(-, +)$, since it is enclosed by the negative $x$-axis and the positive $y$-axis.
*   In the 3rd quadrant the point is of the form $(-, -)$, since it is enclosed by the negative $x$-axis and the negative $y$-axis.
*   In the 4th quadrant the point is of the form $(+, -)$, since it is enclosed by the positive $x$-axis and the negative $y$-axis.

## Worked Example

A point $R$ is 5 units from the $y$-axis, measured along the negative direction of the $x$-axis, and 2 units from the $x$-axis, measured along the positive direction of the $y$-axis. Find its coordinates.

The $x$-coordinate (abscissa) of $R$ is $-5$, because the distance is measured along the negative direction of the $x$-axis [S1]. The $y$-coordinate (ordinate) of $R$ is $+2$, because the distance is measured along the positive direction of the $y$-axis [S1]. Since the $x$-coordinate comes first, the coordinates of $R$ are $(-5, 2)$ [S1]. The signs $(-, +)$ show that $R$ lies in the 2nd quadrant.

## Key points

*   The position of a point in a plane is described with the help of two perpendicular lines, one horizontal and one vertical [S2].
*   The horizontal line is the $x$-axis and the vertical line is the $y$-axis; they meet at the origin $O$ [S1].
*   The axes divide the plane into four quadrants, numbered I, II, III and IV anticlockwise from $OX$ [S1].
*   The $x$-coordinate, or abscissa, is the distance of a point from the $y$-axis, and the $y$-coordinate, or ordinate, is its distance from the $x$-axis [S1].
*   Coordinates are written in the order $(x, y)$, and $(x, y) \neq (y, x)$ when $x \neq y$ [S1].
*   A point on the $x$-axis has coordinates $(x, 0)$, a point on the $y$-axis has coordinates $(0, y)$, and the origin is $(0, 0)$ [S1].
*   The coordinates have the signs $(+, +)$, $(-, +)$, $(-, -)$ and $(+, -)$ in the 1st, 2nd, 3rd and 4th quadrants [S1].
