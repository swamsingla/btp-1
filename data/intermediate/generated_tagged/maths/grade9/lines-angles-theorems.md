# Lines and Angles — Theorems

When two straight lines meet, they create a set of angles that follow strict, predictable rules. Understanding these rules is essential for solving geometry problems, whether you are calculating the angles in a building's framework or analysing the path of a light beam. This page outlines the core theorems governing the relationships between angles, providing clear explanations and practical examples to help you master these fundamental concepts.

## Linear Pair Axiom

When a single ray stands on a straight line, it divides the line into two adjacent angles. Together, these two angles form a straight line and add up to exactly $180^{\circ}$ [S1]. This relationship is known as the Linear Pair Axiom. 

The axiom works both ways. If the sum of two adjacent angles is $180^{\circ}$, then their non-common arms must lie on the same straight line [S1]. This bidirectional rule is incredibly useful for finding missing angle measurements in diagrams where lines intersect.

**Example:**
Imagine a straight line $AB$ with a ray $OC$ standing at a point $O$ on the line. If $\angle AOC$ measures $110^{\circ}$, you can easily find $\angle BOC$ by using the Linear Pair Axiom. Since the sum of the two adjacent angles must be $180^{\circ}$:
$$ \angle AOC + \angle BOC = 180^{\circ} $$
$$ 110^{\circ} + \angle BOC = 180^{\circ} $$
Subtracting $110^{\circ}$ from $180^{\circ}$ gives $\angle BOC = 70^{\circ}$.

## Vertically Opposite Angles

When two straight lines intersect at a single point, they form two pairs of vertically opposite angles. These are the angles that are directly across from each other at the intersection point [S1]. 

A fundamental theorem states that vertically opposite angles are always equal to each other. For instance, if two lines intersect, the angle on the top left will be exactly equal to the angle on the bottom right, and the angle on the top right will equal the angle on the bottom left [S1].

**Example:**
Consider two lines intersecting at a point $O$. Suppose the angle on the left side of the intersection is $45^{\circ}$. Because this angle and the angle directly opposite it are vertically opposite angles, the angle on the right side of the intersection must also be $45^{\circ}$.

## Angles Around a Point

When multiple rays originate from a single point, they divide the space around that point into several angles. The total measure of all the angles formed around a single point always adds up to $360^{\circ}$, which is the measure of a full circle [S3]. 

This rule applies regardless of how many rays intersect the point. If you have four rays creating four angles around a point, the sum of those four angles will be $360^{\circ}$ [S1].

**Example:**
Imagine a central point $O$ with four rays extending from it, creating four distinct angles: $\angle POQ$, $\angle QOR$, $\angle SOR$, and $\angle POS$. If you know that $\angle POQ = 80^{\circ}$, $\angle QOR = 100^{\circ}$, and $\angle SOR = 120^{\circ}$, you can find the measure of $\angle POS$ by subtracting the sum of the known angles from $360^{\circ}$:
$$ \angle POQ + \angle QOR + \angle SOR + \angle POS = 360^{\circ} $$
$$ 80^{\circ} + 100^{\circ} + 120^{\circ} + \angle POS = 360^{\circ} $$
$$ 300^{\circ} + \angle POS = 360^{\circ} $$
$$ \angle POS = 60^{\circ} $$

## Worked example

Let's apply the concepts of vertically opposite angles and linear pairs to a slightly more complex scenario. Suppose two lines intersect at a point, creating four angles. We are given that one of the angles measures $70^{\circ}$. 

Because vertically opposite angles are equal, the angle directly across from the $70^{\circ}$ angle must also measure $70^{\circ}$ [S1]. The other two angles are adjacent to the $70^{\circ}$ angles, meaning they form a linear pair with them. According to the Linear Pair Axiom, the sum of two adjacent angles on a straight line is $180^{\circ}$ [S1]. 

To find the measure of one of the remaining angles, we subtract the known angle from $180^{\circ}$:
$$ 180^{\circ} - 70^{\circ} = 110^{\circ} $$
Therefore, the other two angles must each measure $110^{\circ}$. You can verify this by adding all four angles together: $70^{\circ} + 110^{\circ} + 70^{\circ} + 110^{\circ} = 360^{\circ}$, which confirms that the angles around the point sum up to $360^{\circ}$ [S1, S3].

## Key points
*   A linear pair consists of two adjacent angles whose non-common arms form a straight line, and their sum is always $180^{\circ}$ [S1].
*   When two lines intersect, vertically opposite angles are equal in measure [S1].
*   The sum of all angles formed around a single point is exactly $360^{\circ}$ [S1, S3].
*   If two lines are parallel to the same line, they are parallel to each other [S2].
*   Corresponding angles formed by a transversal cutting parallel lines are equal [S2].
