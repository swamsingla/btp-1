
# Algebraic Identities

An algebraic identity is a special algebraic equation that remains true for every possible value of the variables it contains. [S1] You might have already used some of these identities in earlier classes to multiply expressions quickly or to break down complex algebraic expressions into simpler factors. [S2] In this chapter, we will explore how these standard formulas can be used to simplify our calculations and solve problems much faster.

## The Four Standard Identities

The foundation of algebraic identities rests on four standard formulas that you should memorize. [S1] These are the building blocks for almost every problem you will encounter in this topic.

The first two identities are the expansions of squared binomials. [S1] The first is the expansion of the sum of two variables, and the second is the expansion of their difference.

Identity I: $(x + y)^{2} = x^{2} + 2xy + y^{2}$ [S1]
Identity II: $(x - y)^{2} = x^{2} - 2xy + y^{2}$ [S1]

The third identity is the difference of two squares. [S1] It shows how a subtraction of squares can be factored into a multiplication of two binomials.

Identity III: $x^{2} - y^{2} = (x + y)(x - y)$ [S1]

The fourth identity is a useful formula for multiplying two binomials that share the same first term. [S1] It allows you to find the product without multiplying term by term.

Identity IV: $(x + a)(x + b) = x^{2} + (a + b)x + ab$ [S1]

## Expanding Trinomials

Sometimes, you will encounter an expression with three terms, known as a trinomial. [S1] You can expand the square of a trinomial by using the first standard identity as a stepping stone. [S1] Let us consider the expression $(x + y + z)^2$. [S1] We can treat the first two terms as a single variable, say $t = x + y$, and apply Identity I. [S1]

$(t + z)^2 = t^2 + 2tz + z^2$

Substituting $t = x + y$ back into the equation gives us:

$(x + y)^2 + 2(x + y)z + z^2$

Now, we apply Identity I again to the $(x + y)^2$ part:

$x^2 + 2xy + y^2 + 2xz + 2yz + z^2$

Rearranging the terms in descending order of the variables, we get the standard formula for expanding a trinomial squared. [S1]

Identity V: $(x + y + z)^{2} = x^{2} + y^{2} + z^{2} + 2xy + 2yz + 2zx$ [S1]

This identity is incredibly useful when you need to expand expressions like $(3a + 4b + 5c)^2$ quickly without doing the multiplication manually. [S1]

## Expanding Cubes

Just as we can expand the square of a sum or difference, we can also expand the cube of a binomial. [S1] The first identity for cubes expands the sum of two variables, and the second identity expands their difference.

Identity VI: $(x + y)^{3} = x^{3} + y^{3} + 3xy(x + y)$ [S1]
Identity VII: $(x - y)^{3} = x^{3} - y^{3} - 3xy(x - y) = x^{3} - 3x^{2}y + 3xy^{2} - y^{3}$ [S1]

To use these identities, you simply need to identify which parts of your expression correspond to $x$ and $y$, and then substitute them into the formula. [S1] This saves a lot of time when dealing with complex algebraic terms.

## Using Identities for Quick Calculations

One of the most practical uses of algebraic identities is to perform arithmetic calculations without a calculator. [S1] By cleverly choosing values for your variables, you can turn a difficult multiplication into a simple application of an identity.

Consider the problem of finding the product of $105 \times 106$. [S1] Instead of multiplying directly, you can use Identity IV. [S1] Let us write $105$ as $(100 + 5)$ and $106$ as $(100 + 6)$. [S1] Here, $x = 100$, $a = 5$, and $b = 6$. [S1]

Using Identity IV: $(x + a)(x + b) = x^{2} + (a + b)x + ab$ [S1]

$(100 + 5)(100 + 6) = 100^2 + (5 + 6) \cdot 100 + (5 \cdot 6)$

$= 10000 + 1100 + 30$

$= 11130$

This method is much faster and less prone to errors than traditional multiplication, especially when dealing with larger numbers. [S1]

## Worked example

Let us apply Identity III to factorise the expression $\frac {2 5}{4} x ^ {2} - \frac {y ^ {2}}{9}$. [S1]

First, we need to recognize that $\frac {2 5}{4} x ^ {2}$ is a perfect square. [S1] We can write it as $\left(\frac {5}{2} x\right)^2$ because $\left(\frac {5}{2}\right)^2 = \frac {2 5}{4}$. [S1]

Similarly, $\frac {y ^ {2}}{9}$ is a perfect square because it can be written as $\left(\frac {y}{3}\right)^2$ since $\left(\frac {y}{3}\right)^2 = \frac {y ^ {2}}{9}$. [S1]

Now, our expression becomes $\left(\frac {5}{2} x\right)^2 - \left(\frac {y}{3}\right)^2$. [S1]

We can now apply Identity III: $x^{2} - y^{2} = (x + y)(x - y)$ [S1]

Let $x = \frac {5}{2} x$ and $y = \frac {y}{3}$. [S1]

$\left(\frac {5}{2} x\right)^2 - \left(\frac {y}{3}\right)^2 = \left(\frac {5}{2} x + \frac {y}{3}\right) \left(\frac {5}{2} x - \frac {y}{3}\right)$

This is the fully factorised form of the given expression. [S1]

## Key points

*   An algebraic identity is an equation that holds true for all values of the variables involved. [S1]
*   Identity I and Identity II are used to expand the square of a binomial, while Identity III is used to factorise the difference of two squares. [S1]
*   Identity IV is a shortcut for multiplying two binomials with the same first term. [S1]
*   Identity V expands the square of a trinomial into a sum of squares and product terms. [S1]
*   Identity VI and Identity VII expand the cube of a binomial, making it easier to handle higher powers. [S1]
*   These identities are not just for algebra; they can be used to perform quick arithmetic calculations. [S1]
