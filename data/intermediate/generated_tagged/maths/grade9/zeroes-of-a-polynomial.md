# Zeroes of a Polynomial

This page looks at the value of a polynomial at a number, and at the numbers for which that value is zero. These numbers are called the zeroes of the polynomial.

## Value of a Polynomial at a Number

Consider the polynomial $p(x) = 5x^{3} - 2x^{2} + 3x - 2$. If we replace $x$ by 1 everywhere in $p(x)$, we get $p(1) = 5 \times (1)^{3} - 2 \times (1)^{2} + 3 \times (1) - 2 = 5 - 2 + 3 - 2 = 4$ [S1]. So we say that the value of $p(x)$ at $x = 1$ is 4 [S1]. Similarly, $p(0) = 5(0)^{3} - 2(0)^{2} + 3(0) - 2 = -2$ [S1].

Here are three more examples, finding the value of a polynomial at a given value of the variable [S1]:

*   For $p(x) = 5x^{2} - 3x + 7$ at $x = 1$: $p(1) = 5(1)^{2} - 3(1) + 7 = 5 - 3 + 7 = 9$.
*   For $q(y) = 3y^{3} - 4y + \sqrt{11}$ at $y = 2$: $q(2) = 3(2)^{3} - 4(2) + \sqrt{11} = 24 - 8 + \sqrt{11} = 16 + \sqrt{11}$.
*   For $p(t) = 4t^4 + 5t^3 - t^2 + 6$ at $t = a$: $p(a) = 4a^{4} + 5a^{3} - a^{2} + 6$.

## Zero of a Polynomial

Now consider the polynomial $p(x) = x - 1$. Here $p(1) = 1 - 1 = 0$ [S1]. As $p(1) = 0$, we say that 1 is a zero of the polynomial $p(x)$ [S1]. Similarly, 2 is a zero of $q(x)$, where $q(x) = x - 2$ [S1].

In general, a zero of a polynomial $p(x)$ is a number $c$ such that $p(c) = 0$ [S1].

## Zeroes and Polynomial Equations

The zero of the polynomial $x - 1$ is obtained by equating it to 0, that is $x - 1 = 0$, which gives $x = 1$ [S1]. We say $p(x) = 0$ is a polynomial equation, and 1 is the root of the polynomial equation $p(x) = 0$ [S1]. So 1 is the zero of the polynomial $x - 1$, or a root of the polynomial equation $x - 1 = 0$ [S1].

Finding a zero of $p(x)$ is the same as solving the equation $p(x) = 0$ [S1]. For example, to find a zero of $p(x) = 2x + 1$, we solve $2x + 1 = 0$, which gives $x = -\frac{1}{2}$ [S1]. So $-\frac{1}{2}$ is a zero of the polynomial $2x + 1$ [S1].

## Zero of a Linear Polynomial

Let $p(x) = ax + b$ with $a \neq 0$ be a linear polynomial. Finding a zero of $p(x)$ amounts to solving $p(x) = 0$, that is $ax + b = 0$ with $a \neq 0$ [S1]. Then $ax = -b$, so $x = -\frac{b}{a}$ [S1]. Hence $x = -\frac{b}{a}$ is the only zero of $p(x)$, which means a linear polynomial has one and only one zero [S1]. In this way 1 is the zero of $x - 1$, and $-2$ is the zero of $x + 2$ [S1].

## Zeroes of Constant Polynomials

Consider the constant polynomial 5. It has no zero, because replacing $x$ by any number in $5x^0$ still gives us 5 [S1]. In fact, a non-zero constant polynomial has no zero [S1]. For the zero polynomial, by convention, every real number is a zero [S1].

## Checking Whether a Number Is a Zero

To check whether $-2$ and 2 are zeroes of the polynomial $x + 2$, let $p(x) = x + 2$. Then $p(2) = 2 + 2 = 4$ and $p(-2) = -2 + 2 = 0$ [S1]. Therefore $-2$ is a zero of the polynomial $x + 2$, but 2 is not [S1].

## Observations

Our observations about the zeroes of polynomials are [S1]:

*   A zero of a polynomial need not be 0.
*   0 may be a zero of a polynomial.
*   Every linear polynomial has one and only one zero.
*   A polynomial can have more than one zero.

## Worked Example

Verify whether 2 and 0 are zeroes of the polynomial $x^{2} - 2x$ [S1]. Let $p(x) = x^{2} - 2x$. Then $p(2) = 2^{2} - 4 = 4 - 4 = 0$ and $p(0) = 0 - 0 = 0$ [S1]. Hence 2 and 0 are both zeroes of the polynomial $x^{2} - 2x$ [S1]. This polynomial shows that a polynomial can have more than one zero, and that 0 can be one of them.

## Key points

*   The value of a polynomial $p(x)$ at $x = c$ is found by replacing $x$ by $c$ everywhere in $p(x)$ [S1].
*   A zero of a polynomial $p(x)$ is a number $c$ such that $p(c) = 0$ [S1].
*   Finding a zero of $p(x)$ amounts to solving the polynomial equation $p(x) = 0$ [S1].
*   The linear polynomial $ax + b$, with $a \neq 0$, has one and only one zero, $-\frac{b}{a}$ [S1].
*   A non-zero constant polynomial has no zero, and by convention every real number is a zero of the zero polynomial [S1].
*   A polynomial can have more than one zero [S1].
