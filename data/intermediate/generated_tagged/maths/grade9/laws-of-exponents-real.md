# Laws of Exponents for Real Numbers

This page extends the laws of exponents to the case where the base is a positive real number and the exponents are rational numbers, such as $\frac{1}{2}$ or $\frac{3}{4}$. To do this we first need to understand what an exponent like $\frac{3}{2}$ means. This also lets us write roots, like square roots, as exponents.

## Laws of Exponents for Natural Numbers

Here $a$, $m$ and $n$ are natural numbers, $a$ is called the base, and $m$ and $n$ are the exponents [S1]. The laws of exponents are:

(i) $a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}$

(ii) $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$

(iii) $\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}$, where $m > n$

(iv) $a^m b^m = (ab)^m$ [S1]

For example, $17^{2} \cdot 17^{5} = 17^{7}$, $(5^{2})^{7} = 5^{14}$, $\frac{23^{10}}{23^{7}} = 23^{3}$ and $7^{3} \cdot 9^{3} = 63^{3}$ [S1].

Also $(a)^0 = 1$. Using law (iii), we get $\frac{1}{a^n} = a^{-n}$ [S1]. This lets us extend the laws to negative exponents too. For example, $17^{2} \cdot 17^{-5} = 17^{-3} = \frac{1}{17^{3}}$, $(5^{2})^{-7} = 5^{-14}$, $\frac{23^{-10}}{23^{7}} = 23^{-17}$ and $(7)^{-3} \cdot (9)^{-3} = (63)^{-3}$ [S1].

## Understanding Rational Exponents

Before we can use the laws of exponents with fractions, we need to know what a fraction like $\frac{3}{2}$ actually means. We define the $n$-th root of a positive real number $a$ as the positive number $b$ that satisfies $b^n = a$. In the language of exponents, we write $\sqrt[n]{a} = a^{\frac{1}{n}}$ [S1]. For example, $\sqrt[3]{2}$ is simply $2^{\frac{1}{3}}$.

Now, let us look at a fractional exponent like $4^{\frac{3}{2}}$. We can interpret this in two different ways using our definition. The first way is to take the square root of 4 first, and then raise the result to the power of 3. This gives us $(4^{\frac{1}{2}})^{3} = 2^{3} = 8$. The second way is to raise 4 to the power of 3 first, and then take the square root of the result. This gives us $(4^{3})^{\frac{1}{2}} = 64^{\frac{1}{2}} = 8$ [S1]. Both ways give the same answer, 8.

Here is the formal rule. Let $a > 0$ be a real number. Let $m$ and $n$ be integers such that $m$ and $n$ have no common factors other than 1, and $n > 0$. Then, we define $a^{\frac{m}{n}} = (\sqrt[n]{a})^{m} = \sqrt[n]{a^{m}}$ [S1].

## Applying the Laws to Rational Exponents

The laws of exponents can be extended to rational exponents. They hold when the base $a$ is a positive real number and the exponents $p$ and $q$ are rational numbers [S1].

The first law is the product rule. If you multiply two powers with the same base, you add the exponents. For example, $2^{\frac{2}{3}} \cdot 2^{\frac{1}{3}} = 2^{\left(\frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{3}\right)} = 2^{1} = 2$ [S1].

The second law is the power of a power rule. If you raise a power to another power, you multiply the exponents. For example, $\left(3^{\frac{1}{5}}\right)^{4} = 3^{\frac{4}{5}}$ [S1].

The third law is the quotient rule. If you divide two powers with the same base, you subtract the exponents. For example, $\frac{7^{\frac{1}{5}}}{7^{\frac{1}{3}}} = 7^{\left(\frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{3}\right)} = 7^{\frac{3 - 5}{15}} = 7^{\frac{-2}{15}}$ [S1].

The fourth law is the product to a power rule. If you multiply two different bases raised to the same exponent, you can combine the bases. For example, $13^{\frac{1}{5}} \cdot 17^{\frac{1}{5}} = (13 \times 17)^{\frac{1}{5}} = 221^{\frac{1}{5}}$ [S1].

## The Extended Laws

Putting it all together, the extended laws of exponents for real numbers are as follows. Let $a > 0$ be a real number and let $p$ and $q$ be rational numbers. Then:

(i) $a^p \cdot a^q = a^{p+q}$
(ii) $(a^p)^q = a^{pq}$
(iii) $\frac{a^p}{a^q} = a^{p-q}$
(iv) $a^p b^p = (ab)^p$ [S1]

These laws can be used to simplify expressions that involve roots and powers.

## Worked example

Let us look at a worked example to see how these rules work together. Suppose we want to simplify the expression $8^{\frac{2}{3}} \cdot 8^{\frac{1}{3}}$. We can use the first law, which says that when we multiply powers with the same base, we add the exponents. We add $\frac{2}{3}$ and $\frac{1}{3}$ to get $\frac{3}{3}$, which is 1. So, $8^{\frac{2}{3}} \cdot 8^{\frac{1}{3}} = 8^{1} = 8$.

Alternatively, we can solve this using the definition of rational exponents. We know that $8^{\frac{2}{3}}$ is the same as $(\sqrt[3]{8})^{2}$. Since the cube root of 8 is 2, this becomes $2^{2}$, which is 4. Similarly, $8^{\frac{1}{3}}$ is the cube root of 8, which is 2. Multiplying these two results together gives $4 \times 2 = 8$. Both methods give the same answer, which confirms that our rules are working correctly.

## Key points

*   A positive real number $a$ raised to the power of $\frac{1}{n}$ is defined as the $n$-th root of $a$, written as $a^{\frac{1}{n}} = \sqrt[n]{a}$ [S1].
*   For a positive real number $a$, the expression $a^{\frac{m}{n}}$ can be evaluated as $(\sqrt[n]{a})^{m}$ or $\sqrt[n]{a^{m}}$ [S1].
*   The laws of exponents, including the product, quotient, and power rules, apply to rational exponents just as they do to whole number exponents [S1].
*   When multiplying powers with the same base, you add the exponents [S1].
*   When dividing powers with the same base, you subtract the exponents [S1].
