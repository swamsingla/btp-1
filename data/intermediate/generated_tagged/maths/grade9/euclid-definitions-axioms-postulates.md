# Euclid's Definitions, Axioms and Postulates

Euclid's work provides the foundation for deductive geometry, which is the method of proving mathematical truths step by step. The ancient Greek mathematicians viewed geometry as an abstract model of the world around them, developing concepts like points, lines, and planes from observing solids, surfaces, and space. Understanding these basic assumptions is crucial because they are the starting point for proving more complex theorems and shapes.

## From Solids to Points

Euclid built his geometric system by starting with the physical world and breaking it down into simpler, abstract parts. He began with the idea of a solid object, which has shape, size, and position. The boundaries of these solid objects are called surfaces. A surface separates one part of space from another, and it is said to have no thickness [S1]. 

The boundaries of these surfaces are either curves or straight lines. These straight lines, in turn, end in points [S1]. This creates a three-step progression from solids to points: solids to surfaces, surfaces to lines, and lines to points. In each step, you lose one extension, also called a dimension. Because of this, a solid has three dimensions, a surface has two dimensions, a line has one dimension, and a point has zero dimensions [S1].

Euclid summarized these statements as definitions, starting with 23 definitions in Book 1 of his *Elements*. A few of his core definitions are:
1. A point is that which has no part [S1].
2. A line is breadthless length [S1].
3. The ends of a line are points [S1].
4. A straight line is a line which lies evenly with the points on itself [S1].
5. A surface is that which has length and breadth only [S1].
6. The edges of a surface are lines [S1].
7. A plane surface is a surface which lies evenly with the straight lines on itself [S1].

## The Problem with Definitions

If you study these definitions carefully, you will notice a logical puzzle. To define a point, Euclid says it is something that has no part. But to understand what "part" means, you might say it is something that occupies an area. Then, to understand "area," you need to define it, which requires defining other things. This creates a long chain of definitions without an end [S1].

Because of this, mathematicians agree to leave some geometric terms undefined. We take a point, a line, and a plane (or plane surface) as undefined terms. We rely on our intuitive feeling for these concepts to understand them, and we often represent them with physical models or diagrams. For example, we represent a point as a dot on paper, even though a real dot has some thickness [S1].

## Axioms and Postulates

Starting with his definitions, Euclid assumed certain properties that were not to be proved. These assumptions are obvious universal truths. He divided them into two types: axioms and postulates. He used the term "postulate" for assumptions that were specific to geometry. Common notions, often called axioms, were assumptions used throughout mathematics and not specifically linked to geometry [S1].

Here are some of Euclid's common notions:
(1) Things which are equal to the same thing are equal to one another [S1].
(2) If equals are added to equals, the wholes are equal [S1].
(3) If equals are subtracted from equals, the remainders are equal [S1].
(4) Things which coincide with one another are equal to one another [S1].
(5) The whole is greater than the part [S1].
(6) Things which are double of the same things are equal to one another [S1].
(7) Things which are halves of the same things are equal to one another [S1].

These common notions refer to magnitudes of some kind. Magnitudes of the same kind can be compared and added, but magnitudes of different kinds cannot be compared. For example, a line cannot be compared to a rectangle, nor can an angle be compared to a pentagon [S1].

## Euclid's Five Postulates

Euclid's five postulates are specific to geometry. They are simple and obvious statements that are accepted without any proof [S1].

Postulate 1: A straight line may be drawn from any one point to any other point. Note that this postulate tells us that at least one straight line passes through two distinct points, but it does not explicitly say that there cannot be more than one such line. However, Euclid frequently assumed, without mentioning it, that there is a unique line joining two distinct points. This is stated as an axiom: Given two distinct points, there is a unique line that passes through them [S1].

Postulate 2: A terminated line can be produced indefinitely. In modern terms, a terminated line is a line segment. According to this postulate, a line segment can be extended on either side to form a line [S1].

Postulate 3: A circle can be drawn with any centre and any radius [S1].

Postulate 4: All right angles are equal to one another [S1].

Postulate 5: If a straight line falling on two straight lines makes the interior angles on the same side of it taken together less than two right angles, then the two straight lines, if produced indefinitely, meet on that side on which the sum of angles is less than two right angles. For example, if a line falls on two other lines such that the sum of the interior angles on one side is less than $180^{\circ}$, then those two lines will eventually intersect on that side [S1].

A brief look at the five postulates shows that Postulate 5 is far more complex than any other postulate. On the other hand, Postulates 1 through 4 are so simple and obvious that these are taken as self-evident truths. However, it is not possible to prove them. So, these statements are accepted without any proof [S1].

## Building a Logical Chain

After Euclid stated his postulates and axioms, he used them to prove other results. Then, using these results, he proved some more results by applying deductive reasoning. The statements that were proved are called propositions or theorems. Euclid deduced 465 propositions in a logical chain using his axioms, postulates, definitions, and theorems proved earlier in the chain [S1].

A system of axioms is called consistent if it is impossible to deduce from these axioms a statement that contradicts any axiom or previously proved statement. So, when any system of axioms is given, it needs to be ensured that the system is consistent [S1].

## Worked Example: Adding Line Segments

Let us see how Euclid used his axioms and postulates to prove a simple result. Suppose A, B, and C are three points on a line, and B lies between A and C. We need to prove that $AB + BC = AC$.

In the figure, the line segment AC coincides with the combination of AB and BC. Euclid's Axiom (4) states that things which coincide with one another are equal to one another. Therefore, it can be deduced that $AB + BC = AC$ [S1].

## Worked Example: Constructing an Equilateral Triangle

Euclid used his postulates to construct shapes. For example, to construct an equilateral triangle on any given line segment, you can use Postulate 3 to draw a circle with one endpoint as the centre and the length of the line segment as the radius. Then, draw another circle with the other endpoint as the centre and the same radius. The two circles meet at a point, and drawing lines from this point to the endpoints forms an equilateral triangle [S1].

## Worked Example: Uniqueness of a Line

Euclid's Postulates and Axioms also help us prove that two distinct lines cannot have more than one point in common. Suppose two lines $l$ and $m$ intersect in two distinct points, say P and Q. This would mean you have two distinct lines passing through two distinct points P and Q. But this assumption clashes with the axiom that only one line can pass through two distinct points. Therefore, the assumption that two distinct lines can pass through two distinct points is wrong. We are forced to conclude that two distinct lines cannot have more than one point in common [S1].

## Key points

- Euclid's system starts with undefined terms like point, line, and plane, and builds up using 23 definitions.
- Axioms (common notions) are universal truths used in all mathematics, while postulates are specific to geometry.
- A solid has 3 dimensions, a surface has 2, a line has 1, and a point has 0.
- Postulate 1 states a straight line can be drawn between any two points, and a unique line passes through them.
- Postulate 2 states a terminated line (line segment) can be extended indefinitely to form a line.
- Postulate 3 allows us to draw a circle with any centre and any radius.
- Postulate 4 states all right angles are equal to one another.
- Postulate 5 is a complex rule about parallel lines and interior angles summing to less than $180^{\circ}$.
- Euclid used deductive reasoning to prove 465 propositions, forming the foundation of modern geometry.
