# Types of Triangles

Triangles are three-sided polygons that form the basic building blocks of geometry. Understanding how to classify them is important because it helps you identify their properties, predict their behavior in real-world structures, and solve complex mathematical problems. By looking at the lengths of their sides and the measures of their angles, we can sort triangles into distinct categories.

## Classification by Side Lengths

Triangles can be grouped based on whether their sides are equal or different in length. This classification is straightforward and relies on measuring the lengths of the three sides.

An equilateral triangle is a triangle where all three sides have exactly the same length. Because of this perfect symmetry, all three of its interior angles are also equal. Since the sum of the interior angles in any triangle must be $180^{\circ}$, each angle in an equilateral triangle measures exactly $60^{\circ}$ [S2].

An isosceles triangle has exactly two sides that are equal in length, while the third side is a different length. A key property of isosceles triangles is that the angles opposite to the equal sides are also equal. For example, if a triangle has two sides of equal length, the angles facing those sides will have the same degree measure [S2].

A scalene triangle is a triangle where all three sides have three different lengths. Consequently, all three interior angles in a scalene triangle also have three different degree measures. This lack of symmetry makes the scalene triangle the most irregular of the three main side-based categories [S1].

## Classification by Angle Measures

Just as triangles can be sorted by their sides, they can also be grouped by the types of angles they contain. This classification focuses on the degrees of the interior angles.

A right-angled triangle is a triangle that contains exactly one right angle, which measures exactly $90^{\circ}$. The side opposite the right angle is called the hypotenuse, and it is always the longest side of the triangle [S1].

An acute-angled triangle is a triangle where all three interior angles are acute angles, meaning each angle measures less than $90^{\circ}$. It is incorrect to define an acute-angled triangle as having just one acute angle, because by definition, all three angles must be acute [S1].

An obtuse-angled triangle is a triangle that contains exactly one obtuse angle, which measures greater than $90^{\circ}$ but less than $180^{\circ}$. The other two angles in an obtuse-angled triangle must be acute, because the total sum of the angles must equal $180^{\circ}$ [S1].

## Combining the Classifications

It is possible for a triangle to belong to both a side-based category and an angle-based category at the same time. For instance, a triangle can be both equilateral and acute-angled, as all equilateral triangles have $60^{\circ}$ angles. However, you cannot construct a triangle that is both equilateral and right-angled, because an equilateral triangle requires all angles to be $60^{\circ}$, while a right-angled triangle requires one angle to be $90^{\circ}$ [S1].

Similarly, a triangle can be both isosceles and right-angled, or isosceles and obtuse-angled. In an isosceles right-angled triangle, the two equal sides are the legs that form the $90^{\circ}$ angle, and the angles opposite them are each $45^{\circ}$. In an isosceles obtuse-angled triangle, the obtuse angle is opposite the unique, longest side, while the other two angles are equal and acute.

## Worked example

Consider a triangle where two of the sides are equal in length, and the angle between those two equal sides measures $80^{\circ}$. Because the two sides are equal, the angles opposite to those sides must also be equal [S2]. Therefore, the other two angles in the triangle must be equal to each other. To find the measure of these two equal angles, we subtract the $80^{\circ}$ angle from the total sum of $180^{\circ}$, which leaves $100^{\circ}$. Since these $100^{\circ}$ are split equally between the two remaining angles, each of them measures exactly $50^{\circ}$.

## Key points

*   Equilateral triangles have three sides of equal length and three angles of $60^{\circ}$ each.
*   Isosceles triangles have exactly two sides of equal length, and the angles opposite those sides are equal.
*   Scalene triangles have three sides of different lengths, and consequently, three angles of different measures.
*   Acute-angled triangles have three angles, each measuring less than $90^{\circ}$.
*   Right-angled triangles have exactly one angle measuring $90^{\circ}$, and obtuse-angled triangles have exactly one angle measuring greater than $90^{\circ}$.
