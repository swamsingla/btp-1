# Prime Numbers

Prime numbers are special whole numbers greater than 1 that have exactly two distinct factors. The only numbers that divide them evenly are 1 and the number itself. They matter because every other whole number can be built by multiplying prime numbers together. This makes them the basic building blocks of all numbers.

## What Makes a Number Prime
A prime number is a number greater than 1 that has only 1 and the number itself as its factors [S1]. For example, the number 7 is a prime number. The only numbers that divide 7 evenly are 1 and 7. The number 4 is not a prime number because it can be divided evenly by 1, 2, and 4. Since 4 has more than two factors, it is not prime. The number 2 is the only even prime number.

## Prime Factorisation
Prime factorisation is the process of breaking down a number into a product of prime numbers [S1]. You keep rewriting composite factors until only primes are left [S1]. For example, the number 90 can be broken down into prime factors. We can write 90 as $3 \times 3 \times 2 \times 5$. The number 90 could also be factorised as $3 \times 30$ or $2 \times 45$ [S1]. Remarkably, the resulting prime factors are always the same, perhaps only changing their order. For example, if we consider factorising $3 \times 30$ further, we get $90 = 3 \times 30 = 3 \times 2 \times 15 = 3 \times 2 \times 3 \times 5$ [S1]. We have arrived at the same prime factors, just in a different order. Note that the prime factorisation of a prime number is the prime number itself [S1].

## Using Prime Factorisation to Find Factors
From the prime factorisation of a number, we can construct all its factors [S1]. Any 'subpart' of the factorisation — any group of those prime factors multiplied together — is a factor of the number.

Consider the number 840, whose prime factorisation is $2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 5 \times 7$ [S1]. Taking one prime at a time gives the factors 2, 3, 5 and 7. Taking two at a time gives factors such as $2 \times 2 = 4$, $2 \times 3 = 6$, $3 \times 5 = 15$ and $5 \times 7 = 35$. Taking three at a time gives $2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8$, $2 \times 3 \times 5 = 30$ and $3 \times 5 \times 7 = 105$. Taking all six gives 840 itself, and taking none of them gives 1, which is a factor of every number.

A smaller number lets us list every factor this way without missing any. The prime factorisation of 225 is $3 \times 3 \times 5 \times 5$. Taking one prime at a time gives 3 and 5. Taking two at a time gives $3 \times 3 = 9$, $3 \times 5 = 15$ and $5 \times 5 = 25$. Taking three at a time gives $3 \times 3 \times 5 = 45$ and $3 \times 5 \times 5 = 75$. Taking all four gives $3 \times 3 \times 5 \times 5 = 225$. Adding 1 to this list, the factors of 225 are 1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 25, 45, 75 and 225.

## Worked Example
Let us find the prime factorisation of the number 30. We can use the division method. We divide 30 by the smallest prime number, which is 2. This gives us 15. We then divide 15 by the next smallest prime number, which is 3. This gives us 5. We stop when we reach a prime number. The prime factorisation of 30 is $2 \times 3 \times 5$ [S1].

## Key points
- A prime number is a number greater than 1 that has only 1 and the number itself as its factors [S1].
- Prime factorisation is the process of breaking down a number into a product of prime numbers.
- The resulting prime factors are always the same, perhaps only changing their order [S1].
- From the prime factorisation of a number, we can construct all its factors.
- The prime factorisation of a prime number is the prime number itself [S1].
