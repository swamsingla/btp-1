# Prime Factorisation

Prime factorisation is a way to break down any number into the smallest building blocks of mathematics: prime numbers. Every whole number greater than 1 is either a prime number itself, or it is a product of prime numbers [S1]. This method is very useful because it helps us find the factors of a number quickly, and it makes finding the Highest Common Factor (HCF) of two numbers much easier [S1]. If you know what prime numbers are and the divisibility rules for 2, 4, 5, 8, and 10, you already have some useful tools for finding prime factorisation.

## What is Prime Factorisation?

Any number can be written as a product of prime numbers by keeping factorising the composite numbers until only prime numbers are left. This final result is called the prime factorisation of the given number [S1]. For example, we can find the prime factorisation of 90 by breaking it down: [S1]
$$ 90 = 3 \times 3 \times 2 \times 5 $$ The number 90 could also have been factorised in different ways at the start, like $3 \times 30$ or $2 \times 45$. However, no matter which starting pair you choose, the resulting prime factors are always the same, just possibly in a different order. For example, if we start with $3 \times 30$ and factorise 30 further, we get:
$$ 90 = 3 \times 30 = 3 \times 2 \times 15 = 3 \times 2 \times 3 \times 5 $$ This is the same set of prime factors as before. Also, if you are given a prime number itself, its prime factorisation is simply the prime number itself.

## The Division Method

To make the process of finding prime factors simple and reliable, we use the division method. In this method, we divide the number by the smallest prime factor that goes into it exactly [S1]. We keep doing this until the number left at the bottom is a prime number [S1]. 

Let us find the prime factorisation of 105 this way. The smallest prime that divides 105 is 3, and $105 \div 3 = 35$ [S1]. The smallest prime that divides 35 is 5, and $35 \div 5 = 7$ [S1]. Now 7 is itself a prime number, so we stop [S1]. The prime factorisation is the primes we divided by, together with the prime we were left with: [S1]
$$ 105 = 3 \times 5 \times 7 $$ The same method works for 30. The smallest prime that divides 30 is 2, and $30 \div 2 = 15$. The smallest prime that divides 15 is 3, and $15 \div 3 = 5$. Now 5 is prime, so we stop, which gives $30 = 2 \times 3 \times 5$. The division method is much easier to carry out than randomly picking factors, especially for numbers with many factors.

## Finding Factors from Prime Factorisation

Once you have the prime factorisation of a number, you can use it to find all of its factors. You do this by taking different combinations of the prime factors and multiplying them together [S1]. 

For example, let us find the factors of 225. First we need its prime factorisation, using the division method [S1]. The smallest prime that divides 225 is 3, and $225 \div 3 = 75$ [S1]. Three divides 75 as well, and $75 \div 3 = 25$ [S1]. Three does not divide 25, but 5 does, and $25 \div 5 = 5$ [S1]. Finally 5 is prime, so we stop [S1]. So the prime factorisation of 225 is $225 = 3 \times 3 \times 5 \times 5$ [S1].

Now, we can find all the factors of 225 by taking different combinations of these prime factors. We start with 1, which is always a factor because it uses none of them [S1]. Taking one prime factor at a time gives 3 and 5 [S1]. Taking two at a time gives $3 \times 3 = 9$, $3 \times 5 = 15$ and $5 \times 5 = 25$ [S1]. Taking three at a time gives $3 \times 3 \times 5 = 45$ and $3 \times 5 \times 5 = 75$ [S1]. Finally, taking all four gives $3 \times 3 \times 5 \times 5 = 225$ [S1]. 

Adding 1 to this list, we get all the factors of 225: 1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 25, 45, 75, 225.

## Worked Example

Let us apply the division method to find the prime factorisation of 1200. The smallest prime that divides 1200 is 2, and we can keep dividing by 2 for a while: $1200 \div 2 = 600$, then $600 \div 2 = 300$, then $300 \div 2 = 150$, then $150 \div 2 = 75$ [S1]. That is four divisions by 2 [S1]. Now 2 no longer divides 75, but 3 does, and $75 \div 3 = 25$ [S1]. Then 5 divides 25, and $25 \div 5 = 5$ [S1]. Finally 5 is prime, so we stop [S1].

Collecting the primes we divided by, together with the 5 left at the end, the prime factorisation of 1200 is $1200 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 5 \times 5$. You can check it: $16 \times 3 \times 25 = 1200$ [S1].

## Key points

*   Prime factorisation is the process of breaking down a number into the product of its prime factors.
*   The division method is a reliable way to find the prime factorisation of a number by repeatedly dividing by the smallest prime factor.
*   The resulting prime factors of a number are always the same, regardless of the starting pair of factors.
*   Once you have the prime factorisation of a number, you can find all of its factors by taking different combinations of the prime factors.
*   Prime factorisation is a useful tool for finding the HCF of two or more numbers.
