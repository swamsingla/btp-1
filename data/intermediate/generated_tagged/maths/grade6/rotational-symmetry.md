# Rotational Symmetry

Imagine a paper windmill spinning in the wind. You might think it has a line of symmetry, but if you try to fold it, the two halves will not exactly overlap. However, if you rotate it by $90^{\circ}$ about the pin at its centre, the windmill looks exactly the same [S1]. We say that the windmill has rotational symmetry. When talking of rotational symmetry, there is always a fixed point about which the object is rotated. This fixed point is called the centre of rotation [S1].

## Centre of Rotation and Angle of Symmetry

The centre of rotation is the fixed point around which an object is turned. An angle through which a figure can be rotated to look exactly the same is called an angle of rotational symmetry, or just an angle of symmetry, for short [S1]. For the windmill, the angles of symmetry are $90^{\circ}$ (quarter turn), $180^{\circ}$ (half turn), $270^{\circ}$ (three-quarter turn), and $360^{\circ}$ (full turn). Observe that when any figure is rotated by $360^{\circ}$, it comes back to its original position, so $360^{\circ}$ is always an angle of symmetry [S1]. 

Consider a square. After a rotation of $90^{\circ}$ about its centre, the square overlaps itself exactly: each corner moves into the position the next corner held, and the fourth corner moves into the position of the first. Its other angles of symmetry are $180^{\circ}$, $270^{\circ}$, and $360^{\circ}$ [S1].

## Order of Rotational Symmetry

The number of times a shape looks exactly the same during one complete rotation is called the order of rotational symmetry. The windmill has 4 angles of symmetry, so its order of rotational symmetry is 4 [S1]. A square has 4 angles of symmetry, so its order is also 4. 

The same idea builds a figure with any order we want. A figure with exactly 5 angles of symmetry needs 5 radial arms spaced equally, so the angle between two adjacent arms is $\frac{360^{\circ}}{5} = 72^{\circ}$. Its angles of symmetry are then $72^{\circ}$, $144^{\circ}$, $216^{\circ}$, $288^{\circ}$, and $360^{\circ}$ [S1].

A figure with 6 angles of symmetry needs 6 equally spaced arms, so the angle between two adjacent arms is $\frac{360^{\circ}}{6} = 60^{\circ}$. Its angles of symmetry are $60^{\circ}$, $120^{\circ}$, $180^{\circ}$, $240^{\circ}$, $300^{\circ}$, and $360^{\circ}$ [S1].

## Figures with Radial Arms

Consider a figure made of 4 radial arms spreading out from a centre, like the spokes of a wheel, with equal gaps between them. Each gap is $\frac{360^{\circ}}{4} = 90^{\circ}$. This figure has 4 angles of symmetry: $90^{\circ}$, $180^{\circ}$, $270^{\circ}$, and $360^{\circ}$ [S1]. 

That same four-armed figure can be modified to have only two angles of symmetry. Keep four arms but make the gaps unequal in pairs, so that opposite gaps match and adjacent gaps do not. The figure then looks the same only after a half turn and after a full turn, so its angles of symmetry are just $180^{\circ}$ and $360^{\circ}$, and its order of rotational symmetry is 2.

## Exploring Three Radial Arms

A figure can also be built with exactly 3 angles of symmetry, using 3 radial arms. For a figure with three radial arms to have rotational symmetry, a rotated version of it must overlap the original. That means the first gap must land on the second, the second on the third, and the third back on the first, so all three gaps must be equal. A full turn has 360 degrees, shared equally among the three gaps, so each gap must be $\frac{360^{\circ}}{3} = 120^{\circ}$. 

So the figure with 3 arms shows rotational symmetry when the angle between adjacent arms is $120^{\circ}$. It then has three angles of symmetry: $120^{\circ}$, $240^{\circ}$, and $360^{\circ}$. Its order of rotational symmetry is 3.

## Worked Example

Consider a figure with radial arms having exactly 7 angles of symmetry. Its smallest angle of symmetry is $\frac{360^{\circ}}{7}$. This is not a whole number of degrees. Dividing 360 by 7 gives 51 with a remainder of 3, so as a mixed fraction the smallest angle of symmetry is $51\frac{3}{7}^{\circ}$. The seven angles of symmetry are the multiples of this angle: $51\frac{3}{7}^{\circ}$, $102\frac{6}{7}^{\circ}$, and so on up to $360^{\circ}$.

## Key points

* Rotational symmetry occurs when an object looks exactly the same after being rotated around a fixed point.
* The fixed point around which an object is rotated is called the centre of rotation.
* An angle through which a figure can be rotated to look exactly the same is called an angle of rotational symmetry.
* The order of rotational symmetry is the number of times a shape looks exactly the same during one complete rotation.
* The angle between adjacent radial arms is $\frac{360^{\circ}}{\text{number of arms}}$ to achieve rotational symmetry.
