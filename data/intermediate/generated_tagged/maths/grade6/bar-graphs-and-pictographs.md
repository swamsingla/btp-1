# Bar Graphs, Pictographs and Pie Charts

Data is everywhere, from the number of students in a classroom to the favourite modes of travel. When we collect information, we often end up with long lists of numbers. Reading and comparing these lists can be slow and difficult. Visual representations of categorical data help us make sense of this information. They turn raw numbers into pictures and shapes. This makes it much easier to see patterns, make comparisons, and understand what the data is actually saying.

## Pictographs: Pictures Represent Data

A pictograph is a way to represent data through pictures of objects. It is a visual and suggestive way to show information without writing out every single number [S1]. Instead of a long list of names, you might see a row of small bus icons. Each icon stands for one student who takes the bus. This allows you to answer questions about the data with just a quick glance [S1].

In a pictograph, a scale or key is added to show what each symbol or picture represents. Each symbol can represent one unit or multiple units [S1]. For example, a single apple picture might mean one student likes apples. Another pictograph might use a single apple picture to represent ten students who like apples.

However, preparing a pictograph can be challenging when the amount of data is large or when the frequencies are not exact multiples of the scale. Imagine you are tracking the number of students present in a class, and the numbers are 30, 35, 20, 25, 30, 25, 30, and 20. If you decide to use one symbol for each student, the chart will become very long and crowded [S1]. To solve this, you can choose a larger scale. For instance, you could decide that one symbol represents 5 students, or even 10 students [S1]. 

When you use a larger scale, you must be careful. If you use one symbol to represent 10 students, you might face a problem when the number of students is 25 or 35. You cannot draw exactly 2.5 or 3.5 symbols. In such cases, you might need to use a combination of symbols to show the exact count [S1].

## Bar Graphs: Comparing with Bars

Like pictographs, bar graphs can help us quickly understand and interpret information, such as the highest value or the comparison of values of different categories [S2]. However, when the amount of data is large, presenting it by a pictograph is not only time-consuming but at times difficult too [S2]. This is where bar graphs come in.

A bar graph uses rectangular bars to represent the values. The length or height of each bar corresponds to the amount of data it represents. For example, if you are looking at the number of endangered species in different regions, a bar graph can show which region has the highest number of threatened species [S2]. The bars make it easy to compare the values at a glance.

Bar graphs can be drawn with horizontal bars or vertical bars. When making a visual presentation of data, it is important to make it fit in the intended space. This can be controlled by choosing the scale appropriately [S3]. 

Sometimes, a bar graph is more intuitive and visually appealing if the bars grow upward, vertically from the ground. A bar graph with vertical bars is also called a column graph, because each bar stands upright like a column. It is generally more intuitive to represent heights, which are measured upwards from the ground, using bar graphs that have vertical bars or columns [S3]. Similarly, lengths that are parallel to the ground, such as distances between locations on Earth, are usually best represented using bar graphs with horizontal bars [S3].

## Pie Charts: Showing Parts of a Whole

A pie chart is a circular chart divided into slices to show the proportion of different categories within a whole. Each slice represents a category, and the size of the slice is proportional to the amount of data it represents. For example, if you wanted to show the favourite modes of travel used by students, a pie chart could show what percentage of students use a bus, a car, or a bicycle. The whole circle represents 100% of the data, and each slice represents a part of that 100%.

## Worked example

Imagine you asked a group of children whether they get at least 9 hours of sleep at night, and recorded each answer as Always, Sometimes, or Never. You decide to show the results as a pictograph in which one picture of a moon represents 10 children.

The 'Always' row has 5 moons, so it stands for $5 \times 10 = 50$ children. The 'Sometimes' row has 2 complete moons and one half moon, so it stands for $2 \times 10 = 20$ children plus half of 10, which is 5, giving 25 children. The 'Never' row has 4 complete moons, so it stands for $4 \times 10 = 40$ children. Altogether $50 + 25 + 40 = 115$ children answered, and reading the rows is quicker than counting a list of 115 answers one by one.

## Key points

*   Pictographs represent data through pictures of objects and use a scale or key to show what each symbol represents [S1].
*   Bar graphs use rectangular bars to represent values and are useful when the amount of data is large [S2, S3].
*   A bar graph with vertical bars is also called a column graph [S3].
*   When preparing a visual presentation of data, it is important to make it fit in the intended space by choosing the scale appropriately [S3].
*   Heights are generally best represented using bar graphs with vertical bars or columns [S3].
