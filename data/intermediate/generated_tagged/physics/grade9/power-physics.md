# Power

Imagine two children, A and B, who weigh exactly the same. Both of them start climbing up a rope to reach a height of 8 m. However, A manages to reach the top in just 15 s, while B takes a full 20 s. Even though both of them climb the exact same distance and do the exact same amount of work, A finishes the task much faster. This difference in speed is exactly what we study in physics as power. Power is not about how much work is done, but rather how quickly that work is completed. It tells us how fast an agent is transferring energy or doing work. When we talk about the power of machines like motorbikes and motorcars, we are simply referring to the speed with which they change energy to move. Power is defined as the rate of doing work or the rate of transfer of energy [S2].

## The Formula and the Unit of Power

To understand power mathematically, we need a clear formula. If an agent does a certain amount of work, which we call $W$, in a specific amount of time, $t$, then power is calculated by dividing the work by the time. The formula for power is given by the equation [S2]:

$$
P = \frac{W}{t}\tag{10.8}
$$

The standard unit of power is the watt, which has the symbol W. This unit is named in honour of James Watt, who lived from 1736 to 1819. One watt is defined as the power of an agent that does work at the rate of exactly 1 joule per second [S2]. We can also describe this unit in terms of energy consumption. Power is 1 W when the rate of consumption of energy is $1\mathrm{J}s^{-1}$ [S2]. When dealing with larger rates of energy transfer, such as those used by household appliances or large vehicles, we often express power in kilowatts. One kilowatt is equal to exactly 1000 watts [S3]:

$$
1\mathrm{kW} = 1000\mathrm{W} = 1000\mathrm{J}s^{-1}
$$

## Average Power

The power of an agent is not always exactly the same at every single moment. An agent may be doing work at different rates at different intervals of time. For instance, a car might consume energy at a different rate while accelerating on a highway compared to when it is idling at a red light. Because of these changing rates, the concept of average power is extremely useful. We obtain average power by taking the total energy consumed and dividing it by the total time taken [S2].

## Worked Example

Let us look at a practical scenario to see how this formula works in real life. Consider two girls, A and B, who each weigh 400 N. Both of them climb up a rope through a height of 8 m. Girl A takes 20 s to accomplish this task, while girl B takes 50 s. We can calculate the power expended by each girl using the work done divided by the time taken [S4].

First, let us find the power expended by girl A. The work done is calculated by multiplying her weight by the height she climbed:
$$
\text{Work done by A} = 400\mathrm{N} \times 8\mathrm{m}
$$
Since girl A took 20 s to do this work, her power is:
$$
P = \frac{400\mathrm{N} \times 8\mathrm{m}}{20\mathrm{s}} = 160\mathrm{W}
$$

Next, let us find the power expended by girl B. She also climbed 8 m, so the work done is the exact same:
$$
\text{Work done by B} = 400\mathrm{N} \times 8\mathrm{m}
$$
However, girl B took 50 s to complete the task. Her power is:
$$
P = \frac{400\mathrm{N} \times 8\mathrm{m}}{50\mathrm{s}} = 64\mathrm{W}
$$

As you can see from these calculations, even though both girls did the exact same amount of work, girl A expended more power because she did it in a shorter amount of time [S4].

## Real-World Applications

Understanding the concept of power helps us make sense of the world around us. When we buy an electric appliance for our homes, such as a washing machine or an air conditioner, the label on the back will tell us its power rating in watts or kilowatts. A higher power rating means the appliance consumes energy at a faster rate. This is why using a 1500 W heater for one hour consumes more electrical energy than using a 60 W light bulb for the same duration, even though both are operating for exactly 60 minutes.

You can observe this concept in your own home by looking at the electric meter installed there. You can take the readings of the meter each day at 6.30 am and 6.30 pm. If you do this activity for about a week, you will be able to see exactly how many 'units' are consumed during the day time compared to the night. By tabulating your observations, you can draw inferences about your household's energy usage and compare them with the details given in your monthly electricity bill [S5].
