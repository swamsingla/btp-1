# Work (Physics)

In everyday life, you hear the word work all the time. You do homework, your parents go to work, and you might even work up a sweat playing cricket. In physics, however, work has a very specific and precise meaning. It is not about how tired you feel after lifting a heavy box. It is about whether a force actually causes an object to move. Understanding this concept is essential because it helps us measure exactly how much energy is transferred to an object when we push, pull, or lift it.

## The Scientific Definition of Work

To understand how scientists define work, consider the situations you encounter every day. You might push a pebble lying on a surface and watch it roll. You might pull a heavy trolley across a platform, or you might lift a thick textbook from the floor to a shelf. In each of these cases, you apply a force and the object moves. In the scientific sense, work is done whenever these two specific conditions are met simultaneously. First, a force must act on an object. Second, the object must be displaced. If either one of these conditions is missing, no work is done in the physics sense.

To put this into a mathematical formula, we look at a constant force acting on an object. Imagine a force, which we call $F$, acting on an object. The object moves through a distance, which we call $s$. If the force acts in the exact direction of this displacement, we define work to be equal to the product of the force and the displacement. The work done is equal to the magnitude of the force multiplied by the distance moved in the direction of the force [S1]. 

Work has only magnitude and no direction. This means it is a scalar quantity. We can write this relationship as an equation: Work done = force $\times$ displacement, or $W = F \times s$.

## Units of Work

Whenever we measure a physical quantity in science, we must use a standard unit. For work, the standard unit is the newton metre (N m). However, scientists often use a shorter name for this unit. The unit of work is also called the joule (J) [S1].

One joule is the amount of work done on an object when a force of 1 N displaces it by 1 m along the line of action of the force. This means if you push an object with a force of exactly 1 newton and it moves exactly 1 metre in the same direction as your push, you have done 1 joule of work.

## When Work is Positive

In many everyday situations, the force and the displacement are in the same direction. For example, imagine a baby pulling a toy car parallel to the ground. The baby exerts a force in the exact direction of displacement of the car. In this situation, the work done will be equal to the product of the force and displacement [S1]. Because the force and the movement are aligned, the work done by the force is taken as positive.

To understand this with numbers, imagine you push a heavy wooden box across a smooth floor. You apply a constant force of 10 N, and the box moves 2 m in the exact direction of your push. To find the work done, you simply multiply these two values. The work done is $10 \, N \times 2 \, m = 20 \, N \, m$, which is 20 J.

## When Work is Negative

Sometimes, a force acts in the opposite direction to the movement of an object. Think about an object moving with a uniform velocity along a straight path. Now, imagine a retarding force, which we call $F$, is applied in the opposite direction. This means the angle between the direction of movement and the force is $180^{\circ}$ [S1].

Let us say the object stops after moving a displacement $s$ in its original direction. In such a situation, the work done by the force, $F$, is taken as negative and denoted by the minus sign. The work done by the force is $F \times (-s)$, or $(-F \times s)$.

This might seem strange at first. If you are pushing a car forward but it is rolling backward, the physics of the situation assigns negative work to your forward push relative to the backward motion. It is clear from the above discussion that the work done by a force can be either positive or negative, depending entirely on the relative directions of the force and the displacement.

## When Work is Zero

There are two specific situations where no work is done. You can see this directly from the basic equation for work. If the force on the object is zero, then the work done will be zero, because any number multiplied by zero is zero. Similarly, if the displacement of the object is zero, the work done will also be zero, because you cannot move the object, so no energy is transferred to it.

Another way to think about zero work is to consider the angle between the force and the displacement. If the force is applied but the object does not move, the conditions for work are not satisfied. Likewise, if the force is zero, no matter how far the object moves, no work is done.

## Worked Example

Let us apply these rules to a practical problem. Imagine a student is carrying a heavy school bag. The student walks 10 m along a straight corridor. The bag has a weight of 5 N due to gravity pulling it downward. However, the student is holding the bag and moving horizontally forward. The force of gravity acts vertically downward, while the displacement is horizontal. Because the force and the displacement are perpendicular to each other, the angle between them is $90^{\circ}$.

Using the general rule for work, the work done is $F \times s \times \cos(\theta)$. Here, the angle $\theta$ is $90^{\circ}$. The cosine of $90^{\circ}$ is zero. Therefore, the work done by the force of gravity on the bag is zero. The student is definitely getting tired and doing physical work, but in the strict physics sense, the force of gravity did zero joules of work on the bag because the bag did not move in the direction of the gravitational force [S1, S2].

## Key points

*   In physics, work is done only when a force acts on an object and the object is displaced, satisfying both conditions [S2].
*   The work done by a constant force acting in the direction of displacement is the product of the force and the displacement [S1].
*   The SI unit of work is the joule (J), where $1 \, \mathrm{J} = 1 \, \mathrm{N} \cdot \mathrm{m}$ [S1].
*   Work done is positive when the force and displacement are in the same direction [S1].
*   Work done is negative when the force and displacement are in opposite directions [S1].
*   Work done is zero if either the force or the displacement is zero [S1].
