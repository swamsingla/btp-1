# Sound — Production and Propagation

Sound is a form of energy that allows us to communicate, listen to music, and stay aware of our surroundings. It is produced by vibrating objects and requires a medium to travel from the source to our ears. Understanding how sound works is essential because it explains why we cannot hear anything in the vacuum of space and why different materials carry sound at different speeds.

## How Sound is Produced

Sound is produced by vibrating objects [S1]. When an object vibrates, it moves back and forth rapidly. This movement disturbs the surrounding air or other material. The vibrating object pushes the particles of the medium in front of it. This creates a region of high pressure called a compression (C) [S1]. When the object moves backward, it leaves behind a region of low pressure called rarefaction (R) [S1]. As the object continues to move back and forth rapidly, a series of compressions and rarefactions are created [S1]. These alternating high and low pressure regions form the sound wave that travels through the medium [S1].

## The Medium and Wave Propagation

The matter or substance through which sound is transmitted is called a medium [S1]. A medium can be solid, liquid, or gas [S1]. Air is the most common medium through which sound travels [S1]. Sound moves through a medium from the point of generation to the listener [S1]. When an object vibrates, it sets the particles of the medium around it vibrating [S1]. A particle of the medium in contact with the vibrating object is first displaced from its equilibrium position [S1]. It then exerts a force on the adjacent particle [S1]. As a result of which the adjacent particle gets displaced from its position of rest [S1]. After displacing the adjacent particle the first particle comes back to its original position [S1]. This process continues in the medium till the sound reaches your ear [S1]. The disturbance created by a source of sound in the medium travels through the medium and not the particles of the medium [S1].

A wave is a disturbance that moves through a medium when the particles of the medium set neighbouring particles into motion [S1]. They in turn produce similar motion in others [S1]. The particles of the medium do not move forward themselves, but the disturbance is carried forward [S1]. This is what happens during propagation of sound in a medium, hence sound can be visualised as a wave [S1]. Sound waves are characterised by the motion of particles in the medium and are called mechanical waves [S1].

## Speed of Sound in Different Media

Sound propagates through a medium at a finite speed [S3]. The speed of sound depends on the properties of the medium through which it travels [S3]. The speed of sound decreases when we go from solid to gaseous state [S3]. In any medium as we increase the temperature, the speed of sound increases [S3]. For example, the speed of sound in air is $331 \, \text{m s}^{-1}$ at $0^{\circ}C$ and $344 \, \text{m s}^{-1}$ at $22^{\circ}C$ [S3]. ## Worked Example

Suppose a lightning strike occurs and the sound travels through the air. At a temperature of $22^{\circ}C$, the speed of sound in air is $344 \, \text{m s}^{-1}$ [S3]. If the storm is $1.72 \, \text{km}$ away, which is $1720 \, \text{m}$, the time taken for the sound to reach an observer can be calculated using the formula $\text{time} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{speed}}$ [S3]. Substituting the values, $\text{time} = \frac{1720}{344}$. This gives $\text{time} = 5 \, \text{s}$. Therefore, the observer will hear the thunder 5 seconds after seeing the lightning.

## Range of Hearing

The audible range of sound for human beings extends from about 20 Hz to 20000 Hz [S2]. One Hz equals one cycle per second [S2]. Children under the age of five and some animals, such as dogs can hear up to 25 kHz [S2]. One kHz equals 1000 Hz [S2]. As people grow older their ears become less sensitive to higher frequencies [S2]. Sounds of frequencies below 20 Hz are called infrasonic sound or infrasound [S2]. If we could hear infrasound we would hear the vibrations of a pendulum just as we hear the vibrations of the wings of a bee [S2]. Rhinoceroses communicate using infrasound of frequency as low as 5 Hz [S2]. Whales and elephants produce sound in the infrasound range [S2]. It is observed that some animals get disturbed before earthquakes [S2]. Earthquakes produce low-frequency infrasound before the main shock waves begin which possibly alert the animals [S2]. Frequencies higher than 20 kHz are called ultrasonic sound or ultrasound [S2]. Ultrasound is produced by animals such as dolphins, bats and porpoises [S2]. Moths of certain families have very sensitive hearing equipment [S2]. These moths can hear the high frequency squeaks of the bat and know when a bat is flying nearby, and are able to escape capture [S2]. Rats also play games by producing ultrasound [S2].

## Hearing Aids

People with hearing loss may need a hearing aid [S2]. A hearing aid is an electronic, battery operated device [S2]. The hearing aid receives sound through a microphone [S2]. The microphone converts the sound waves to electrical signals [S2]. These electrical signals are amplified by an amplifier [S2]. The amplified electrical signals are given to a speaker of the hearing aid [S2]. The speaker converts the amplified electrical signal to sound and sends to the ear for clear hearing [S2].

## Key points
- Sound is produced by vibrating objects and requires a medium to travel [S1].
- Sound propagates as a wave of compressions and rarefactions in the medium [S1].
- Sound waves are mechanical waves because they require a medium [S1].
- The speed of sound decreases from solids to liquids to gases [S3].
- The audible range for humans is 20 Hz to 20000 Hz, with infrasound below and ultrasound above this range [S2].
