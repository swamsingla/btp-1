# Newton's Third Law of Motion

Newton's Third Law of Motion is a fundamental rule in physics that explains how forces always work in pairs. While the first two laws of motion tell us how an applied force changes the motion of an object, the third law focuses on the interaction between two objects [S1]. This rule is important because it helps us understand that forces never exist alone; whenever you push or pull something, that object pushes or pulls back on you with equal strength.

## The Core Rule of Action and Reaction

The third law of motion states that when one object exerts a force on another object, the second object instantaneously exerts a force back on the first [S1]. These two forces are always equal in magnitude but opposite in direction. This means if you push a wall with a force of 10 Newtons, the wall pushes back on you with exactly 10 Newtons. It is crucial to remember that these two opposing forces are also known as action and reaction forces [S1].

There is a common misunderstanding about these forces: they always act on two different objects and never on the same object [S1]. Because the action and reaction forces act on different objects, they do not cancel each other out. For example, when you try to kick a football, you apply a force to the ball. At the same time, the football applies an equal and opposite force to your foot. Both you and the ball feel the impact of this pair of forces.

## Everyday Examples of Action and Reaction

Understanding action and reaction forces helps explain many everyday situations. Consider a game of football where a player collides with an opponent while trying to kick the ball. Both players feel hurt because each applies a force to the other, demonstrating that there is a pair of forces and not just one force [S1].

Another example is walking. Suppose you are standing at rest and intend to start walking on a road. You must accelerate, and this requires a force in accordance with the second law of motion. You push the road below backwards. The road exerts an equal and opposite force on your feet to make you move forward [S1]. 

When a gun is fired, it exerts a forward force on the bullet. The bullet exerts an equal and opposite force on the gun, resulting in the recoil of the gun. Since the gun has a much greater mass than the bullet, the acceleration of the gun is much less than the acceleration of the bullet [S1]. Similarly, when a sailor jumps out of a rowing boat, the force on the boat moves it backwards as the sailor jumps forward [S1].

## Why the Accelerations Are Different

It is important to note that even though the action and reaction forces are always equal in magnitude, these forces may not produce accelerations of equal magnitudes [S1]. This is because each force acts on a different object that may have a different mass. 

When a gun is fired, the forward force on the bullet and the recoil of the gun are equal and opposite. However, the bullet has a very small mass, so it experiences a huge acceleration and flies forward rapidly. The gun has a much greater mass, so it experiences a much smaller acceleration, which we feel as a kick or recoil [S1]. Because the forces act on different masses, the resulting changes in motion are very different.

## Measuring Action and Reaction Forces

We can demonstrate this law using a simple setup with two spring balances connected together. When a force is applied through the free end of spring balance A, it is observed that both spring balances show the same readings on their scales [S1]. This means that the force exerted by spring balance A on balance B is equal but opposite in direction to the force exerted by balance B on balance A.

This observation gives us an alternative statement of the third law of motion: to every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction [S1]. However, it must be remembered that the action and reaction always act on two different objects, simultaneously [S1].

## Worked Example

Let us look at an example involving a sailor jumping from a boat. Suppose a sailor of mass $80 \, \text{kg}$ jumps forward from a rowing boat with a velocity of $2 \, \text{m/s}$. The sailor pushes the boat backwards. We can use the law of conservation of momentum, which is closely related to Newton's Third Law, to find the velocity of the boat.

The total momentum of the system before the jump is zero, since both the sailor and the boat are at rest. After the jump, the momentum of the sailor is $p_{\text{sailor}} = m_{\text{sailor}} \times v_{\text{sailor}} = 80 \times 2 = 160 \, \text{kg m/s}$ forward. To keep the total momentum zero, the boat must move backwards with an equal momentum. If the mass of the boat is $200 \, \text{kg}$, the velocity of the boat is $v_{\text{boat}} = \frac{160}{200} = 0.8 \, \text{m/s}$ backwards. The force the sailor exerts on the boat is equal and opposite to the force the boat exerts on the sailor, perfectly illustrating the third law [S1].

## Key points

- When one object exerts a force on a second object, the second object instantaneously exerts a force back on the first [S1].
- These two forces are always equal in magnitude but opposite in direction [S1].
- The action and reaction forces always act on two different objects and never on the same object [S1].
- Even though the forces are equal, they may not produce accelerations of equal magnitudes because each force acts on a different object with a different mass [S1].
- The law can be stated as: to every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction [S1].
