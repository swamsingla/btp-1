# Kinetic Energy

Kinetic energy is the energy that an object possesses because it is in motion. This means that anything that is moving, whether it is a rolling stone, flowing water, or a speeding car, has kinetic energy. The faster an object moves, the more kinetic energy it has. This concept is important because a moving object can do work on other things. For example, a moving trolley can hit a wooden block and push it, doing work and transferring energy in the process [S2].

## Everyday Examples of Kinetic Energy

You can observe kinetic energy in many everyday situations. A falling coconut, a rolling stone, a flying aircraft, blowing wind, and a running athlete all possess kinetic energy because they are moving [S3]. A bullet can pierce a target because it has kinetic energy. The wind can move the blades of a windmill for the same reason. Objects in motion possess energy, and we call this energy kinetic energy. The kinetic energy of an object increases with its speed, which is why a fast-moving object can do more work than an identical object moving relatively slow [S2].

## The Work-Energy Theorem

The work-energy theorem connects the work done on an object to its kinetic energy. By definition, the kinetic energy of a body moving with a certain velocity is equal to the work done on it to make it acquire that velocity [S3]. This means that if you want to make an object move, you must do work on it to give it kinetic energy. The work done on an object causes a change in its kinetic energy. If an object starts from a stationary position, the work done is equal to its kinetic energy [S4].

## The Formula for Kinetic Energy

We can express the kinetic energy of an object in the form of an equation. Consider an object of mass, $m$, moving with a uniform velocity, $u$ [S3]. Let a constant force, $F$, act on it in the direction of its displacement through a distance $s$. The work done by the force is $W = Fs$ [S3]. The work done on the object will cause a change in its velocity from $u$ to $v$. Using the equations of motion, we can show that the work done is $W = \frac{1}{2} m(v^2 - u^2)$ [S3]. If the object is starting from its stationary position, that is, $u = 0$, then the work done is $W = \frac{1}{2} mv^2$ [S4]. Thus, the kinetic energy possessed by an object of mass, $m$, and moving with a uniform velocity, $v$, is $E_k = \frac{1}{2} mv^2$ [S4].

## Using the Kinetic Energy Formula

Let us look at an example to see how to use this formula. Consider an object of mass 15 kg moving with a uniform velocity of 4 m s$^{-1}$ [S4]. The kinetic energy is $E_k = \frac{1}{2} \times 15 \, \text{kg} \times 4 \, \text{m s}^{-1} \times 4 \, \text{m s}^{-1} = 120 \, \text{J}$. The kinetic energy of the object is 120 J.

## Calculating Work Done

We can also use the work-energy theorem to calculate the work done to change an object's speed. Consider a car with a mass of 1500 kg. We want to find the work to be done to increase its velocity from $30 \, \text{km h}^{-1}$ to $60 \, \text{km h}^{-1}$ [S5]. First, we convert the velocities to m s$^{-1}$. The initial velocity is $u = \frac{25}{3} \, \text{m s}^{-1}$ and the final velocity is $v = \frac{50}{3} \, \text{m s}^{-1}$. The initial kinetic energy is $E_{ki} = \frac{1}{2} \times 1500 \, \text{kg} \times (\frac{25}{3} \, \text{m s}^{-1})^2 = 156250/3 \, \text{J}$. The final kinetic energy is $E_{kf} = \frac{1}{2} \times 1500 \, \text{kg} \times (\frac{50}{3} \, \text{m s}^{-1})^2 = 625000/3 \, \text{J}$. The work done is equal to the change in kinetic energy, which is $E_{kf} - E_{ki} = 156250 \, \text{J}$ [S5].

## Key points
*   Kinetic energy is the energy possessed by an object due to its motion [S3].
*   The kinetic energy of an object increases with its speed [S3].
*   The kinetic energy of an object of mass, $m$, and moving with a uniform velocity, $v$, is $E_k = \frac{1}{2} mv^2$ [S4].
*   The work done on an object is equal to the change in its kinetic energy [S4].
*   A moving object can do work on other objects [S2].
