# Buoyancy and Archimedes' Principle

Have you ever felt lighter while swimming in a pool? Have you ever noticed that a bucket of water feels heavier when you pull it out of a well? These everyday experiences happen because of a force called buoyancy. This page explains why a massive ship made of iron and steel can float on the sea, while a flat sheet of the exact same metal sinks. It also introduces a famous rule, Archimedes' Principle, that explains how upthrust works.

## The Upward Push of Water

Buoyancy is the upward force exerted by a fluid on an object that is immersed in it [S2]. You can feel this force yourself. Take an empty plastic bottle and close its mouth with an airtight stopper. When you place this bottle in a bucket of water, it floats on the surface [S2]. If you push the bottle down into the water, you will feel a push in the upward direction. As you push it deeper and deeper, this upward push becomes stronger until the bottle is completely immersed [S2]. 

When you let go of the bottle, it bounces back to the surface. This happens because the earth's gravity pulls the bottle downward, giving it a weight. However, the water pushes it upward. When the bottle is immersed, the upward force exerted by the water is greater than its weight, so it rises up [S2]. To keep the bottle completely underwater, you must apply a downward force from the outside. This force must be at least equal to the difference between the upward force and the weight of the bottle [S2, S3].

## Why Some Things Float and Some Sink

Whether an object floats or sinks depends on the relationship between its weight and the upthrust of the water. The upthrust is the upward force exerted by the water. For an object to float, the upthrust must be greater than or equal to its weight [S5, S6]. 

Consider an iron nail placed on the surface of water. The downward force due to gravity on the iron nail pulls it downwards. There is an upthrust of water on the nail, which pushes it upwards. But the downward force acting on the nail is greater than the upthrust of water on the nail. So it sinks [S5]. 

Now consider a piece of cork and an iron nail of equal mass. When placed on the surface of water, the cork floats while the nail sinks. This happens because of the difference in their densities. The density of a substance is defined as the mass per unit volume. The density of cork is less than the density of water. This means that the upthrust of water on the cork is greater than the weight of the cork. So it floats. The density of an iron nail is more than the density of water. This means that the upthrust of water on the iron nail is less than the weight of the nail. So it sinks [S6]. 

Therefore, objects of density less than that of a liquid float on the liquid. The objects of density greater than that of a liquid sink in the liquid [S6].

## Archimedes' Principle

Archimedes' Principle tells us exactly how much upthrust a body experiences. The magnitude of the buoyant force depends on the density of the fluid [S3]. When a body is immersed fully or partially in a fluid, it experiences an upward force that is equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by it [S7, S8]. 

You can observe this with a spring balance. Suspend a stone from a spring balance and note the reading due to its weight. Slowly dip the stone in a container of water. You will find that the reading of the balance decreases as the stone is gradually lowered in the water. However, no further change is observed once the stone gets fully immersed in the water. The decrease in the reading means that an upward force acts on the stone. As the stone is fully immersed, the amount of water it pushes aside stops changing, so the upward force stops changing [S7].

Archimedes was a Greek scientist who discovered this principle [S8]. He noticed that the water in a bathtub overflowed when he stepped into it. He ran through the streets shouting “Eureka!”, which means “I have got it”. This knowledge helped him to determine the purity of the gold in the crown made for the king. His work in the field of Geometry and Mechanics made him famous. His understanding of levers, pulleys, wheels-and-axle helped the Greek army in its war with the Roman army [S8].

## Applications of Archimedes' Principle

Archimedes' principle has many practical applications. It is used in designing ships and submarines [S8]. Lactometers, which are used to determine the purity of a sample of milk, and hydrometers, used for determining the density of liquids, are based on this principle [S8].

## Worked example

Suppose you have a block of wood with a volume of $0.05 \, \text{m}^3$ and a mass of $30 \, \text{kg}$. You place it in a container of water. According to Archimedes' Principle, the upward force on the wood is equal to the weight of the water it displaces. If the density of water is $1000 \, \text{kg/m}^3$, the mass of the displaced water is $1000 \times 0.05 = 50 \, \text{kg}$. The weight of this displaced water is $50 \, g$. The weight of the wood is $30 \, g$. Since the upthrust of $50 \, g$ is greater than the weight of $30 \, g$, the wood will float [S6, S7].

## Key points

- Buoyancy is the upward force exerted by a fluid on an object that is immersed in it [S2, S3].
- The upthrust or buoyant force increases as an object is pushed deeper into the fluid until it is completely immersed [S2, S3].
- An object floats if the upthrust is greater than or equal to its weight, and it sinks if the upthrust is less than its weight [S5, S6].
- Objects of density less than that of a liquid float on the liquid. Objects of density greater than that of a liquid sink in the liquid [S6].
- Archimedes' Principle states that when a body is immersed fully or partially in a fluid, it experiences an upward force that is equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by it [S7, S8].
