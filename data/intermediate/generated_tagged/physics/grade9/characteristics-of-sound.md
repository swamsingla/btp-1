# Characteristics of Sound

Sound is a form of energy that travels through a medium as a wave. When an object vibrates, it creates disturbances in the medium around it, such as air. These disturbances travel to our ears, allowing us to hear the world around us. To understand the different sounds we hear, from a loud clap to a soft whisper, we need to look at the specific properties of the sound wave. These properties tell us how high or low a sound is, how loud it is, and what makes it unique.

## Pitch and Frequency

Pitch is the characteristic of a sound that allows us to distinguish between a sharp, high sound and a flat, low sound. The pitch of a sound is determined by its frequency. Frequency tells us how frequently an event occurs. For a sound wave, the frequency is the number of oscillations per unit time. An oscillation is the complete change in the medium's density from its maximum value to its minimum value, and back to its maximum value. We can also count the number of compressions or rarefactions that pass a fixed point per unit time to find the frequency. A compression is a region where particles are crowded together, and a rarefaction is a region where particles are spread apart. The faster the vibration of the source, the higher the frequency and the higher the pitch. A high pitch sound corresponds to more number of compressions and rarefactions passing a fixed point per unit time.

The frequency of a sound wave is usually represented by the symbol $v$ (Greek letter, nu). Its SI unit is hertz (symbol, Hz). The time taken by two consecutive compressions or rarefactions to cross a fixed point is called the time period of the wave. In other words, the time taken for one complete oscillation is called the time period of the sound wave. It is represented by the symbol $T$. Its SI unit is second (s). Frequency and time period are related as follows: $$ \left| v = \frac {1}{T} \right| $$

Heinrich Rudolph Hertz was born on 22 February 1857 in Hamburg, Germany and educated at the University of Berlin. He confirmed J.C. Maxwell's electromagnetic theory by his experiments. He laid the foundation for future development of radio, telephone, telegraph and even television. He also discovered the photoelectric effect which was later explained by Albert Einstein. The SI unit of frequency was named as hertz in his honour.

## Loudness and Amplitude

The loudness or softness of a sound is determined basically by its amplitude. The amplitude of a sound wave is the magnitude of the maximum disturbance in the medium on either side of the mean value. The amplitude of the sound wave depends upon the force with which an object is made to vibrate. If we strike a table lightly, we hear a soft sound because we produce a sound wave of less energy (amplitude). If we hit the table hard, we hear a louder sound because we produce a sound wave of more energy (amplitude). A sound wave spreads out from its source. As it moves away from the source, its amplitude as well as its loudness decreases. Louder sound can travel a larger distance as it is associated with higher energy.

## Quality and Timbre

The quality or timbre of sound is that characteristic which enables us to distinguish one sound from another having the same pitch and loudness. The sound which is more pleasant is said to be of a rich quality. A sound of single frequency is called a tone. The sound which is produced due to a mixture of several frequencies is called a note and is pleasant to listen to. Noise is unpleasant to the ear! Music is pleasant to hear and is of rich quality.

## Speed of Sound

The speed of sound is defined as the distance which a point on a wave, such as a compression or a rarefaction, travels per unit time. We know, speed, $v =$ distance / time $$ = \frac {\lambda}{T} $$ Here $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the sound wave. It is the distance travelled by the sound wave in one time period (T) of the wave. Thus, $$ v = \lambda v \left(\because \frac {1}{\mathrm{T}} = v\right) $$ $$ \text{or} \quad v = \lambda v $$ That is, speed $=$ wavelength $\times$ frequency.

The speed of sound remains almost the same for all frequencies in a given medium under the same physical conditions.

## Worked Example

Example: A sound wave has a frequency of 2 kHz and wavelength 35 cm. How long will it take to travel 1.5 km?

Solution: Given, Frequency, $\nu = 2\mathrm{kHz} = 2000\mathrm{Hz}$ Wavelength, $\lambda = 35\mathrm{cm} = 0.35\mathrm{m}$

We know that speed, $v$ of the wave $$ = \text{wavelength} \times \text{frequency} $$ $$ V = \lambda V $$ $$ = 0. 3 5 \mathrm{m} \quad 2 0 0 0 \mathrm{Hz} = 7 0 0 \mathrm{m/s} $$

The time taken by the wave to travel a distance, d of 1.5 km is $$ t = \frac {d}{v} = \frac {1 . 5 \times 1 0 0 0 m}{7 0 0 m s ^ {- 1}} = \frac {1 5}{7} s = 2. 1 s. $$

Thus sound will take 2.1 s to travel a distance of 1.5 km.

## Key points

- Pitch is determined by the frequency of a sound wave.
- Loudness is determined by the amplitude of a sound wave.
- Quality or timbre allows us to distinguish between sounds of the same pitch and loudness.
- The speed of sound is the product of its wavelength and frequency.
- Frequency and time period are inversely related: $v = \frac {1}{T}$.
