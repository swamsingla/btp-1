# Acceleration Due to Gravity

When you throw a stone upwards, it eventually falls back down to the ground. This happens because the earth attracts objects towards it through the gravitational force [S1]. Whenever objects fall towards the earth under this gravitational force alone, we say that the objects are in free fall. While falling, there is no change in the direction of motion of the objects. But due to the earth's attraction, there will be a change in the magnitude of the velocity [S1]. Any change in velocity involves acceleration. Therefore, whenever an object falls towards the earth, an acceleration is involved. This acceleration is due to the earth's gravitational force and is called the acceleration due to gravity. It is denoted by $g$. The unit of $g$ is the same as that of acceleration, that is, $\mathrm{m~s^{-2}}$ [S1].

## The Universal Law of Gravitation

Before we understand the acceleration due to gravity, we need to look at the force that causes it. Every object in the universe attracts every other object with a force which is proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them [S3]. The force is along the line joining the centres of two objects [S3].

Let two objects A and B of masses $M$ and $m$ lie at a distance $d$ from each other as shown in Fig. 9.2. Let the force of attraction between two objects be $F$. According to the universal law of gravitation, the force between two objects is directly proportional to the product of their masses. That is,
$$
F \propto M \times m\tag{9.1}
$$
And the force between two objects is inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them, that is,
$$
F \propto \frac {1}{d ^ {2}}\tag{9.2}
$$
Combining Eqs. (10.1) and (10.2), we get
$$
F \propto \frac {M \times m}{d ^ {2}}\tag{9.3}
$$
or,
$$
F = \mathrm{G} \frac {M \times m}{d ^ {2}}\tag{9.4}
$$
where $\mathrm{G}$ is the constant of proportionality and is called the universal gravitation constant [S3]. By multiplying crosswise, Eq. (9.4) gives
$$
\mathrm{G} = \frac {F d ^ {2}}{M \times m}\tag{9.5}
$$
The SI unit of $\mathrm{G}$ can be obtained by substituting the units of force, distance and mass in Eq. (9.5) as $\mathrm{N~m^{2}~kg^{-2}}$.

The value of $\mathrm{G}$ was found out by Henry Cavendish (1731 - 1810) by using a sensitive balance. The accepted value of $\mathrm{G}$ is $6.673 \times 10^{-11} \mathrm{~N~m^{2}~kg^{-2}}$ [S3].

## Deriving the Formula for Acceleration Due to Gravity

We know from the second law of motion that force is the product of mass and acceleration. Let the mass of an object falling towards the earth be $m$. We already know that there is acceleration involved in falling objects due to the gravitational force and is denoted by $g$ [S1]. Therefore the magnitude of the gravitational force $F$ will be equal to the product of mass and acceleration due to the gravitational force, that is,
$$
F = m g\tag{9.6}
$$
From Eqs. (9.4) and (9.6) we have
$$
m g = \mathrm{G} \frac {M \times m}{d ^ {2}}
$$
$$
\text{or} \quad g = \mathrm{G} \frac {M}{d ^ {2}}\tag{9.7}
$$
where $M$ is the mass of the earth, and $d$ is the distance between the object and the earth [S1]. Let an object be on or near the surface of the earth. The distance $d$ in Eq. (9.7) will be equal to $R$, the radius of the earth. Thus, for objects on or near the surface of the earth,
$$
m g = \mathrm{G} \frac {M \times m}{R ^ {2}}\tag{9.8}
$$
$$
g = \mathrm{G} \frac {M}{R ^ {2}}\tag{9.9}
$$
The earth is not a perfect sphere. As the radius of the earth increases from the poles to the equator, the value of $g$ becomes greater at the poles than at the equator. For most calculations, we can take $g$ to be more or less constant on or near the earth. But for objects far from the earth, the acceleration due to gravitational force of earth is given by Eq. (9.7) [S1].

## Calculating the Value of g

To calculate the value of $g$, we should put the values of $\mathrm{G}$, $M$ and $R$ in Eq. (9.9), namely, universal gravitational constant, $\mathrm{G} = 6.7 \times 10^{-11} \mathrm{~N~m^{2}~kg^{-2}}$, mass of the earth, $M = 6 \times 10^{24} \mathrm{~kg}$, and radius of the earth, $R = 6.4 \times 10^6 \mathrm{~m}$ [S2].
$$
\begin{array}{r l} g & = \mathrm{G} \frac {M}{R ^ {2}} \\ & = \frac {6 . 7 \times 1 0 ^ {- 1 1} \mathrm{Nm} ^ {2} \mathrm{kg} ^ {- 2} \times 6 \times 1 0 ^ {2 4} \mathrm{kg}}{(6 . 4 \times 1 0 ^ {6} \mathrm{m}) ^ {2}} \\ & = 9. 8 \mathrm{ms} ^ {- 2}. \end{array}
$$
Thus, the value of acceleration due to gravity of the earth, $g = 9.8 \, \mathrm{ms^{-2}}$ [S2].

## Variation with Altitude, Depth, and Latitude

The value of $g$ is not exactly the same everywhere on the earth's surface. The earth is not a perfect sphere. As the radius of the earth increases from the poles to the equator, the value of $g$ becomes greater at the poles than at the equator [S1].

For objects far from the earth, the acceleration due to gravitational force of earth is given by Eq. (9.7) [S1].

## Worked Example

Let us calculate the acceleration due to gravity at a certain altitude. Suppose an object is at a distance of $2.0 \times 10^6 \mathrm{~m}$ from the centre of the earth. We know that the mass of the earth $M = 6 \times 10^{24} \mathrm{~kg}$ and $\mathrm{G} = 6.7 \times 10^{-11} \mathrm{~N~m^{2}~kg^{-2}}$. We can use Eq. (9.7) to find the acceleration due to gravity at this altitude.
$$
g = \mathrm{G} \frac {M}{d ^ {2}}
$$
Substituting the values:
$$
g = \frac {6 . 7 \times 1 0 ^ {- 1 1} \times 6 \times 1 0 ^ {2 4}}{(2 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ {6}) ^ {2}}
$$
$$
g = \frac {40 . 2 \times 1 0 ^ {1 3}}{4 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ {1 2}}
$$
$$
g = 10 . 0 5 \mathrm{ms} ^ {- 2}
$$
Thus, the acceleration due to gravity at this altitude is $10.05 \, \mathrm{ms^{-2}}$.

## Key points

- Acceleration due to gravity, $g$, is the acceleration involved when objects fall towards the earth under the gravitational force alone, and its unit is $\mathrm{m~s^{-2}}$ [S1].
- The magnitude of the gravitational force $F$ is equal to the product of mass and acceleration due to the gravitational force, that is, $F = m g$ [S1].
- The acceleration due to gravity on or near the surface of the earth is given by the formula $g = \mathrm{G} \frac {M}{R ^ {2}}$ [S1].
- The accepted value of the universal gravitation constant $\mathrm{G}$ is $6.673 \times 10^{-11} \mathrm{~N~m^{2}~kg^{-2}}$ [S3].
- The value of $g$ is more or less constant on or near the earth for most calculations, but it varies with altitude, depth, and latitude [S1, S2].
- The value of $g$ becomes greater at the poles than at the equator because the radius of the earth increases from the poles to the equator [S1].
