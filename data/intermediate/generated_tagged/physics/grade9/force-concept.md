# Force — Concept

A force is simply a push or a pull [S1]. It is something we experience constantly in our daily lives. Whenever you open a door, throw a ball, or ride a bicycle, you are using force. Forces are not just about making things move; they are about interactions between objects. For a force to exist, at least two objects must interact with each other. One object applies the force, and the other object experiences it.

## Contact and Non-Contact Forces

There are two main ways forces can act on objects. The first is through direct physical touch. This is called a contact force [S1]. The second is without physical touch. This is called a non-contact force [S1].

A very common example of a contact force is muscular force. Whenever you perform a physical activity, such as walking, running, lifting, pushing, jumping, or stretching, the force is generated by the action of muscles in your body. This resulting force is known as muscular force. Muscles contract and elongate while you are doing any activity, and this action allows you to exert a push or a pull on objects.

Living beings rely heavily on muscular force for movement and survival. Animals, birds, fish, and insects all use muscular forces for movement and survival. For a long time, humans have also used the muscular force of some animals to carry out many tasks.

Not all forces require physical contact. There are forces whose effects can be experienced even if the objects are not touching [S1]. A great example of a non-contact force is the magnetic force. If you remember learning about magnets, you know that a magnet attracts objects made of magnetic materials. When you bring two magnets close to each other, like poles (North–North, South–South) will push each other away, while unlike poles (North–South) will pull each other closer. This attraction and repulsion between objects is also a form of push and pull, which means it is a force. A magnet can exert this force on another magnet or a magnetic material without being in physical contact with it [S1].

## Balanced and Unbalanced Forces

When a force is applied to an object, it can cause a variety of changes or effects [S1]. A force can change the state of motion of an object. It can make a stationary object start moving, make a moving object go faster or slower, stop it, or change the direction in which it is moving [S1].

Consider a wooden block on a horizontal table [S1]. If we apply a force by pulling the block from one side, it begins to move in that direction. Similarly, if we pull the block from the opposite side with equal force, the block will not move. Such forces are called balanced forces and do not change the state of rest or of motion of an object [S1].

Now, let us consider a situation in which two opposite forces of different magnitudes pull the block [S1]. In this case, the block would begin to move in the direction of the greater force. Thus, the two forces are not balanced and the unbalanced force acts in the direction the block moves. This suggests that an unbalanced force acting on an object brings it in motion [S1].

## The Role of Friction

Friction is a force that acts between two surfaces in contact. It always opposes motion [S1]. If some children try to push a box on a rough floor and push with a small force, the box does not move because of friction acting in a direction opposite to the push [S1]. This friction force arises between two surfaces in contact; in this case, between the bottom of the box and the floor's rough surface. It balances the pushing force and therefore the box does not move [S1].

If the children push the box harder but the box still does not move, it is because the friction force still balances the pushing force [S1]. If the children push the box harder still, the pushing force becomes bigger than the friction force. There is an unbalanced force, and so the box starts moving [S1].

When we ride a bicycle, friction plays a key role. When we stop pedalling, the bicycle begins to slow down. This is again because of the friction forces acting opposite to the direction of motion [S1]. In order to keep the bicycle moving, we have to start pedalling again.

However, it is quite incorrect to think that an object maintains its motion under the continuous application of an unbalanced force [S1]. An object moves with a uniform velocity when the forces acting on the object are balanced and there is no net external force on it. If an unbalanced force is applied on the object, there will be a change either in its speed or in the direction of its motion [S1]. Thus, to accelerate the motion of an object, an unbalanced force is required. And the change in its speed (or in the direction of its motion) would continue as long as this unbalanced force is applied [S1]. However, if this force is removed completely, the object would continue to move with the velocity it has acquired till then [S1].

## Worked example

Imagine two friends are playing tug-of-war with a heavy rope. One friend pulls the rope to the left with a force of $200 \text{ N}$. The other friend pulls the rope to the right with a force of $150 \text{ N}$. 

To find the net force, we subtract the smaller force from the larger force and note the direction of the larger force:
$$200 \text{ N} - 150 \text{ N} = 50 \text{ N}$$

Because $200 \text{ N}$ is greater than $150 \text{ N}$, the forces are unbalanced. The net force acting on the rope is $50 \text{ N}$ towards the left [S1]. According to the rule, the rope will begin to move in the direction of the greater force, which is to the left.

## Key points

- A force is a push or a pull on an object resulting from the object's interaction with another object [S1].
- Forces result only when two or more objects interact with each other.
- Contact forces require physical contact between objects, and muscular force is a common example [S1].
- Non-contact forces, like magnetic force, can act on objects without physical contact [S1].
- Balanced forces do not change the state of rest or of motion of an object, while unbalanced forces do [S1].
- Friction acts opposite to the direction of motion between two surfaces in contact [S1].
- An object moves with a uniform velocity when the forces acting on it are balanced [S1].
