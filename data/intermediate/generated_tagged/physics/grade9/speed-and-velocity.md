# Speed and Velocity

When you ride a bicycle to school, you might want to know how fast you are going, or exactly where you are heading. Speed tells us how fast an object is moving, while velocity tells us both how fast it is moving and the direction it is moving in. These two ideas are the foundation of motion, and they help us describe everything from a cricket ball flying through the air to a train travelling across the country.

## What is Speed?

Speed is the most basic way to describe how quickly an object covers distance. It is simply the total distance an object travels divided by the total time it takes to travel that distance. The formula for speed is given by the equation:

$$
\text{speed} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}}
$$

The standard unit for speed in science is metres per second, or $\text{m s}^{-1}$. If you walk 100 metres in 20 seconds, your speed is 5 metres per second. Speed is a scalar quantity, which means it only has a magnitude and no direction. It tells us the rate of motion, but it does not tell us which way the object is going.

## What is Velocity?

While speed only tells us how fast an object is moving, velocity tells us how fast it is moving in a specific direction. The quantity that specifies both speed and direction is called velocity. Velocity is the speed of an object moving in a definite direction [S1]. 

Because velocity includes direction, it is a vector quantity. For example, if a bus is moving at 50 kilometres per hour, that is its speed. However, if the bus is moving at 50 kilometres per hour towards the north, that is its velocity. The units for velocity are the same as for speed, which is $\text{m s}^{-1}$ or $\text{m/s}$ [S1].

## Uniform and Non-Uniform Motion

Objects do not always move at a constant rate. We can divide motion into two main categories: uniform and non-uniform. During uniform motion, an object moves along a straight line at a constant speed. In this case, the velocity remains constant with time, meaning the change in velocity for any time interval is zero [S2]. 

On the other hand, non-uniform motion occurs when an object's speed or direction changes over time. In non-uniform motion, velocity varies with time. It has different values at different instants and at different points of the path. Thus, the change in velocity of the object during any time interval is not zero [S2]. 

For example, a car driving on a highway might maintain a steady 60 kilometres per hour, which is uniform motion. However, if that same car enters a busy city street and keeps stopping and starting, its motion is non-uniform.

## Average Velocity

When an object is moving along a straight line at a variable speed, we can express the magnitude of its rate of motion in terms of average velocity. It is calculated in the same way as we calculate average speed [S1]. 

However, there is a special case for when the velocity of an object is changing at a uniform rate. In this situation, the average velocity is given by the arithmetic mean of the initial velocity and the final velocity for a given period of time. Mathematically, this is expressed as:

$$
\mathcal {V} _ {a v} = \frac {u + v}{2}\tag{7.2}
$$

where $v_{av}$ is the average velocity, $u$ is the initial velocity and $v$ is the final velocity of the object [S1].

## Acceleration: The Rate of Change of Velocity

If an object's velocity is changing, we need a way to measure how quickly it is changing. To do this, we introduce a physical quantity called acceleration. Acceleration is a measure of the change in the velocity of an object per unit time [S2]. 

If the velocity of an object changes from an initial value $u$ to a final value $v$ in time $t$, the acceleration $a$ is given by the formula:

$$
a = \frac {v - u}{t}\tag{7.3}
$$

This kind of motion is known as accelerated motion. The acceleration is taken to be positive if it is in the direction of velocity and negative when it is opposite to the direction of velocity. The SI unit of acceleration is $\text{m s}^{-2}$ [S2].

If an object travels in a straight line and its velocity increases or decreases by equal amounts in equal intervals of time, then the acceleration of the object is said to be uniform. The motion of a freely falling body is an example of uniformly accelerated motion. On the other hand, an object can travel with non-uniform acceleration if its velocity changes at a non-uniform rate. For example, if a car travelling along a straight road increases its speed by unequal amounts in equal intervals of time, then the car is said to be moving with non-uniform acceleration [S2].

## Velocity-Time Graphs

We can represent the variation in velocity with time for an object moving in a straight line by using a velocity-time graph. In this graph, time is represented along the $x$-axis and the velocity is represented along the $y$-axis [S3]. 

If the object moves at uniform velocity, the height of its velocity-time graph will not change with time. It will be a straight line parallel to the $x$-axis. For instance, a car moving with uniform velocity of $40 \, \text{km h}^{-1}$ will have a horizontal line on the graph [S3].

We know that the product of velocity and time gives the displacement of an object moving with uniform velocity. The area enclosed by the velocity-time graph and the time axis will be equal to the magnitude of the displacement. For a uniform velocity graph, the distance moved can be calculated by finding the area of the rectangle formed by the velocity and time intervals.

We can also study about uniformly accelerated motion by plotting its velocity-time graph. For all uniformly accelerated motion, the velocity-time graph is a straight line. The area under the velocity-time graph gives the distance (magnitude of displacement) moved by the car in a given interval of time [S3]. 

In the case of non-uniformly accelerated motion, velocity-time graphs can have any shape. For example, a graph might show a curve, indicating that the velocity is changing at a non-uniform rate over time [S3].

## Worked Example

Let us look at a practical application of the formula for average velocity. Imagine a sprinter running a race. She starts from a standstill and finishes the race with a final velocity of $8 \, \text{m s}^{-1}$. Because she started from rest, her initial velocity is $0 \, \text{m s}^{-1}$. 

Using the formula for average velocity when acceleration is uniform, we can calculate her average velocity during the race:

$$
\mathcal {V} _ {a v} = \frac {u + v}{2} = \frac {0 + 8}{2}
$$

$$
\mathcal {V} _ {a v} = 4 \, \text{m s}^{-1}
$$

This means that, on average, the sprinter was moving at a velocity of 4 metres per second in her direction of motion.

## Key points

*   Speed is the total distance divided by the total time, and it has no direction.
*   Velocity is speed in a definite direction, making it a vector quantity.
*   Uniform motion means constant velocity and zero change in velocity over time.
*   Non-uniform motion means the velocity changes over time, requiring the concept of acceleration.
*   Acceleration is the change in velocity per unit time, measured in $\text{m s}^{-2}$.
*   The area under a velocity-time graph represents the magnitude of the displacement.
*   For uniform acceleration, the average velocity is the arithmetic mean of the initial and final velocities.
