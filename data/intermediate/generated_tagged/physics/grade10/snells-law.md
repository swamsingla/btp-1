
# Snell's Law

When you look at a straw in a glass of water, it looks bent. This happens because light changes direction when it moves from the water into the air. This bending of light is called refraction, and it is a fundamental concept in physics. The amount by which the light bends depends on the specific materials it travels through. To predict exactly how much the light will bend, we use a very useful mathematical rule. This rule is called Snell's Law, and it is a cornerstone of optics. It helps us understand everything from how our eyes work to how lenses and prisms are designed. By learning this law, you can calculate the exact path a ray of light will take as it crosses the boundary between two different transparent materials.

## Refractive Index and Speed

To understand Snell's Law, you first need to know about the refractive index. The refractive index of a medium is a number that tells us how much that medium slows down light. Light travels fastest in a vacuum, at a speed of $3 \times 10^{8}$ m s$^{-1}$ [S1]. In air, the speed of light is only marginally less compared to a vacuum [S1]. However, when light enters a denser material like glass or water, it slows down considerably [S1]. 

The refractive index is calculated by comparing the speed of light in two different media. Let us say light travels from medium 1 into medium 2. The refractive index of medium 2 with respect to medium 1 is given by the ratio of the speed of light in medium 1 to the speed of light in medium 2. This is usually represented by the symbol $n_{21}$. This relationship is expressed in the equation $n_{21} = \frac{v_1}{v_2}$ [S1]. 

Sometimes, we measure the refractive index against a vacuum or air, which we consider the absolute refractive index [S1]. If $c$ is the speed of light in air and $v$ is the speed of light in a medium, then the refractive index of the medium $n_m$ is given by $n_m = \frac{c}{v}$ [S1]. 

## Optical Density and Direction

The refractive index is directly linked to a medium's optical density. Optical density is not the same as mass density, which is how heavy a material is for its size [S1]. Instead, optical density refers to a medium's ability to refract, or bend, light. When comparing two media, the one with the larger refractive index is the optically denser medium [S1]. The other medium, with the lower refractive index, is the optically rarer medium [S1]. 

Because of this difference in refractive index, light changes its speed and direction when it moves from one medium to another. When a ray of light travels from a rarer medium to a denser medium, it slows down and bends towards the normal [S1]. The normal is an imaginary line drawn perpendicular to the surface at the exact point where the light hits. Conversely, when light travels from a denser medium to a rarer medium, it speeds up and bends away from the normal [S1]. 

## The Law of Refraction

Now we can define Snell's Law. It is the rule that describes exactly how much a light ray bends when it passes from one medium into another. The law states that the product of the refractive index and the sine of the angle is constant across the boundary. 

The formula for Snell's Law is $n_1 \sin \theta_1 = n_2 \sin \theta_2$ [S1]. 

In this formula, $n_1$ is the refractive index of the first medium, and $n_2$ is the refractive index of the second medium. The angles, $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$, are the angles of incidence and refraction. Both angles are measured from the normal to the surface [S1, S2]. 

This equation is incredibly useful because it allows you to calculate one unknown value if you know the other three. For example, if you know the refractive indices of the two materials and the angle at which the light enters, you can use this law to find out exactly what angle the light will take inside the second material. It is a fundamental principle that governs the behaviour of light at the boundary of any two transparent substances.

## Worked Example

Let us apply Snell's Law to a real-world scenario to see how it works in practice. Imagine you have a block of crown glass, and you shine a light ray into it from air. You want to find out how much the light will bend. 

First, we need to know the refractive indices of the two media. The refractive index of air is approximately 1.0003, and the refractive index of crown glass is 1.52 [S1, S3]. Let us assume the light hits the glass at an angle of incidence, $\theta_1$, of 30 degrees. 

Using Snell's Law, we can set up the equation: $1.0003 \times \sin(30^\circ) = 1.52 \times \sin(\theta_2)$. 

We know that the sine of 30 degrees is 0.5. So, $1.0003 \times 0.5 = 1.52 \times \sin(\theta_2)$. 

This simplifies to $0.50015 = 1.52 \times \sin(\theta_2)$. 

To find $\sin(\theta_2)$, we divide both sides by 1.52, which gives us $\sin(\theta_2) = 0.329$. 

To find the angle $\theta_2$, we take the inverse sine of 0.329, which is approximately 19.2 degrees. 

This means that the light ray, which entered the crown glass at 30 degrees, will bend towards the normal and travel inside the glass at an angle of about 19.2 degrees. This calculation shows exactly how the denser medium slows the light down and changes its path, perfectly demonstrating the principles of Snell's Law.

## Key points

*   Snell's Law describes how a ray of light bends when it moves from one transparent medium to another.
*   The formula for Snell's Law is $n_1 \sin \theta_1 = n_2 \sin \theta_2$, where $n$ is the refractive index and $\theta$ is the angle measured from the normal [S1].
*   The refractive index is the ratio of the speed of light in the first medium to the speed of light in the second medium, represented by $n_{21} = \frac{v_1}{v_2}$ [S1].
*   Light slows down and bends towards the normal when it travels from an optically rarer medium to an optically denser medium [S1].
*   Light speeds up and bends away from the normal when it travels from an optically denser medium to an optically rarer medium [S1].
