
# Snell's Law

When light travels from one transparent material to another, it does not always travel in a straight line. The change in direction at the boundary between two media is called refraction. Snell's Law provides the precise mathematical relationship that governs this bending. It is a foundational rule in physics because it allows us to predict exactly how a light ray will change direction when it enters a new medium. This law is essential for designing lenses, prisms, and the fiber optic cables that form the backbone of modern telecommunications.

## The Law and the Refractive Index

Snell's Law states that the product of the refractive index of the first medium and the sine of the angle of incidence is equal to the product of the refractive index of the second medium and the sine of the angle of refraction [S1]. Mathematically, this is expressed as:

$$
n_1 \sin i = n_2 \sin r
$$

In this equation, $i$ represents the angle of incidence and $r$ represents the angle of refraction [S2]. Both angles are measured from the normal to the surface, which is an imaginary line drawn perpendicular to the boundary at the point where the light strikes. The refractive index, denoted by $n$, is a measure of how much a medium slows down light compared to its speed in a vacuum [S2]. The speed of light in a vacuum is represented by $c$, and the speed of light in any medium is $v$. The relationship between these speeds and the refractive index is given by the formulas:

$$
n_1 = \frac{c}{v_1}
$$

and

$$
n_2 = \frac{c}{v_2}
$$

Because the refractive index is defined as the ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum to the speed of light in the medium, a higher refractive index means that light travels more slowly in that medium [S2]. 

## The Speed of Light and Wavelength

The bending of light is directly related to how fast light travels in the two different media. If a light ray bends toward the normal when it enters a new medium, it means the speed of the light wave in the second medium is less than the speed in the first medium [S2]. This prediction is a crucial result of the wave theory of light. 

When light moves from one medium to another, its speed and wavelength change, but its frequency remains exactly the same [S1]. The relationship between speed, wavelength, and frequency is given by the equation $v = \nu \lambda$. Because the frequency $\nu$ stays constant, the ratio of the wavelengths in the two media is equal to the ratio of the speeds of light in those media [S1]:

$$
\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2} = \frac{v_1}{v_2}
$$

This means that when light enters a denser medium where it slows down, its wavelength decreases proportionally [S1].

## Total Internal Reflection

Snell's Law also explains a phenomenon called total internal reflection, which occurs when light travels from a denser medium to a rarer medium. The critical angle is the specific angle of incidence that results in an angle of refraction of exactly 90 degrees [S4]. 

According to Snell's Law, if the angle of incidence is larger than the critical angle, the law cannot be satisfied because the sine of the angle of refraction would have to be greater than 1, which is mathematically impossible [S4]. When this happens, the light does not pass into the second medium at all; instead, it is entirely reflected back into the first medium [S4]. The refractive index of a denser medium with respect to a rarer medium can be calculated directly from the critical angle using the formula:

$$
n_{12} = \frac{1}{\sin i_c}
$$

For example, the critical angle for water with respect to air is 48.75 degrees, while for dense flint glass it is 37.31 degrees [S4]. 

## Worked example

Consider a ray of light traveling from air into a glass prism. The refractive index of air is approximately 1.00, and the refractive index of dense flint glass is 1.62 [S4]. If the ray of light strikes the glass at an angle of incidence of 30 degrees, we can use Snell's Law to find the angle of refraction. 

Using the formula $n_1 \sin i = n_2 \sin r$, we substitute the known values. The angle of incidence $i$ is 30 degrees, so $\sin(30)$ is 0.5. The equation becomes:

$$
1.00 \times 0.5 = 1.62 \times \sin r
$$

Solving for $\sin r$ gives:

$$
\sin r = \frac{0.5}{1.62} \approx 0.3086
$$

Taking the inverse sine of 0.3086, we find that the angle of refraction $r$ is approximately 18.0 degrees. Because the light entered a denser medium where the speed of light is lower, the ray bent toward the normal, and the angle of refraction is smaller than the angle of incidence.

## Key points

* Snell's Law is expressed as $n_1 \sin i = n_2 \sin r$, relating the refractive indices and the angles of incidence and refraction [S1, S2].
* The refractive index $n$ is defined as the ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum ($c$) to the speed of light in the medium ($v$) [S2].
* When light is refracted into a denser medium, its speed and wavelength decrease, but its frequency remains unchanged [S1].
* Total internal reflection occurs when light travels from a denser to a rarer medium at an angle of incidence greater than the critical angle [S4].
* The refractive index of a denser medium with respect to a rarer medium is the reciprocal of the sine of the critical angle ($n_{12} = 1 / \sin i_c$) [S4].
