# Human Health and Disease — Detail

Human health is a complex interplay between our bodies and the microscopic organisms that surround us. While many of these organisms are harmless or even beneficial, some act as pathogens that can cause a wide range of diseases. Understanding how these pathogens invade, multiply, and damage our systems is essential for developing effective treatments and prevention strategies. This page details specific diseases caused by various pathogens and outlines the crucial measures we take to control them.

## Bacterial and Viral Infections

Pathogens are disease-causing organisms belonging to bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoans, helminths, and other groups, which enter our bodies through various means, multiply, and interfere with normal vital activities, resulting in morphological and functional damage [S1]. 

Typhoid fever is caused by the pathogenic bacterium *Salmonella typhi*, which generally enters the small intestine through food and water contaminated with them and migrates to other organs through blood [S1]. Sustained high fever (39° to 40°C), weakness, stomach pain, constipation, headache, and loss of appetite are some of the common symptoms of this disease [S1]. Intestinal perforation and death may occur in severe cases. 

Pneumonia is another significant bacterial disease, caused by *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and *Haemophilus influenzae*, which infect the alveoli (air-filled sacs) of the lungs [S1]. As a result of the infection, the alveoli get filled with fluid leading to severe problems in respiration [S1]. The symptoms of pneumonia include fever, chills, cough, and headache, and in severe cases, the lips and fingernails may turn gray to bluish in colour [S1]. A healthy person acquires the infection by inhaling the droplets/aerosols released by an infected person or even by sharing glasses and utensils with an infected person [S1].

Viruses also cause numerous human ailments. Rhino viruses represent one such group of viruses which cause one of the most infectious human ailments – the common cold [S1]. They infect the nose and respiratory passage but not the lungs [S1]. The common cold is characterised by nasal congestion and discharge, sore throat, hoarseness, cough, headache, tiredness, etc., which usually last for 3-7 days [S1]. Droplets resulting from cough or sneezes of an infected person are either inhaled directly or transmitted through contaminated objects such as pens, books, cups, doorknobs, computer keyboard or mouse, etc., and cause infection in a healthy person [S1].

## Protozoan and Helminth Diseases

Some of the human diseases are caused by protozoans too, such as malaria, a disease man has been fighting since many years [S1]. *Plasmodium*, a tiny protozoan is responsible for this disease [S1]. Different species of *Plasmodium* (*P. vivax*, *P. malariae* and *P. falciparum*) are responsible for different types of malaria [S1]. Of these, malignant malaria caused by *Plasmodium falciparum* is the most serious one and can even be fatal [S1].

*Plasmodium* enters the human body as sporozoites (infectious form) through the bite of infected female *Anopheles* mosquito [S1]. The parasites initially multiply within the liver cells and then attack the red blood cells (RBCs) resulting in their rupture [S1]. The rupture of RBCs is associated with release of a toxic substance, haemozoin, which is responsible for the chill and high fever recurring every three to four days [S1]. When a female *Anopheles* mosquito bites an infected person, these parasites enter the mosquito's body and undergo further development [S1]. The parasites multiply within them to form sporozoites that are stored in their salivary glands [S1]. When these mosquitoes bite a human, the sporozoites are introduced into his/her body, thereby initiating the events mentioned above [S1]. It is interesting to note that the malarial parasite requires two hosts – human and mosquitoes – to complete its life cycle; the female *Anopheles* mosquito is the vector (transmitting agent) too [S1].

*Entamoeba histolytica* is a protozoan parasite in the large intestine of human which causes amoebiasis (amoebic dysentery) [S1]. Symptoms of this disease include constipation, abdominal pain and cramps, stools with excess mucous and blood clots [S1]. Houseflies act as mechanical carriers and serve to transmit the parasite from faeces of infected person to food and food products, thereby contaminating them [S1]. Drinking water and food contaminated by the faecal matter are the main source of infection [S1].

Ascaris, the common round worm and Wuchereria, the filarial worm, are some of the helminths which are known to be pathogenic to man [S1]. Ascaris, an intestinal parasite causes ascariasis [S1]. Symptoms of these disease include internal bleeding, muscular pain, fever, anemia and blockage of the intestinal passage [S1]. The eggs of the parasite are excreted along with the faeces of infected persons which contaminate soil, water, plants, etc. [S1]. A healthy person acquires this infection through contaminated water, vegetables, fruits, etc. [S1].

Wuchereria (W. bancrofti and W. malayi), the filarial worms cause a slowly developing chronic inflammation of the organs in which they live for many years, usually the lymphatic vessels of the lower limbs and the disease is called elephantiasis or filariasis [S1]. The genital organs are also often affected, resulting in gross deformities [S1]. The pathogens are transmitted to a healthy person through the bite by the female mosquito vectors [S1].

## Fungal Infections and Prevention

Many fungi belonging to the genera *Microsporum*, *Trichophyton* and *Epidermophyton* are responsible for ringworms which is one of the most common infectious diseases in man [S1]. Appearance of dry, scaly lesions on various parts of the body such as skin, nails and scalp are the main symptoms of the disease [S1]. These lesions are accompanied by intense itching [S1]. Heat and moisture help these fungi to grow, which makes them thrive in skin folds such as those in the groin or between the toes [S1]. Ringworms are generally acquired from soil or by using towels, clothes or even the comb of infected individuals [S1].

Maintenance of personal and public hygiene is very important for prevention and control of many infectious diseases [S1]. Measures for personal hygiene include keeping the body clean; consumption of clean drinking water, food, vegetables, fruits, etc. [S1]. Public hygiene includes proper disposal of waste and excreta; periodic cleaning and disinfection of water reservoirs, pools, cesspools and tanks and observing standard practices of hygiene in public catering [S1]. These measures are particularly essential where the infectious agents are transmitted through food and water such as typhoid, amoebiasis and ascariasis [S1]. In cases of air-borne diseases such as pneumonia and common cold, in addition to the above measures, close contact with the infected persons or their belongings should be avoided [S1]. For diseases such as malaria and filariasis that are transmitted through insect vectors, the most important measure is to control or eliminate the vectors and their breeding places [S1]. This can be achieved by avoiding stagnation of water in and around residential areas, regular cleaning of household coolers, use of mosquito nets, introducing fishes like Gambusia in ponds that feed on mosquito larvae, spraying of insecticides in ditches, drainage areas and swamps, etc. [S1]. In addition, doors and windows should be provided with wire mesh to prevent the entry of mosquitoes [S1]. Such precautions have become more important especially in the light of recent widespread incidences of the vector-borne (Aedes mosquitoes) diseases like dengue and chikungunya in many parts of India [S1].

The advancements made in biological science have armed us to effectively deal with many infectious diseases [S1]. The use of vaccines and immunisation programmes have enabled us to completely eradicate a deadly disease like smallpox [S1]. A large number of other infectious diseases like polio, diphtheria, pneumonia and tetanus have been controlled to a large extent by the use of vaccines [S1]. Biotechnology is at the verge of making available newer and safer vaccines [S1]. Discovery of antibiotics and various other drugs has also enabled us to effectively treat infectious diseases [S1].

## Worked example

Consider a scenario where a student in India is trying to prevent mosquito-borne diseases like malaria and filariasis. According to the principles of vector control, the student must eliminate the breeding places of the female *Anopheles* and Wuchereria mosquitoes. If the student has a household water cooler, they should empty and clean it regularly to prevent water stagnation, which serves as a breeding ground [S1]. Additionally, the student should ensure that their doors and windows have wire mesh to physically block the entry of these insect vectors [S1]. By applying these two specific rules—cleaning stagnant water and installing mesh—the student effectively reduces the local vector population and minimizes the risk of contracting these chronic inflammatory diseases.

## Key points

- Pathogens are disease-causing organisms that enter the body, multiply, and cause morphological and functional damage [S1].
- Typhoid fever and pneumonia are bacterial diseases transmitted through contaminated food, water, or inhaled droplets from infected persons [S1].
- The common cold is a viral disease spread through droplets or contaminated objects, while malaria is a protozoan disease requiring both human and mosquito hosts [S1].
- Amoebiasis is spread by houseflies as mechanical carriers, and ascariasis is contracted by ingesting parasite eggs from contaminated soil, water, or food [S1].
- Elephantiasis (filariasis) is caused by filarial worms transmitted through the bite of female mosquito vectors, leading to chronic inflammation of lymphatic vessels [S1].
- Ringworm is a fungal infection thriving in heat and moisture, acquired from soil or infected personal items [S1].
- Prevention relies on personal and public hygiene, avoiding vector breeding sites, and utilizing vaccines and antibiotics for treatment [S1].
