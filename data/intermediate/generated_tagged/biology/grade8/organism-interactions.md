# Interactions Among Organisms

Organisms do not live alone. In any habitat, many kinds of living things share the same space, food and water, and they depend on one another and on the non-living things around them [S4]. This page looks at how living things are grouped in nature and how they interact.

## Populations and Communities

You may already know that a habitat is the place where organisms live. If you look at fish in a pond, you will probably see many fish of the same kind. This group of fish of the same kind living together in a pond habitat is called a population of that fish [S1]. We can understand the population of a plant by counting the plants at a given place and time, for example in a $1 \mathrm{~m} \times 1 \mathrm{~m}$ area of a school garden. So a population is a group of the same type of organisms in a habitat at a given time [S2].

Can a habitat have only one type of living organism? If all the organisms were the same, they would have the same requirements for food, water and space, which would lead to competition and possible scarcity of resources [S2]. In nature, different groups of organisms live together in a habitat. A community comprises different populations sharing the same habitat. The biotic components of a habitat, such as plants, animals and microorganisms, together form the community, and these organisms interact and depend on one another for survival [S2].

## Interactions Among Living Things and with Their Surroundings

Living organisms, or the biotic community, depend on non-living things, which are called abiotic components, for their survival. Plants and animals also depend on each other for nutrition, respiration and reproduction. These are interactions among the biotic components. Both kinds of interaction, among biotic components and between biotic and abiotic components, are important for survival in any habitat [S4].

Interactions among biotic components can influence the availability of resources needed for life processes like nutrition, respiration and reproduction [S5]. A frog eats insects, and a water snake eats fish. Frogs and fish may compete for small insect larvae. A fish lays its eggs in water near vegetation, which protects them from other fish or frogs [S5].

Abiotic components matter just as much. Sunlight, carbon dioxide and water are essential for plants to make food. Soil provides a medium and essential nutrients for plant growth. Air provides oxygen for respiration in plants as well as animals, and water is essential for all living organisms. This shows how living organisms depend on the non-living parts of their surroundings [S6].

The dependence goes both ways. Plants release oxygen during photosynthesis, their roots hold soil in place and prevent erosion, and plants retain soil moisture and help cool the atmosphere [S7].

## Ecosystems

The biotic components (plants, animals and microorganisms) and the abiotic components (air, water, soil, sunlight and temperature) of a habitat interact with each other to form an ecosystem. Organisms in an ecosystem interact with abiotic components for food, shelter and protection [S6]. There are two main types of ecosystems in nature. Aquatic ecosystems include ponds, rivers and lakes, while terrestrial ecosystems include forests, farms or even large trees like banyan, mango or pilkhan. Hence, ecosystems can be large or small [S6].

Ecosystems can also overlap. A small river is an aquatic ecosystem, but it can lie among mountains, forests, grassland and farmland, which are examples of terrestrial ecosystems. Farmland is a human-made ecosystem. These ecosystems interact with each other at any given point [S6].

## Producers and Consumers

Organisms also depend on one another for food. Plants make their own food by the process of photosynthesis, so they are called producers or autotrophs [S8]. Organisms that cannot produce their own food and depend on other organisms for their food are called consumers or heterotrophs. Consumers can be grouped by what they eat. Herbivores eat only plants, such as deer and hare. Carnivores eat only animals, such as the leopard. Omnivores eat both plants and animals, such as crows, foxes and mice [S8].

## Worked example

Let us look at a pond with fish. Researchers compared two ponds. Pond A had fish and a large number of flowering plants around it. Pond B had no fish and fewer flowering plants around it. Pond A also had fewer dragonflies than Pond B [S3]. Why?

Fish eat dragonfly larvae, so a pond with fish has fewer dragonflies. Dragonflies usually eat flies, bees and butterflies, so with fewer dragonflies more bees, flies and butterflies were found. These insects help pollinate flowers by moving pollen from one flower to another, which helps plants produce seeds. So flowers near a pond with fish may produce more seeds than those near a pond without fish [S3].

This shows that fish have a direct effect on dragonflies and an indirect effect on the plants around the pond. It also shows how the biotic components (fish, dragonflies, pollinators, plants) and the abiotic components (temperature, water, nutrients) interact with and affect each other [S3].

## Key points

- A population is a group of the same type of organisms in a habitat at a given time; different populations sharing a habitat form a community [S2].
- Biotic components are plants, animals and microorganisms, and abiotic components are air, water, soil, sunlight and temperature; interactions among biotic components and between biotic and abiotic components are both important for survival [S4, S6].
- The biotic and abiotic components of a habitat interact to form an ecosystem, which may be aquatic or terrestrial, large or small [S6].
- Producers (autotrophs) make their own food, while consumers (heterotrophs) depend on other organisms for food; consumers include herbivores, carnivores and omnivores [S8].
- A change in one population, such as the fish in a pond, can affect other organisms indirectly, such as flowering plants [S3].
