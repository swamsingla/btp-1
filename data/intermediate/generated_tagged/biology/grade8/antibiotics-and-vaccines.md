# Antibiotics and Vaccines

When our immune system fails to protect us against an infectious disease, we fall ill and need to visit a doctor [S1]. The doctor may give us medicines called antibiotics, which kill the bacteria that might have caused the disease [S1]. Vaccines also help us, but they work differently: they prepare our body to fight diseases before we ever get sick. Understanding both of these tools is very important in biology.

## What are Antibiotics?

Antibiotics are special medicines that doctors give us when bacteria make us sick. They work only against bacterial infections because they target parts of bacterial cells that are different from human or other animal cells [S1]. They do not work against viruses or diseases caused by protozoa [S1]. If you have a cold or the flu, which are caused by viruses, taking antibiotics will not help you get better.

## The Discovery of Penicillin

The first antibiotic used to treat bacterial infections was discovered in 1928 by Alexander Fleming, a bacteriologist from London [S1]. While studying harmful bacteria, he noticed that a mould on a discarded petri dish stopped the bacteria from growing [S1]. He realized the mould released a substance that killed the bacteria. This chance discovery led to the discovery of penicillin, the first antibiotic [S1]. Antibiotics have saved millions of lives since their discovery [S1].

## The Problem of Antibiotic Resistance

Though antibiotics are effective in protecting us against bacterial infections and have saved millions of lives since their discovery, their indiscriminate use has led to a decline in their effectiveness [S1]. Nowadays, there are news headlines about antibiotic resistance, a phenomenon where bacteria that were earlier killed by a given antibiotic are found to survive and multiply despite treatment with that antibiotic [S1]. This makes common infections harder to treat and increases the risk of complications, prolonged illness, and even death [S1].

## How Vaccines Work

Vaccines work by training your immune system to recognize and fight specific germs. They help your body build a defense so that if the real disease-causing germs enter your body, your immune system already knows how to destroy them. This is why getting vaccinated is a very important step in staying healthy.

## The Importance of Immunisation

The phrase ‘Prevention is better than cure’ is very true [S2]. It is important to protect ourselves from both communicable and non-communicable diseases [S2]. Immunisation, which is the process of getting vaccines, is one of the best ways to prevent diseases. By getting vaccinated on time, you protect yourself, your family, and your community from serious illnesses.

## Worked Example

Let us look at a simple example to understand how vaccines help. Imagine that the germs of a disease enter your body. If you have not been vaccinated against that disease, your immune system has to learn how to fight these germs from scratch. This takes time, and you might become very ill during that time. If you have been vaccinated against that same disease, your immune system has already been trained to recognise those germs, so it can respond much faster. A vaccine protects only against the disease it was made for. It does not protect against other diseases, and it does not replace an antibiotic when bacteria have already made someone ill.

## Key points

* Antibiotics are medicines that kill bacteria, but they do not work against viruses or protozoa [S1].
* Penicillin was the first antibiotic, discovered in 1928 by Alexander Fleming [S1].
* The indiscriminate use of antibiotics has led to antibiotic resistance, which makes common infections harder to treat [S1].
* Vaccines train the immune system to fight a disease before it makes you ill.
* Prevention is better than cure, and immunisation is one way to prevent disease [S2].
