# Solar System

There are perhaps billions of planets in the universe, but Earth is the only one where life, as we know it today, exists and thrives in all its forms [S1]. Earth is one of eight planets that go around the Sun [S3]. This page looks at what makes our planet so special.

## A Thin Layer of Life

All the mountains, rivers, forests, animals and people are found on just a very thin layer on the surface of our planet [S1]. From the tallest mountain to the deepest ocean trench, the crust, where all of life exists, is tiny compared to the size of Earth. If Earth were the size of an apple, the crust would be as thin as the apple's skin [S1]. This delicate, life-supporting layer is what makes Earth truly special [S1]. Earth provides us with the air that we breathe, the water that we drink and the soil that helps in growing crops. It also gives us materials like rock and timber with which we build our homes, buildings and roads [S2].

## Why Venus Is the Hottest Planet

You may already know that the eight planets, in order of increasing distance from the Sun, are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune, and that they go around the Sun in nearly circular orbits [S3]. All planets get their energy from the Sun, so a planet close to the Sun should be very hot, and planets farther away should be colder [S4]. This is generally correct, except that Venus, the second planet from the Sun, has the highest average temperature and is the hottest planet [S4]. Venus is the hottest not because it is the closest to the Sun, but because its thick atmosphere traps heat [S4].

The air on Venus is almost entirely made up of carbon dioxide gas, which does not let the heat escape. This is called the greenhouse effect, and it makes Venus even hotter than Mercury, which is closer to the Sun [S5]. On Earth also, gases like carbon dioxide in the atmosphere trap heat by absorbing the radiation given off by the Earth after it gets warmed by the Sun. So the greenhouse effect plays an important role in maintaining just the right temperature on Earth [S5].

## The Right Distance from the Sun

The most important reason why Earth can support life is its distance from the Sun. It is at just the right distance, where the temperature allows water to exist in a liquid form [S6]. If Earth were closer to the Sun, it would be too hot and all the water would evaporate; if it were farther away, it would be too cold and all the water would freeze. In such extreme conditions it would be impossible for most life forms, especially plants, animals and humans, to grow and thrive on Earth [S6]. The range of distances from the Sun (or another star) over which water remains liquid is called the habitable zone, or sometimes the 'Goldilocks zone' [S6].

Most of Earth's surface is covered with water. When seen from space, Earth looks blue because of the vast amount of water, which is why it is called the Blue Planet [S6]. The orbits of most planets, including Earth, are almost circular. This keeps the amount of sunlight and heat nearly steady throughout the year, preventing extreme summers and winters at most places [S7].

## The Right Size and an Atmosphere

Moderate temperature is not the only factor that makes Earth habitable. The planet is also the right size to support an atmosphere, the layer of gases that surrounds Earth [S7]. Earth's gravity pulls objects towards it. If Earth were much smaller, its gravity would be too weak to hold on to the gases of the atmosphere, and they would escape into space. On Mars the atmosphere is 100 times thinner than on Earth, and Mercury has no atmosphere at all [S7]. If a planet were too large, its gravity would be much stronger and would perhaps pull us down with such a large force that our bones could get crushed [S7].

The oxygen in the atmosphere allows us to breathe and is needed by almost all forms of life on Earth. Some of this oxygen gets converted to ozone, a three-atom oxygen molecule, which forms the ozone layer. This layer acts like a shield, blocking harmful ultraviolet (UV) rays from the Sun that can damage living cells [S7].

## The Magnetic Shield

A freely suspended magnet always settles in a fixed direction because the Earth itself behaves like a giant magnet. It is believed that the movement of molten iron in Earth's core may be the origin of Earth's magnetic field [S8]. Earth is constantly hit by tiny, high-energy particles from space. Some come from far across the universe and are called cosmic rays. Others come from the Sun and are called the solar wind [S8]. These particles can damage the atmosphere, reduce the ozone layer and let in more harmful UV rays, which can affect life on Earth [S8].

Thankfully, the Earth's magnetic field acts like a protective shield. It pushes many of these harmful particles away from Earth, keeping our atmosphere, and hence life on our planet, safe [S8].

## Worked example

Compare Mercury and Venus. Mercury is closer to the Sun than Venus, so you might expect it to be hotter. But the average temperature of Mercury is 170 °C, while that of Venus is 450 °C, so Venus is hotter by $450 - 170 = 280$ °C [S4]. The reason is the atmosphere. Mercury has no atmosphere, while Venus has a thick atmosphere made up almost entirely of carbon dioxide, which traps heat [S4, S5]. So the temperature of a planet depends not only on its distance from the Sun but also on its atmosphere.

## Key points

- Earth is the only planet known to have life, and all of that life is found in a very thin layer on its surface [S1].
- Venus is the hottest planet, not because it is the closest to the Sun but because its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat; on Earth the greenhouse effect keeps the temperature just right [S4, S5].
- Earth is at the right distance from the Sun for water to stay liquid, in the habitable zone [S6].
- Earth's size lets it hold an atmosphere with oxygen and an ozone layer that blocks harmful UV rays [S7].
- Earth's magnetic field pushes away harmful particles from space, such as cosmic rays and the solar wind [S8].
