
# States of Matter

Everything around you is made of tiny bits. Some of these bits are packed tightly together, while others move around freely. This page explains the three main states of matter: solid, liquid, and gas. It shows how the arrangement and movement of these tiny bits decide what state a material is in. Understanding this helps you see why ice feels hard, water flows, and air fills a room.

## What is Matter?

Before we look at the different states, we need to know what matter is. Anything that occupies space and has mass is called matter [S2]. The mass gives the quantity of matter, and the units to measure it are gram (g) and kilogram (kg). The space occupied by matter is its volume. The units to measure the volume are litre (L) and millilitre (mL) [S2]. 

Mass tells us how much material there is, while volume tells us how much space it takes up. For example, a heavy rock has a lot of mass, and a large bucket of water has a lot of volume. You can measure these properties to understand the materials around you.

## The Solid State

In the solid state, the tiny bits of matter are packed very tightly together. They are arranged in a fixed pattern and cannot move around easily. Because they are so close, solids have a definite shape and volume. If you drop a solid, it will keep its shape because the bits are locked in place [S1].

A great everyday example is ice. Ice is the solid state of water. When you heat ice, it melts and gets converted into its liquid state. This happens because the heat makes the tightly packed bits start to move a little bit [S1].

## The Liquid State

In the liquid state, the tiny bits are still close together, but they are not locked in a fixed pattern. They can slide past one another, which allows the material to flow. Liquids have a definite volume, but they take the shape of whatever container you put them in.

When you heat liquid water, it gets converted into its gaseous state. This means the bits gain enough energy to break free from each other and spread out completely.

## The Gaseous State

In the gaseous state, the tiny bits are very far apart and move around very quickly. They take up a lot of space and spread out to fill any container. Because the bits are so far apart, gases do not have a fixed shape or a fixed volume [S1].

## Changing the States

You can change a material from one state to another by adding or removing heat. If you want to change ice into water, you must supply heat to it. If you want to change water into ice, you must place it in a cold environment, such as a freezer, to cool it down [S3].

This process of changing states is not just for water. A candle is made of wax, which is a solid. If you heat the wax, it melts and changes into a liquid state. If you cool the liquid wax, it changes back into a solid [S3]. You can see this happen with coconut oil during the winter season, too.

## Worked Example

Let us look at a practical example using the rules of mass and volume. Imagine you have a block of ice that weighs exactly 5 kg. The mass of this ice is 5 kg, and it takes up a certain amount of space in your freezer. Now, you put the ice on a stove and heat it until it melts completely. The mass of the melted water is still 5 kg because heating does not change the amount of matter, it only changes its state [S2].

Next, you take this 5 kg of water and pour it into a measuring jug. You measure its volume and find it is 5 L. Finally, you boil the water until it turns into steam. The steam now takes up a much larger space in the air, but its mass is still 5 kg. This shows that heating changes the volume and arrangement of the bits, but the mass stays the same.

## Key points

- Anything that occupies space and has mass is called matter [S2].
- The mass of matter is measured in grams (g) and kilograms (kg) [S2].
- The volume of matter is measured in litres (L) and millilitres (mL) [S2].
- In a solid, the bits are tightly packed and have a fixed shape [S1].
- In a liquid, the bits can slide past each other and flow [S1].
- In a gas, the bits are far apart, move quickly, and spread out [S1].
- Heating a solid turns it into a liquid, and heating a liquid turns it into a gas [S1, S3].
- Cooling a liquid turns it into a solid [S3].
