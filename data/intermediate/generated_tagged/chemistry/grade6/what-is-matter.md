
# Matter and Its Properties

Everything you can touch, see, or feel in your daily life is made of something. From the wooden desk you sit at to the water you drink, every single thing takes up space and has mass. In science, we give a special name to all of these things. Understanding this concept is the very first step in chemistry because it helps us describe and group the physical world around us.

## What is Matter?

Any substance that has mass and occupies space is called matter [S1]. Mass tells us how much matter is present in an object. To measure this quantity, we use the gram (g) or the kilogram (kg) [S1]. On the other hand, the space that an object takes up is known as its volume. The units used to measure volume are the litre (L) and the millilitre (mL) [S1]. 

Because matter takes up space and has mass, it can be grouped and classified based on its properties. Just like we group living things, we can group non-living materials to study their patterns [S1].

## Measuring Mass

When we measure mass, we must always leave a space between the number and the unit. For example, if you have a mass of 7 kilograms, it should be written as 7 kg. It must never be written as 7 kgs. The kilogram is the standard unit of mass in the International System of Units, and it is abbreviated in lower case as kg. There is no space between the 'k' and the 'g', and no full stop is placed after the symbol, except at the end of a sentence [S1].

## Measuring Volume

The units for measuring volume also have specific rules for how they are written. The litre is abbreviated as a capital L. The millilitre is abbreviated as mL, where the 'm' is in lower case and the 'L' is in upper case. There is no space between the 'm' and the 'L' [S1]. For instance, 500 millilitres of water is written as 500 mL.

The SI unit for volume is the cubic metre, abbreviated as $m^{3}$. The abbreviation is written with a superscript 3 to show it is cubic [S1]. Just like with mass, you must always leave a space between the number and the unit. For example, a volume of 2 cubic metres is written as $2\ m^{3}$. It is also useful to know that $1\ m^{3} = 1000\ L$ [S1].

## Worked Example

Let us look at how to write measurements correctly using the rules for mass and volume. 

Imagine you are measuring ingredients for a school science project. You have a bag of sand that weighs 5 kilograms. According to the rules, you must write this as 5 kg. Next, you have a small beaker of water with a volume of 250 millilitres. You must write this as 250 mL. Finally, you have a large box that takes up a volume of 4 cubic metres. You must write this as $4\ m^{3}$. Notice how there is always a space between the number and the unit, and the abbreviations are written exactly as specified.

## Key points

- Anything that has mass and occupies space is called matter [S1].
- Mass is measured in grams (g) and kilograms (kg), and there must be a space between the number and the unit (e.g., 7 kg) [S1].
- Volume is the space occupied by matter and is measured in litres (L) and millilitres (mL) [S1].
- The SI unit for volume is the cubic metre ($m^{3}$), and it is written with a space between the number and the unit (e.g., $2\ m^{3}$) [S1].
- The relationship between cubic metres and litres is $1\ m^{3} = 1000\ L$ [S1].
