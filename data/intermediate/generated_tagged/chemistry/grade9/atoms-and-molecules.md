# Atoms and Molecules

Imagine you are watching a mason build a wall. The mason uses small grains of sand to build a room, and then combines many rooms to create a massive building. Just as a grain of sand is the fundamental building block of an ant-hill or a building, atoms are the fundamental building blocks of all matter that we can see, feel, or touch [S2]. These atoms are incredibly tiny, smaller than anything we can easily imagine or compare. If you were to stack millions of atoms on top of each other, the resulting layer would be barely as thick as a single sheet of paper [S4]. Even though we cannot see them with our naked eyes, our entire world is constantly made up of these invisible particles [S4].

## Dalton's Atomic Theory

The idea that matter is made of tiny, indivisible particles is not just a philosophy; it is a scientific theory. In 1808, British chemist John Dalton presented his atomic theory, which was a major turning point in the study of matter [S3]. Dalton stated that all matter, whether it is an element, a compound, or a mixture, is composed of small particles called atoms [S3]. 

Dalton's theory is based on a few key postulates:
*   All matter is made of very tiny particles called atoms, which participate in chemical reactions [S3].
*   Atoms are indivisible particles, which cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction [S3].
*   Atoms of a given element are identical in mass and chemical properties [S3].
*   Atoms of different elements have different masses and chemical properties [S3].
*   Atoms combine in the ratio of small whole numbers to form compounds [S3].
*   The relative number and kinds of atoms are constant in a given compound [S3].

This theory successfully provided an explanation for two important laws of chemistry: the law of conservation of mass and the law of definite proportions [S3]. 

## The Law of Definite Proportions

Long before Dalton, scientists like Lavoisier noticed a pattern in chemical compounds. They observed that many compounds are composed of two or more elements, and each such compound has the same elements in the same proportions, irrespective of where the compound came from or who prepared it [S3]. 

For example, in a compound such as water, the ratio of the mass of hydrogen to the mass of oxygen is always 1:8, whatever the source of water. Thus, if 9 g of water is decomposed, 1 g of hydrogen and 8 g of oxygen are always obtained. Similarly, in ammonia, nitrogen and hydrogen are always present in the ratio 14:3 by mass [S3]. 

This observation led to the law of constant proportions, which is also known as the law of definite proportions. This law was stated by Proust as: "In a chemical substance the elements are always present in definite proportions by mass" [S3]. Dalton's atomic theory provided the basic explanation for why this law holds true [S3].

## Atoms and Molecules

Atoms of most elements are not able to exist independently [S2]. Instead, they form molecules and ions. These molecules or ions aggregate in large numbers to form the matter that we can see, feel, or touch [S2].

The molecules of an element are constituted by the same type of atoms [S1]. Molecules of many elements, such as argon (Ar) and helium (He), are made up of only one atom of that element. But this is not the case with most of the nonmetals. For example, a molecule of oxygen consists of two atoms of oxygen and hence it is known as a diatomic molecule, $O_{2}$ [S1]. If 3 atoms of oxygen unite into a molecule, instead of the usual 2, we get ozone, $O_{3}$ [S1]. 

The number of atoms constituting a molecule is known as its atomicity [S1].

Metals and some other elements, such as carbon, do not have a simple structure but consist of a very large and indefinite number of atoms bonded together [S1].

## How Big Are Atoms?

Atoms are very small, they are smaller than anything that we can imagine or compare with. More than millions of atoms when stacked would make a layer barely as thick as this sheet of paper [S4].

We might think that if atoms are so insignificant in size, why should we care about them? This is because our entire world is made up of atoms. ## Worked example

Let us apply Dalton's atomic theory and the law of definite proportions to a real-world scenario. Imagine you are given a sample of water. According to the law of definite proportions, the ratio of the mass of hydrogen to the mass of oxygen is always 1:8, whatever the source of water [S3]. This means that if you decompose 9 g of water, you will always obtain 1 g of hydrogen and 8 g of oxygen [S3]. 

Now, let us consider a molecule of oxygen. A molecule of oxygen consists of two atoms of oxygen and hence it is known as a diatomic molecule, $O_{2}$ [S1]. The number of atoms constituting this molecule is 2, which is its atomicity [S1]. 

If you were to decompose a large amount of water, say 45 g, you would find that the ratio of the mass of hydrogen to the mass of oxygen is still 1:8 [S3]. This is because the relative number and kinds of atoms are constant in a given compound [S3].

## Key points

*   All matter is made of very tiny particles called atoms, which participate in chemical reactions [S2, S3].
*   Atoms are indivisible particles, which cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction [S3].
*   Atoms of a given element are identical in mass and chemical properties, while atoms of different elements have different masses and chemical properties [S3].
*   The law of definite proportions states that in a chemical substance, the elements are always present in definite proportions by mass [S3].
*   The molecules of an element are constituted by the same type of atoms [S1].
*   The number of atoms constituting a molecule is known as its atomicity [S1].
*   Atoms of most elements are not able to exist independently, but form molecules and ions [S2].
