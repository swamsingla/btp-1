# Atomic Number and Mass Number

Atoms are the tiny building blocks of everything around us, from the water we drink to the air we breathe. To understand how these atoms are organized, scientists use two important numbers: the atomic number and the mass number. These numbers tell us about the particles inside the nucleus, which is the very center of the atom. By learning these two values, you can figure out exactly what kind of element you are looking at and how heavy it is. This is a basic but very important step in chemistry that helps us understand the world at the smallest scale.

## What is the Atomic Number?

The atomic number is a special code that tells us what kind of element an atom belongs to. It is defined as the total number of protons present in the nucleus of an atom. Protons are tiny particles that carry a positive charge and they live inside the center of the atom. Because every element has a unique number of protons, the atomic number is like a fingerprint for that element. All atoms of an element have the same atomic number, Z. In fact, elements are defined by the number of protons they possess [S1].

For example, hydrogen is the simplest element. In a hydrogen atom, there is only one proton in the nucleus. Therefore, for hydrogen, Z = 1. Similarly, if you look at carbon, it has six protons in its nucleus. Therefore, for carbon, Z = 6 [S1]. Because the number of protons is always the same for a given element, the atomic number never changes. If you find an atom with six protons, you know it must be carbon, no matter what else is happening around it.

## What is the Mass Number?

While the atomic number tells us what element we have, the mass number tells us how heavy that atom is. The mass of an atom is practically due to protons and neutrons alone. These are present in the nucleus of an atom. Hence protons and neutrons are also called nucleons [S2]. Because electrons are so incredibly tiny, they do not add much weight. The mass of an atom resides in its nucleus [S4].

The mass number is defined as the sum of the total number of protons and neutrons present in the nucleus of an atom. It is denoted by ‘A’ [S2]. To find the mass number, you simply count the protons and add the neutrons. For example, let us look at carbon. Carbon has 6 protons in its nucleus. It also has 6 neutrons. To find the mass number, you add these two numbers together: $6 + 6 = 12$. Therefore, the mass number of carbon is 12 [S2].

Another example is aluminium. Aluminium has 13 protons and 14 neutrons. If you add these together, $13 + 14 = 27$. So, the mass number of aluminium is 27 [S2]. 

## How to Write Atomic Notation

Scientists use a special shorthand to write down an atom's atomic number and mass number quickly. In the notation for an atom, the atomic number, mass number and symbol of the element are to be written as [S2].

The atomic number is written as a small subscript on the left side, and the mass number is written as a larger subscript on the left side of the element's symbol. For example, nitrogen is written as $^{14}_{7}N$. Here, the 14 is the mass number, and the 7 is the atomic number. This tells us that a nitrogen atom has 7 protons and a total of 14 protons and neutrons [S2].

## Understanding Isotopes

Sometimes, atoms of the same element can have different mass numbers. This happens when the number of neutrons changes. These different versions of the same element are called isotopes. For example, all carbon atoms have 6 protons. However, some carbon atoms might have 6 neutrons, while others might have 8 neutrons. Both of these would still be carbon because they have the same atomic number, but they would have different mass numbers, 12 and 14, respectively.

## What are Isobars?

Isobars are atoms that have the same mass number but different atomic numbers. This means they have different numbers of protons and different numbers of neutrons, but when you add the protons and neutrons together, the total is exactly the same. For instance, two different elements might both have a mass number of 20. One atom might have 10 protons and 10 neutrons, while the other might have 11 protons and 9 neutrons. Both would have a mass number of 20, making them isobars, but they would behave completely differently in chemical reactions because of their different atomic numbers [S1, S2].

## Worked Example

Let us apply what we have learned. Imagine you are given an atom of an element. You count the particles in its nucleus and find that it has 8 protons and 8 neutrons. First, you find the atomic number, Z. Since the atomic number is the total number of protons present in the nucleus of an atom, the atomic number is 8. Next, you find the mass number, A. The mass number is the sum of the total number of protons and neutrons present in the nucleus of an atom. So, you add $8 + 8 = 16$. The mass number is 16. Finally, you look up the element with atomic number 8 in the periodic table. You find that the symbol for oxygen is O. Therefore, you can write this atom as $^{16}_{8}O$ [S1, S2].

## Key points

- The atomic number, Z, is the total number of protons present in the nucleus of an atom [S1].
- The atomic number determines the element, and all atoms of an element have the same atomic number [S1].
- The mass number, A, is the sum of the total number of protons and neutrons present in the nucleus of an atom [S2].
- Protons and neutrons are also called nucleons, and they are the only particles that contribute significantly to the mass of an atom [S2, S4].
- Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have different numbers of neutrons [S1, S2].
- Isobars are atoms that have the same mass number but different atomic numbers [S1, S2].
