# Writing Chemical Formulae

The chemical formula of a compound is a symbolic representation of its composition [S1]. To write these formulae, we must understand the symbols and combining capacity of the elements. The combining power of an element is known as its valency [S1]. The valency of an atom can be thought of as the hands or arms of that atom. For example, a human has two arms, while an octopus has eight [S1]. If one octopus has to catch hold of a few people in such a manner that all the eight arms of the octopus and both arms of all the humans are locked, how many humans do you think the octopus can hold? Represent the octopus with O and humans with H. The formula for this combination is $OH_{4}$ [S1]. The subscript 4 indicates the number of humans held by the octopus.

## Understanding Valency

The valency of an atom tells us how the atoms of an element will combine with the atom(s) of another element to form a chemical compound [S1]. We can look at the valencies of common ions to help us write formulae. For instance, sodium has a valency of 1 and is written as $Na^{+}$, while magnesium has a valency of 2 and is written as $Mg^{2+}$ [S1]. Some elements show more than one valency. A Roman numeral shows their valency in a bracket, such as copper (II) or iron (III) [S1]. When we write the formula of a compound, the valencies or charges on the ions must balance [S1].

## The Criss-Cross Method

When writing the chemical formulae for compounds, we write the constituent elements and their valencies. Then we must criss-cross the valencies of the combining atoms [S2]. This method ensures that the positive and negative charges balance each other. The overall structure must be neutral [S2]. Note that in the formula, the charges on the ions are not indicated [S2].

For example, to find the formula of magnesium chloride, we write the symbol of the cation $(\mathrm{Mg}^{2+})$ first followed by the symbol of the anion (Cl $^{-}$ ) [S2]. Then their charges are criss-crossed to get the formula, which is $\mathrm{MgCl}_{2}$ [S2]. Thus, in magnesium chloride, there are two chloride ions (Cl $^{-}$ ) for each magnesium ion (Mg $^{2+}$ ).

## Rules for Writing Formulae

There are specific rules that you must follow while writing a chemical formula [S1]. First, the valencies or charges on the ion must balance. Second, when a compound consists of a metal and a non-metal, the name or symbol of the metal is written first. For example, calcium oxide is written as CaO, sodium chloride as NaCl, iron sulphide as FeS, and copper oxide as CuO [S1]. In these examples, oxygen, chlorine, and sulphur are non-metals and are written on the right, whereas calcium, sodium, iron, and copper are metals and are written on the left [S1].

Third, in compounds formed with polyatomic ions, the number of ions present in the compound is indicated by enclosing the formula of the ion in a bracket and writing the number of ions outside the bracket [S1]. For example, magnesium hydroxide is written as Mg(OH)$_{2}$. In case the number of polyatomic ion is one, the bracket is not required. For example, sodium hydroxide is written as NaOH [S1].

## Molecular and Empirical Formulae

The simplest compounds, which are made up of two different elements, are called binary compounds [S2]. We can use the valencies of some ions to write formulae for compounds [S2]. For instance, the formula of hydrogen chloride is HCl, the formula of hydrogen sulphide is $\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{S}$, and the formula of carbon tetrachloride is $CCl_{4}$ [S2].

For aluminium oxide, the formula is $Al_{2}O_{3}$ [S2]. For calcium oxide, the valencies of the two elements are the same. You may arrive at the formula $Ca_{2}O_{2}$, but we simplify the formula as CaO [S2].

## Using Polyatomic Ions

When using polyatomic ions, we must be careful with brackets. For example, the formula for sodium nitrate is $\mathrm{NaNO}_{3}$ [S2]. The formula of calcium hydroxide is $\mathrm{Ca(OH)_2}$ and not $CaOH_2$ [S2]. We use brackets when we have two or more of the same ions in the formula. Here, the bracket around OH with a subscript 2 indicates that there are two hydroxyl (OH) groups joined to one calcium atom. In other words, there are two atoms each of oxygen and hydrogen in calcium hydroxide [S2].

In the above example of sodium carbonate, brackets are not needed if there is only one ion present [S2]. The formula is $\mathrm{Na}_{2}\mathrm{CO}_{3}$. For ammonium sulphate, the formula is $(\mathrm{NH}_{4})_{2}\mathrm{SO}_{4}$ [S2].

## Worked Example

Let us apply the criss-cross method to find the formula of aluminium sulphate. First, we identify the valencies of the ions involved. Aluminium has a valency of 3 and is written as $Al^{3+}$, while sulphate is a polyatomic ion with a valency of 2 and is written as $SO_{4}^{2-}$ [S1, S2].

Next, we write the symbols and their charges. We place the cation first, followed by the anion:

Symbol/ Formula: Al, $SO_{4}$
Charge: $3+$ , $2-$

Now, we criss-cross the valencies to get the formula. The 3 goes down to become the subscript of $SO_{4}$, and the 2 goes down to become the subscript of Al. This gives us $Al_{2}(SO_{4})_{3}$.

We use brackets because there are two or more of the same polyatomic ion [S1]. Here, the bracket around $SO_{4}$ with a subscript 3 indicates that there are three sulphate groups joined to two aluminium atoms.

## Key points

* The chemical formula of a compound is a symbolic representation of its composition [S1].
* The combining power of an element is known as its valency [S1].
* The valencies or charges on the ion must balance [S1].
* When a compound consists of a metal and a non-metal, the name or symbol of the metal is written first [S1].
* In compounds formed with polyatomic ions, the number of ions present in the compound is indicated by enclosing the formula of the ion in a bracket and writing the number of ions outside the bracket [S1].
* The criss-cross method is used to write the chemical formulae for compounds [S2].
