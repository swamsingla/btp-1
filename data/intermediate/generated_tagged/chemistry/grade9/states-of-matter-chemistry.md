# States of Matter

Matter is all around us. It is anything that occupies space and has mass. The way matter behaves depends on the arrangement and movement of its tiny particles. By understanding how these particles are packed and how they move, we can explain why a rock is hard, water flows, and air fills a room.

## The Solid State

In a solid, the particles are packed very tightly together in a fixed pattern. Because they are so close, solids have a definite shape, distinct boundaries, and a fixed volume. Solids are generally rigid, meaning they maintain their shape when subjected to outside force. If you drop a solid, it will keep its shape because the particles are locked in place.

However, not all solids behave in exactly the same way. For example, a rubber band changes its shape when you stretch it. It regains its original shape when the force is removed, but if you apply excessive force, it will break. Similarly, the shape of an individual sugar or salt crystal remains fixed whether you hold it in your hand, place it on a plate, or put it in a jar. A sponge is also a solid, but it has minute holes where air is trapped. When you press a sponge, you compress it by expelling the trapped air.

## The Liquid State

In a liquid, the particles are still close together, but they are not locked in a fixed pattern. They can slide past one another. Because of this, liquids have no fixed shape but they do have a fixed volume. A liquid takes up the shape of whatever container it is kept in. When you pour a liquid from one container into another, it flows easily.

Liquids are not rigid, which is why we call them fluids. The particles in a liquid move freely and have greater space between each other compared to particles in a solid. Because of this increased movement and space, the rate of diffusion of liquids is higher than that of solids. For instance, gases from the atmosphere diffuse and dissolve in water. These dissolved gases, especially oxygen and carbon dioxide, are essential for the survival of aquatic animals and plants.

## The Gaseous State

In a gas, the particles are very far apart. They move about randomly at high speed. Because they are so far apart, gases do not have a fixed shape or a fixed volume. They spread out to fill any container they are placed in.

Gases are highly compressible compared to solids and liquids. If you try to push a piston into a syringe filled with a gas, the piston moves in easily. Due to this high compressibility, large volumes of a gas can be compressed into a small cylinder. For example, the liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) cylinder used in homes or the oxygen supplied to hospitals in cylinders contains compressed gas. Compressed natural gas (CNG) is also used as fuel in vehicles.

The random movement of gas particles also explains why gases exert pressure. The particles hit each other and the walls of the container. The force exerted by these gas particles per unit area on the walls of the container is what we call gas pressure.

Because gas particles move very fast and are far apart, gases diffuse very rapidly into other gases. You can smell hot cooked food in seconds because the aroma particles mix with the particles of air, spread from the kitchen, and reach your nostrils.

## Worked Example

Let us apply the rule that gases are highly compressible compared to solids and liquids [S5]. Imagine you have three 100 mL syringes. You remove the pistons and fill one syringe with water, another with pieces of chalk, and leave the third syringe empty. You then insert the pistons back in. When you push the piston in, you will observe that the piston is easily pushed in for the empty syringe, but it is very difficult to push in the syringes containing water and chalk. This shows that gases are highly compressible as compared to solids and liquids [S5].

## Key points

- Matter exists in three main states: solid, liquid, and gas.
- Solids have a definite shape, distinct boundaries, and a fixed volume, and they are rigid.
- Liquids have no fixed shape but have a fixed volume, and they flow easily.
- Gases have no fixed shape or volume, and they are highly compressible.
- In solids, particles are tightly packed. In liquids, particles slide past each other. In gases, particles move randomly at high speed.
- The rate of diffusion is highest in gases, followed by liquids, and lowest in solids.
