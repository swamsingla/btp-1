# Particle Nature of Matter

Have you ever wondered what a piece of chalk is really made of, or why an iron nail keeps its shape while water takes the shape of any container it is poured into? The answers lie in the tiny particles that make up matter. Understanding the particle nature of matter helps us explain why solids, liquids and gases behave so differently.

## Breaking Down Matter

Take a stick of chalk and break it into two pieces. Keep breaking it until it becomes difficult to break further by hand, then grind the pieces into a fine powder. If you look at the powder under a magnifying glass, each tiny grain is still a speck of chalk [S1]. Grinding chalk is a physical change: the chalk does not change into a new substance, and only the size of each speck becomes smaller [S1].

Imagine the grinding continuing until the chalk particles cannot be broken down any further. The tiny units obtained at this stage are the basic building blocks that the chalk was made up of [S1]. So one whole piece of chalk is made up of a large number of smaller units, which are called constituent particles of chalk. A constituent particle is the basic unit that makes up a larger piece of a substance or material. Just like chalk, the grains of sand and clay are not the smallest units of bigger rocks. They are also made up of a large number of their constituent particles [S2].

## Forces Between Particles

The constituent particles of matter are held together through forces which are attractive in nature. These forces are called interparticle attractions. The strength of these attractions depends on the nature of the substance and the interparticle distance. Even a slight increase in the distance decreases the interparticle forces drastically. The strength of these forces ultimately decides the physical state of the substances [S3].

## Solids

Solid objects such as an iron nail, a stone or a key have a definite shape and volume. This is because in solids the particles are tightly packed and the interparticle attractions are very strong. These strong forces hold the particles in fixed positions, preventing them from moving freely. The particles can only move to and fro about their positions (vibrate or oscillate), but they cannot move past each other [S4].

When a solid is heated, its particles vibrate more vigorously. A stage is reached when the vibrations become so vigorous that the particles start leaving their positions. The interparticle forces of attraction get weakened and the solid is converted into the liquid state [S4]. The minimum temperature at which a solid melts to become a liquid at atmospheric pressure is called its melting point. Some solids have weak interparticle attractions, so their melting points are low, while others have strong attractive forces and high melting points. For example, ice melts at 0 °C, urea at 133 °C and iron at 1538 °C [S5].

## Liquids

Liquids do not have a fixed shape; they take the shape of the container they are kept in, because the particles of liquids are free to move. But the particles move freely only within a limited space, so liquids have a fixed volume. If you pour 200 mL of water from one container into others of different shapes, it takes the shape of each container, and its level stays at 200 mL [S6].

You can move your finger through water without breaking or cutting it permanently, which cannot be done with a solid. You only displace the water temporarily, and as soon as you remove your finger, the water goes back into its place. This shows that in liquids the interparticle attractions are slightly weaker than in solids, but still strong enough to keep the particles close together [S7]. Generally, in a liquid the particles are somewhat farther from each other than in the solid state. Ice is an exception, as its particles are farther apart than those in water [S5].

## Gases

When a liquid is heated, a stage comes when it starts boiling. The temperature at which a liquid boils and turns into vapour at atmospheric pressure is called its boiling point. The movement of the particles becomes so vigorous that they move apart from each other, and the interparticle forces of attraction decrease. Eventually the constituent particles can escape from the liquid state, and the liquid is converted into vapour, the gaseous state [S7].

At the boiling point, the formation of vapour is very fast and happens not only at the surface but also within the liquid, which we see as bubbles. However, vapour formation occurs at all temperatures, even below the boiling point, though slowly and only at the surface. This slower process is known as evaporation [S7].

## Space Between Particles

The space left between the particles of a substance is called the interparticle spacing [S9]. In solids the particles are held together by strong forces of attraction, so they do not move from one place to another and are closely packed. Even so, some space is left between the particles. You might think this space is filled with air, but it is not. It contains nothing at all [S12].

Gas particles have a lot of space between them in their natural state, and this space can be reduced by applying external pressure. If you compress the air in a syringe by pushing the plunger, the particles are forced to come closer. If you stop pushing, the gas particles spread and the plunger moves back to its original position. Water, however, is practically incompressible [S10].

## Worked example

Mark the level of water in a glass vessel as A. Add two teaspoons of sugar and mark the new level as B: the level of the water has risen. Now stir to dissolve the sugar and mark the level again as C. The level may fall to some extent [S11]. Since the volume of the solution is less than the sum of the volumes of water and sugar, there must be some space between the water particles, and the particles of the dissolved sugar occupy these spaces [S11].

Now repeat the activity with sand. Sand does not dissolve in water. The sand particles settle down and occupy some space in the container, so the total volume increases [S11].

## Key points

- Matter is made of tiny constituent particles held together by attractive interparticle forces, and the strength of these forces decides the physical state [S2, S3].
- In solids the particles are tightly packed in fixed positions and only vibrate, so solids have a definite shape and volume; in liquids the particles move freely within a limited space, so liquids have a fixed volume but no fixed shape [S4, S6].
- The melting point is the minimum temperature at which a solid melts at atmospheric pressure, and the boiling point is the temperature at which a liquid boils and turns into vapour at atmospheric pressure [S5, S7].
- Evaporation is the slow formation of vapour at the surface at any temperature below the boiling point, while boiling is fast and also forms bubbles within the liquid [S7].
- There is space between particles in every state: gases can be compressed because their particles have a lot of space between them, and dissolved particles occupy the spaces between water particles [S10, S11].
