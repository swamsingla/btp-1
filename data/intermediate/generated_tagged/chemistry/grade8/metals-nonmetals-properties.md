# Properties of Metals and Non-metals

Every day, you use objects made from metals and non-metals. A copper wire carries electricity, and an iron plough breaks the soil. These everyday tools work because the materials they are made from have specific physical and chemical properties. Understanding these properties helps us choose the right material for a specific job, from wrapping food to building bridges. 

## Metallic Lustre and Hardness

When you look at a piece of iron or aluminium, it shines. This shine is called metallic lustre. Most metals, like copper and aluminium, are hard and solid at room temperature [S1]. However, not every metal is hard. Some metals, like sodium and potassium, are very soft. You can actually cut them with a knife [S1]. There is also one special metal, mercury, that is a liquid at room temperature [S1]. On the other hand, many non-metals, like coal and sulfur, do not shine and are not as hard as metals [S1].

## Malleability

Look at a sweet from a sweet shop. Some sweets are covered with a thin, shiny silver layer. This layer is called silver foil. The property that allows metals to be beaten into thin sheets is called malleability [S1]. If you take a piece of copper, an iron nail, or aluminium and hit it with a hammer, it flattens out into a sheet [S1]. Because of this property, we can make thin silver foil for sweets and aluminium foil for wrapping food [S1]. Gold and silver are the most malleable metals. If you take a piece of coal or a lump of sulfur and hit it with a hammer, it does not flatten. Instead, it breaks into pieces. Materials that break easily when hit are called brittle [S1]. Wood is different. When you hit wood, it does not flatten, and it does not break into pieces easily. Therefore, wood is neither malleable nor brittle [S1].

## Ductility

Look at the electrical wires in your house or the strings on a sitar. These are made of metal wires. The property that allows a material to be drawn into thin wires is called ductility [S2]. This property is mainly found in metals. Gold is so ductile that one gram of it can be drawn into a wire that is 2 kilometres long [S2]. Because metals are ductile, we can make useful items like tea strainers using metal wires [S2]. We also use ropes made of steel wires in suspension bridges and cranes to lift heavy loads [S2]. Steel is a mixture of a metal (iron) and a non-metal (carbon) [S2]. You will never see a rope made of coal or sulfur, because coal and sulfur are not ductile [S2].

## Electrical and Thermal Conductivity

Metals are excellent conductors of electricity and heat. This means they allow electricity and heat to pass through them easily. For example, copper and aluminium are used to make electrical wires because they carry electricity well [S1, S2]. Non-metals, on the other hand, are generally poor conductors. This is why we use non-metal materials like wood or plastic to make the handles of cooking utensils, so the heat from the food does not burn our hands [S1, S2].

## Reactivity with Air and Water

When metals are exposed to air and moisture, they often react to form a layer of rust or tarnish. For instance, iron reacts with oxygen and water to form iron oxide, which we call rust [S1]. This is why old iron objects, like the ploughs used by farmers, can weaken over time. Non-metals behave differently. Sulfur, for example, does not rust or tarnish in the same way as iron [S1]. 

## Worked Example

A student is trying to decide which material to use for a specific task. They need to choose a material that can be drawn into a long, thin wire to support a heavy load in a bridge. Ductility is a property of metals, so metals can be drawn into wires [S2]. Therefore, the student should choose a metal like steel, which is a mixture of iron and carbon [S2]. This is exactly how suspension bridges and cranes are built to lift heavy objects [S2].

## Key points
*   Metals generally have a shiny appearance called metallic lustre and are hard, though some like sodium and potassium are very soft [S1].
*   Mercury is a metal that is liquid at room temperature [S1].
*   Malleability is the property of being beaten into thin sheets, which most metals possess [S1].
*   Non-metals like coal and sulfur are brittle and break when hit with a hammer [S1].
*   Ductility is the property of being drawn into wires, a property mainly found in metals [S2].
*   Steel is a mixture of the metal iron and the non-metal carbon, and it is used in suspension bridges and cranes [S2].
*   Metals are good conductors of electricity and heat, while non-metals are generally poor conductors [S1, S2].
*   Iron reacts with air and water to form rust [S1].
