# Extraction of Metals

Metals are the backbone of modern infrastructure, from the steel beams of skyscrapers to the copper wiring in our homes. However, they do not naturally exist in their pure, shiny metallic form underground. Instead, they are locked inside rocks as ores, mixed with unwanted materials called gangue. Extracting a pure metal from its ore is a massive industrial process. It requires careful chemical manipulation to separate the valuable metal from the waste, ensuring we have the raw materials needed for everything from smartphones to railways.

## Converting Ores to Oxides

Before a metal can be extracted, it must be converted into a form that is easier to process. The ores of metals in the middle of the activity series, such as iron, zinc, and lead, are often found as sulphides or carbonates. It is much easier to obtain a metal from its oxide compared to its sulphides or carbonates. Therefore, these ores must be converted into metal oxides before they can be reduced.

Sulphide ores are converted into oxides by heating them strongly in the presence of excess air. This specific process is known as roasting [S2]. For example, when zinc sulphide is roasted, it reacts with oxygen to form zinc oxide and sulphur dioxide gas. The chemical equation for this is $2 \mathrm{ZnS} (\mathrm{s}) + 3 \mathrm{O} _ {2} (\mathrm{g}) \xrightarrow {\text{Heat}} 2 \mathrm{ZnO} (\mathrm{s}) + 2 \mathrm{SO} _ {2} (\mathrm{g})$ [S2].

Similarly, carbonate ores are changed into oxides by heating them strongly in limited air. This process is specifically called calcination [S2]. A classic example is the calcination of zinc carbonate, which breaks down into zinc oxide and carbon dioxide gas, represented by the equation $\mathrm{ZnCO} _ {3} (\mathrm{s}) \xrightarrow {\text{Heat}} \mathrm{ZnO} (\mathrm{s}) + \mathrm{CO} _ {2} (\mathrm{g})$ [S2]. 

## Reducing Metal Oxides

Once the ores are converted into oxides, the metal must be separated from the oxygen. This is achieved through a reduction process. For metals of medium reactivity, such as zinc and iron, carbon is used as the reducing agent [S2]. When zinc oxide is heated with carbon, it is reduced to metallic zinc. The chemical equation for this reaction is $\mathrm{ZnO(s)} + \mathrm{C(s)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Zn(s)} + \mathrm{CO(g)}$ [S2].

Sometimes, displacement reactions are used instead of carbon. Highly reactive metals like aluminium or sodium can displace metals of lower reactivity from their compounds [S2]. For instance, when manganese dioxide is heated with aluminium powder, the aluminium acts as the reducing agent. The reaction is $3 \mathrm{MnO} _ {2} (\mathrm{s}) + 4 \mathrm{Al} (\mathrm{s}) \rightarrow 3 \mathrm{Mn} (\mathrm{l}) + 2 \mathrm{Al} _ {2} \mathrm{O} _ {3} (\mathrm{s}) + \text{Heat}$ [S2]. These reactions are highly exothermic, meaning they release a massive amount of heat. This heat is so intense that the metals are produced in a molten state, or liquid state.

A famous application of this highly exothermic displacement reaction is the thermit process [S2]. In this process, iron(III) oxide ($Fe_{2}O_{3}$) is reacted with aluminium. The intense heat generated by this reaction is hot enough to melt iron. This molten iron is then used to join railway tracks or to weld cracked machine parts together [S2].

## Electrolytic Reduction

Metals at the top of the reactivity series, such as sodium, magnesium, calcium, and aluminium, are extremely reactive [S1]. They cannot be obtained from their compounds by simply heating them with carbon [S3]. This is because these highly reactive metals have a stronger affinity for oxygen than carbon does. Therefore, carbon cannot reduce their oxides to the pure metal.

To extract these highly reactive metals, we must use a much stronger reducing method: electrolytic reduction [S3]. This process involves passing a direct electric current through the molten compound of the metal. For example, sodium, magnesium, and calcium are obtained by the electrolysis of their molten chlorides [S3]. During this process, the metal ions move towards the negatively charged electrode, known as the cathode, and gain electrons to form the pure metal. Simultaneously, non-metal ions move towards the positively charged electrode, the anode, and lose electrons.

Aluminium is another critical metal extracted using this method [S3]. It is obtained by the electrolytic reduction of aluminium oxide [S3]. This requires specialized industrial setups to maintain the extremely high temperatures needed to keep the aluminium oxide molten.

## Electrolytic Refining

The metals produced by reduction processes are rarely pure enough for industrial use. They typically contain impurities that must be removed. The most widely used method for refining these impure metals is electrolytic refining [S4].

In this refining process, the impure metal is made the anode, which is the positive electrode, in an electrolytic cell [S4]. A thin strip of pure metal is used as the cathode, the negative electrode [S4]. A solution of the metal salt is used as the electrolyte [S4]. When an electric current is passed through the cell, the pure metal at the anode dissolves into the electrolyte solution [S4]. Simultaneously, an equivalent amount of pure metal from the electrolyte is deposited onto the cathode [S4].

This process effectively transfers the pure metal from the impure anode to the pure cathode. Meanwhile, the soluble impurities from the anode dissolve into the electrolyte solution [S4]. The insoluble impurities, however, do not dissolve. Instead, they fall to the bottom of the cell and are known as anode mud [S4]. This method is highly effective and is widely used to refine metals such as copper, zinc, tin, nickel, silver, and gold [S4].

## Worked example

Consider the extraction of zinc from its ore. Zinc is a moderately reactive metal, so it is found in nature mainly as zinc carbonate or zinc sulphide [S1, S2]. If the ore is zinc carbonate, it must first undergo calcination. By heating the zinc carbonate strongly in limited air, it is converted into zinc oxide. The reaction is $\mathrm{ZnCO} _ {3} (\mathrm{s}) \xrightarrow {\text{Heat}} \mathrm{ZnO} (\mathrm{s}) + \mathrm{CO} _ {2} (\mathrm{g})$ [S2]. 

Next, the zinc oxide must be reduced to obtain pure zinc. Since zinc is moderately reactive, carbon is the appropriate reducing agent [S2]. When zinc oxide is heated with carbon, it is reduced to metallic zinc. The chemical equation for this reduction is $\mathrm{ZnO(s)} + \mathrm{C(s)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Zn(s)} + \mathrm{CO(g)}$ [S2]. The resulting zinc is collected, but if it is intended for high-conductivity electrical wiring, it might later undergo electrolytic refining to remove any remaining impurities [S2, S4].

## Key points

*   Metals are grouped by reactivity into three categories: low, medium, and high, which dictates their extraction methods [S1].
*   Sulphide ores are converted to oxides by roasting in excess air, while carbonate ores are converted by calcination in limited air [S2].
*   Medium reactivity metals are reduced from their oxides using carbon or through highly exothermic displacement reactions [S2].
*   Highly reactive metals at the top of the activity series cannot be reduced by carbon and must be extracted using electrolytic reduction [S1, S3].
*   Electrolytic refining uses an impure metal anode and a pure metal cathode to transfer pure metal from the anode to the cathode, leaving impurities behind as anode mud [S4].
