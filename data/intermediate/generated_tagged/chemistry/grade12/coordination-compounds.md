# Coordination Compounds

Coordination compounds are fascinating chemical species where a central metal atom or ion is bound to a specific number of ions or molecules. These compounds are vital in everyday life, from the hemoglobin in your blood that carries oxygen to the vibrant dyes used in the textile industry. Understanding their structure and behaviour is crucial for advanced chemistry, particularly in the study of transition metals.

## Werner's Theory

In the late 19th century, Alfred Werner, a Swiss chemist, revolutionized our understanding of these compounds. He proposed that metal ions exhibit two distinct types of linkages or valences: primary and secondary [S1]. The primary valences are ionisable and are satisfied by negative ions, much like the charges in simple ionic compounds. In contrast, the secondary valences are non-ionisable. These are satisfied by neutral molecules or negative ions that bind directly to the metal [S1]. 

Werner's theory was supported by experiments with cobalt(III) chloride and ammonia. When he added silver nitrate to these compounds, he observed that some chloride ions precipitated as silver chloride while others remained in the solution [S1]. This led him to conclude that six groups remain bonded to the cobalt ion during the reaction. He termed the number of groups bound directly to the metal ion as the secondary valence, which is equal to the coordination number and is fixed for a metal [S1].

## Ligands

The ions or molecules bound to the central atom or ion in a coordination entity are called ligands [S1]. These can range from simple ions like chloride ($\text{Cl}^-$) to larger molecules like proteins. When a ligand is bound to a metal ion through a single donor atom, as with $\text{Cl}^-$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, or $\text{NH}_3$, it is said to be unidentate [S1]. 

If a ligand can bind through two donor atoms, as in $\text{H}_2\text{NCH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{NH}_2$ (ethane-1,2-diamine) or $\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-}$ (oxalate), it is called didentate. When several donor atoms are present in a single ligand, such as in $\text{N}(\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{NH}_2)_3$, it is termed polydentate. The ethylenediaminetetraacetate ion ($\text{EDTA}^{4-}$) is an important hexadentate ligand that can bind through two nitrogen and four oxygen atoms to a central metal ion [S1]. 

When a di- or polydentate ligand uses its two or more donor atoms simultaneously to bind a single metal ion, it forms a chelate ligand. Such complexes, called chelate complexes, tend to be more stable than similar complexes containing unidentate ligands [S1]. Ligands that have two different donor atoms and can coordinate either through one or the other, such as $\text{NO}_2^-$ and $\text{SCN}^-$, are called ambidentate ligands [S1].

## Coordination Number and Polyhedra

The coordination number (CN) of a metal ion in a complex is defined as the number of ligand donor atoms to which the metal is directly bonded [S1]. For example, in the complex ions $[Pt\text{Cl}_6]^{2-}$ and $[Ni(\text{NH}_3)_4]^{2+}$, the coordination numbers of Pt and Ni are 6 and 4, respectively [S1]. 

The spatial arrangement of the ligand atoms which are directly attached to the central atom or ion defines a coordination polyhedron about the central atom [S1]. The most common coordination polyhedra in coordination compounds of transition metals are octahedral, square planar, and tetrahedral [S1]. For instance, $[Co(\text{NH}_3)_6]^{3+}$ is an octahedral entity, while $[Ni(\text{CO})_4]$ is tetrahedral and $[Pt\text{Cl}_4]^{2-}$ is square planar [S1].

## Isomerism

Isomerism in coordination compounds is a crucial concept where compounds with the same empirical formula exhibit distinct properties. This was notably observed in cobalt(III) chloride-ammonia complexes. For example, two compounds with the formula $\text{CoCl}_3 \cdot 4\text{NH}_3$ have distinct properties: one is green and the other is violet. These compounds are termed isomers [S1]. 

Werner's theory successfully explained such isomerism by proposing different spatial arrangements of the ligands around the central metal ion. He showed that the ions or groups bound by secondary linkages to the metal have characteristic spatial arrangements corresponding to different coordination numbers [S1].

## Crystal Field Theory

The Crystal Field Theory is a model used to explain the properties of coordination compounds, particularly the colours and magnetic behaviour of transition metal complexes. This theory is based on the idea that the d-orbitals of the central metal ion are affected by the electrostatic field of the ligands. The theory explains why certain complexes are coloured and why some are paramagnetic while others are diamagnetic.

The theory states that when a central metal ion is surrounded by ligands, the five degenerate d-orbitals split into two sets of different energy levels. The extent of this splitting, known as the crystal field splitting energy, depends on the nature of the metal ion and the ligands. This splitting leads to the absorption of specific wavelengths of light, resulting in the observed colours of the complexes [S1].

## Worked Example

Consider the coordination compound $\text{K}_3[\text{Cr}(\text{C}_2\text{O}_4)_3]$. To determine the oxidation number of chromium, we first note that oxalate ($\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-}$) is a didentate ligand. Since there are three oxalate ligands, the total charge from the ligands is $3 \times (-2) = -6$. The overall charge of the complex ion is $-3$. Therefore, the oxidation number of chromium must be +3 to balance the charges, as $x + (-6) = -3$, which gives $x = +3$. The name of this compound is potassium trioxalatochromate(III) [S1].

## Key points

*   Coordination compounds consist of a central metal atom or ion bonded to a fixed number of ions or molecules.
*   Alfred Werner proposed the concepts of primary and secondary valences to explain the structure of these compounds.
*   Ligands are the ions or molecules bound to the central metal atom or ion.
*   The coordination number is the number of ligand donor atoms directly bonded to the metal.
*   The spatial arrangement of ligands around the central metal ion is called a coordination polyhedron.
*   Isomerism occurs when compounds with the same empirical formula have different properties.
*   Crystal Field Theory explains the colours and magnetic properties of transition metal complexes.
