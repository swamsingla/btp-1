# Batteries and Fuel Cells

Every time you switch on a torch, start a car, or watch a spacecraft land safely on the moon, you are witnessing the direct conversion of chemical energy into electrical energy. In your earlier studies of electrochemistry, you learned how galvanic cells use spontaneous redox reactions to generate a flow of electrons. However, a standard galvanic cell is designed for a single experiment and is not practical for everyday portable devices or large-scale power generation. This is where batteries and fuel cells come in, acting as practical, engineered versions of galvanic cells. A battery is essentially a galvanic cell that has been packaged to be reasonably light, compact, and capable of delivering a steady voltage over its operational life [S1].

## Primary Batteries

Primary batteries are designed for single use. In these cells, the chemical reaction occurs only once, and after the battery is used over a period of time, it becomes dead and cannot be reused again [S2]. Because of this one-time use, primary cells are ideal for low-drain, disposable devices like remote controls, wall clocks, and basic transistor radios. 

The most familiar example of a primary battery is the dry cell, historically known as the Leclanche cell after its discoverer. A commercial dry cell consists of a graphite (carbon) cathode surrounded by a mixture of powdered manganese dioxide and carbon. The cathode is housed inside a zinc container that acts as the anode [S2]. The space between the electrodes is filled with a moist paste of ammonium chloride $(\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl})$ and zinc chloride $(\mathrm{ZnCl}_2)$. The electrode reactions in a dry cell are complex, but they can be approximated as follows:

Anode:
$$
\mathrm{Zn(s)} \longrightarrow \mathrm{Zn} ^ {2 +} + 2 \mathrm{e} ^ {-}
$$

Cathode:
$$
\mathrm{MnO} _ {2} + \mathrm{NH} _ {4} ^ {+} + \mathrm{e} ^ {-} \longrightarrow \mathrm{MnO(OH)} + \mathrm{NH} _ {3}
$$

In the reaction at the cathode, manganese is reduced from the +4 oxidation state to the +3 state. The ammonia produced during this reaction forms a complex with $\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}$ to give the tetraamminezinc ion, $[\mathrm{Zn}\left(\mathrm{NH}_{3}\right)_{4}]^{2+}$. The cell has a potential of nearly 1.5 V.

Another common primary cell is the mercury cell, which is suitable for low current devices like hearing aids and watches. It consists of a zinc-mercury amalgam as the anode and a paste of mercury(II) oxide (HgO) and carbon as the cathode. The electrolyte is a paste of potassium hydroxide (KOH) and zinc oxide (ZnO). The electrode reactions are:

Anode:
$$
\mathrm{Zn(Hg)} + 2 \mathrm{OH} ^ {-} \longrightarrow \mathrm{ZnO(s)} + \mathrm{H} _ {2} \mathrm{O} + 2 \mathrm{e} ^ {-}
$$

Cathode:
$$
\mathrm{HgO + H_2O + 2e^- \longrightarrow Hg(l) + 2OH^-}
$$

The overall reaction is represented by:
$$
\mathrm{Zn(Hg)} + \mathrm{HgO(s)} \longrightarrow \mathrm{ZnO(s)} + \mathrm{Hg(l)}
$$

The cell potential is approximately 1.35 V and remains constant during its life. This stability occurs because the overall reaction does not involve any ion in solution whose concentration can change during its lifetime [S2].

## Secondary Batteries

In contrast to primary batteries, secondary batteries can be recharged. After use, a secondary cell can be recharged by passing current through it in the opposite direction, allowing it to be used again. A good secondary cell can undergo a large number of discharging and charging cycles. The most important secondary cell is the lead storage battery, commonly used in automobiles and inverters [S3]. It consists of a lead anode and a grid of lead packed with lead dioxide ($\mathrm{PbO}_{2}$) as the cathode. A 38% solution of sulphuric acid is used as the electrolyte.

The cell reactions when the battery is in use are:

Anode:
$$
\mathrm{Pb(s) + SO_4^{2-}(aq)\rightarrow PbSO_4(s) + 2e^-}
$$

Cathode:
$$
\mathrm{PbO_{2}(s) + SO_{4}^{2-}(aq) + 4H^{+}(aq) + 2e^{-} \rightarrow PbSO_{4}(s) + 2H_{2}O(l)}
$$

The overall cell reaction is:
$$
\mathrm{Pb(s)} + \mathrm{PbO} _ {2} (\mathrm{s}) + 2 \mathrm{H} _ {2} \mathrm{SO} _ {4} (\mathrm{aq}) \rightarrow 2 \mathrm{PbSO} _ {4} (\mathrm{s}) + 2 \mathrm{H} _ {2} \mathrm{O(l)}
$$

On charging the battery, the reaction is reversed, and the lead(II) sulphate ($\mathrm{PbSO}_{4}$) on the anode and cathode is converted back into lead and lead dioxide, respectively [S3].

Another important secondary cell is the nickel-cadmium cell. It has a longer life than the lead storage cell but is more expensive to manufacture. It is often arranged in a jelly roll configuration and separated by a layer soaked in moist sodium or potassium hydroxide. The overall reaction during discharge is:
$$
\mathrm{Cd(s)} + 2 \mathrm{Ni(OH)} _ {3} (\mathrm{s}) \rightarrow \mathrm{CdO(s)} + 2 \mathrm{Ni(OH)} _ {2} (\mathrm{s}) + \mathrm{H} _ {2} \mathrm{O}
$$

## Hydrogen Fuel Cell

The production of electricity by thermal plants is not a very efficient method and is a major source of pollution. In such plants, the chemical energy (heat of combustion) of fossil fuels is first used for converting water into high pressure steam, which is then used to run a turbine to produce electricity [S3]. We know that a galvanic cell directly converts chemical energy into electricity and is highly efficient. It is now possible to make such cells in which reactants are fed continuously to the electrodes and products are removed continuously from the electrolyte compartment.

Galvanic cells that are designed to convert the energy of combustion of fuels like hydrogen, methane, or methanol directly into electrical energy are called fuel cells [S3]. One of the most successful fuel cells uses the reaction of hydrogen with oxygen to form water. The cell was used for providing electrical power in the Apollo space programme. The water vapours produced during the reaction were condensed and added to the drinking water supply for the astronauts.

In the hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell, hydrogen and oxygen are bubbled through porous carbon electrodes into concentrated aqueous sodium hydroxide solution. Catalysts like finely divided platinum or palladium metal are incorporated into the electrodes to increase the rate of the electrode reactions. The electrode reactions are:

Cathode:
$$
\mathrm{O}_2(\mathrm{g}) + 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}(\mathrm{l}) + 4\mathrm{e}^-\longrightarrow 4\mathrm{OH}^- \text{ (aq)}
$$

Anode:
$$
2\mathrm{H}_{2}(\mathrm{g}) + 4\mathrm{OH}^{-}(\mathrm{aq}) \longrightarrow 4\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}(\mathrm{l}) + 4\mathrm{e}^{-}
$$

The overall reaction being:
$$
2 \mathrm{H} _ {2} (\mathrm{g}) + \mathrm{O} _ {2} (\mathrm{g}) \longrightarrow 2 \mathrm{H} _ {2} \mathrm{O} (\mathrm{l})
$$

The cell runs continuously as long as the reactants are supplied. Fuel cells produce electricity with an efficiency of about 70% compared to thermal plants, whose efficiency is about 40% [S3]. There has been tremendous progress in the development of new electrode materials, better catalysts, and electrolytes for increasing the efficiency of fuel cells. These have been used in automobiles on an experimental basis. Fuel cells are pollution free, and in view of their future importance, a variety of fuel cells have been fabricated and tried.

## Worked example

Let us apply the concept of secondary batteries to a practical scenario. Suppose you have a lead storage battery with a potential of 12.6 V. When you turn on your car's headlights, the battery begins to discharge. During discharge, the anode reaction converts solid lead into solid lead(II) sulphate, while the cathode reaction converts solid lead dioxide into solid lead(II) sulphate. The overall reaction consumes sulphuric acid and produces water. If you drive the car and the engine is running, the alternator supplies an electrical current to the battery in the opposite direction. This charging current forces the reverse reactions to occur, converting the lead(II) sulphate on both electrodes back into pure lead and lead dioxide, thereby restoring the battery's chemical energy for the next use.

## Key points

- A battery is a practical, engineered galvanic cell that converts the chemical energy of a redox reaction into electrical energy, designed to be reasonably light, compact, and deliver a steady voltage [S1].
- Primary batteries, such as the dry cell and mercury cell, are designed for single use. The reaction occurs only once, after which the battery becomes dead and cannot be reused [S2].
- Secondary batteries, like the lead storage battery and the nickel-cadmium cell, can be recharged. They undergo a large number of discharging and charging cycles by reversing the electrode reactions [S2, S3].
- Fuel cells are galvanic cells that convert the chemical energy of fuels like hydrogen directly into electrical energy with high efficiency (about 70%) [S3].
- In a hydrogen fuel cell, hydrogen and oxygen react to form water. The cell runs continuously as long as the reactants are supplied, and it produces no pollution [S3].
