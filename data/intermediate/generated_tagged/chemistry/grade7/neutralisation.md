# Neutralisation Reaction

A neutralisation reaction is a chemical process where an acid and a base combine to form a salt and water, while also releasing heat into the surroundings. This process is highly important in our daily lives because it helps us manage and counteract the harmful effects of too much acid or too much base in various environments. When you experience a sharp sting from an insect bite, or when a farmer notices their soil is too sour for crops, a neutralisation reaction is exactly what provides the relief. Understanding how acids and bases interact allows us to solve real-world problems, from treating skin irritations to restoring the health of lakes and agricultural land.

## The Basic Rule of Neutralisation

The fundamental rule of a neutralisation reaction is that when an acid and a base are mixed in the correct proportions, they cancel each other out, resulting in a solution that is neither acidic nor basic. This means the new mixture loses the characteristic properties of both the original acid and the base. The chemical equation that represents this process is Acid + Base → Salt + Water + Heat. The heat released during this reaction is a key indicator that a chemical change has successfully taken place, proving that the acid and base have reacted completely.

For example, if you start with 20 drops of pure lemon juice and slowly add drops of lime water to it while swirling the test tube, you will notice a distinct colour change. Initially, the solution turns red when a blue litmus solution is added to it. As you continue adding the lime water, the solution eventually changes from red to blue, showing that the acid has been completely neutralised [S2].

## Everyday Examples of Neutralisation

Neutralisation reactions are not just confined to chemistry laboratories; they happen all around us in everyday situations. When an ant bites, it injects a highly acidic liquid called formic acid into the skin. This acid causes the skin to turn red and produces a sharp stinging pain. To relieve this discomfort, people often apply moist baking soda to the affected area. Baking soda is a base, and it reacts with the formic acid to neutralise its harmful effects, thereby stopping the sting [S1].

Another common example involves agriculture. Sometimes, farmers use excessive chemical fertilisers to help their plants grow better. However, over time, this can make the soil too acidic, which prevents the plants from growing well. To fix this, farmers treat the acidic soil with lime, which is also a base. The lime reacts with the excess acid in the soil, neutralising it and restoring the soil to a healthy state where crops can thrive [S1].

## Neutralisation in Water Bodies

The principles of neutralisation also apply to protecting our natural water bodies. In some industrial areas, factory waste is released directly into nearby lakes and rivers. If this waste is highly acidic, it can cause the fish population to decline rapidly, eventually leading to the complete death of aquatic life. To save the fish and the ecosystem, basic substances are added to the factory waste to neutralise its acidic nature before the water is released into the lake [S1].

Similarly, if the soil in a garden becomes too basic, farmers and gardeners add organic matter such as manure and composted leaves. This organic matter releases natural acids that neutralise the basic nature of the soil, bringing the pH level back to a healthy balance for plants [S1].

## Worked Example

Let us look at a practical scenario to see how the rule Acid + Base → Salt + Water + Heat works in real life. Imagine you have a small test tube containing a solution of lemon juice. You add a drop of blue litmus solution to it, and the mixture immediately turns red, confirming that it is acidic. Now, you begin to add drops of lime water to the test tube. You swirl the mixture gently after each drop. As the amount of lime water increases, the red colour of the solution begins to fade. Eventually, the solution turns from red to blue, which proves that the acid has been fully neutralised by the base [S2].

To apply the chemical equation, we can say that the lemon juice (the acid) reacted with the lime water (the base) to produce a salt and water. Because the heat of the reaction was released into the surrounding air of the test tube, the overall temperature of the mixture slightly increased. This heat release is a clear sign that a neutralisation reaction took place, perfectly matching the rule Acid + Base → Salt + Water + Heat.

## Key points

*   A neutralisation reaction occurs when an acid and a base mix to form a salt and water, releasing heat in the process.
*   The resulting solution is neither acidic nor basic, meaning it has a neutral pH.
*   In everyday life, neutralisation is used to treat ant bites with baking soda and to fix acidic soil with lime.
*   Factory waste is neutralised with basic substances before being released into lakes to protect aquatic life.
*   The chemical equation for this reaction is Acid + Base → Salt + Water + Heat.
