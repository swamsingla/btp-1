## 2. Introduction  
Imagine navigating a city grid: streets run horizontally and vertically, allowing you to pinpoint locations using addresses like "3rd Street, 5th Avenue." This is the essence of the **Cartesian system**, a foundational tool in coordinate geometry. In this topic, you’ll learn how to represent points on a plane using two perpendicular number lines—the **x-axis** and **y-axis**—and understand how these axes divide the plane into regions called **quadrants**. By mastering this system, you’ll gain the ability to describe positions mathematically, laying the groundwork for advanced topics like graphing linear equations and transformations.  

This connects directly to your prior study of number lines, where you learned to associate numbers with distances from a fixed origin. Now, we extend this idea to two dimensions!

## 3. Explanation  

### 3.1 Definition of the Cartesian System  
> **The Cartesian system** is a method of representing points on a plane using two perpendicular number lines, the **x-axis** (horizontal) and the **y-axis** (vertical), which intersect at a common point called the **origin**.  

#### Key Components:  
- **Origin (O)**: The intersection point of the x-axis and y-axis, denoted as $(0, 0) $.  
- **Axes**:  
  - **x-axis**: Horizontal line with positive direction towards the right (OX) and negative direction towards the left (OX').  
  - **y-axis**: Vertical line with positive direction upwards (OY) and negative direction downwards (OY').  
- **Quadrants**: The axes divide the plane into four regions labeled **I, II, III, and IV** in counterclockwise order from the positive x-axis.  

> **Important Theorem**: The position of any point on the plane is uniquely determined by its coordinates $ (x, y) $, where $ x $ measures the horizontal distance from the y-axis and $ y $ measures the vertical distance from the x-axis.  

### 3.2 Construction of the Cartesian Plane  
To construct the Cartesian plane:  
1. Draw two perpendicular number lines, X′X (horizontal) and Y′Y (vertical), intersecting at their zeros (origins).  
2. Label the horizontal line as the **x-axis** and the vertical line as the **y-axis**.  
3. Divide the plane into four quadrants using these axes.  

> **Note**: The choice of horizontal and vertical axes simplifies calculations and ensures consistency in representing points.  

### 3.3 Quadrant Identification  
Each quadrant has distinct sign conventions for coordinates:  
- **Quadrant I**: $ x > 0 $, $ y > 0 $ (positive x and y)  
- **Quadrant II**: $ x < 0 $, $ y > 0 $ (negative x, positive y)  
- **Quadrant III**: $ x < 0 $, $ y < 0 $ (both negative)  
- **Quadrant IV**: $ x > 0 $, $ y < 0 $ (positive x, negative y)  

> **Tip**: Remember the phrase *"All Students Take Calculus"* to recall quadrant signs:  
- **Quadrant I**: All positive  
- **Quadrant II**: Students (y) positive  
- **Quadrant III**: Take (x and y) negative  
- **Quadrant IV**: Calculus (y) negative  

### 3.4 Locating Points on the Plane  
To plot a point $ (a, b) $:  
1. Move $ a $ units along the x-axis (right for positive, left for negative).  
2. Move $ b $ units parallel to the y-axis (up for positive, down for negative).  
3. Mark the intersection as the point $ (a, b) $.  

> **Example**: To plot $ (2, -3) $, move 2 units right on the x-axis, then 3 units down to reach the point.

## 4. Solved Examples  

### Example 1: Identifying Quadrants  
**Problem**: Determine the quadrant(s) where the following points lie:  
- $ (-2, 4) $  
- $ (3, -5) $  
- $ (0, -1) $  
- $ (-6, -7) $  

**Solution**:  
1. $ (-2, 4) $: Negative $ x $, positive $ y $ → **Quadrant II**  
2. $ (3, -5) $: Positive $ x $, negative $ y $ → **Quadrant IV**  
3. $ (0, -1) $: Lies on the **y-axis** (not in any quadrant).  
4. $ (-6, -7) $: Both negative → **Quadrant III**  

### Example 2: Plotting Points  
**Problem**: Plot the points $ A(1, 2) $, $ B(-3, 4) $, and $ C(0, -5) $ on the Cartesian plane.  

**Solution**:  
1. **Point A(1, 2)**: Move 1 unit right on the x-axis, then 2 units up.  
2. **Point B(-3, 4)**: Move 3 units left on the x-axis, then 4 units up.  
3. **Point C(0, -5)**: Stay on the y-axis, move 5 units down.  

### Example 3: Determining Coordinates  
**Problem**: What are the coordinates of the point that lies 4 units left of the origin and 2 units down?  

**Solution**:  
- Moving left from the origin implies $ x = -4 $.  
- Moving down implies $ y = -2 $.  
Thus, the coordinates are $ (-4, -2) $.  

### Example 4: Quadrant Sign Analysis  
**Problem**: Which quadrant does the point $ (5, -3) $ lie in?  

**Solution**:  
- $ x = 5 $ is positive, $ y = -3 $ is negative → **Quadrant IV**.  

### Example 5: Origin and Axes  
**Problem**: Is the origin considered part of any quadrant? Why or why not?  

**Solution**:  
No. The origin $ (0, 0) $ is the intersection of the axes and does not belong to any quadrant. It serves as the reference point for all coordinates.

## 6. Summary  
- The **Cartesian system** uses two perpendicular axes (x-axis and y-axis) to locate points on a plane.  
- The **origin** is the intersection point of the axes, denoted as $ (0, 0) $.  
- The plane is divided into **four quadrants** with distinct sign conventions for coordinates.  
- Points are represented as ordered pairs $ (x, y) $, where $ x $ is the horizontal distance and $ y $ is the vertical distance.  
- The **x-axis** runs horizontally, while the **y-axis** runs vertically.  
- Quadrants are numbered I to IV counterclockwise from the positive x-axis.

## 8. Student Corner  

### Common Mistakes  
- **Mistake**: Confusing the x-axis and y-axis when plotting points.  
  **Correction**: Always move along the x-axis first, then the y-axis.  
- **Mistake**: Misidentifying quadrants due to incorrect sign analysis.  
  **Correction**: Use the acronym *"All Students Take Calculus"* to remember quadrant signs.  

### Exam Tips  
- Practice plotting points quickly to save time during exams.  
- Memorize quadrant sign conventions to avoid errors in coordinate interpretation.  

### Memory Aid  
**Rhyme**: *"Right and Up, Quadrant One; Left and Up, Two; Left and Down, Three; Right and Down, Four!"*
