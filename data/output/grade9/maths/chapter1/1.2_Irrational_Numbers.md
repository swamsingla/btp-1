## 1. Prerequisites  
Before studying irrational numbers, students should:  
- Understand **rational numbers** (numbers expressible as $ \frac{p}{q} $, where $ p $ and $ q $ are integers and $ q \neq 0 $)  
- Be familiar with **square roots** and **decimal expansions** of numbers  
- Know basic **set notation**, such as $ \in $ (element of) and $ \notin $ (not an element of)

## 2. Introduction  
**Have you ever wondered if there are numbers that can’t be expressed as fractions?**  
This question led ancient Greeks, like the Pythagoreans, to discover **irrational numbers**—numbers that defy the simple fraction representation. In this section, you’ll explore what defines an irrational number, how they differ from rationals, and their significance in the number system. By the end, you’ll understand why numbers like $ \sqrt{2} $ and $ \pi $ are unique and how they fill gaps on the number line.  

---  

## 3. Explanation  

### 3.1 Definition of Irrational Numbers  
> **An irrational number** is a number that **cannot be expressed** in the form $ \frac{p}{q} $, where $ p $ and $ q $ are integers and $ q \neq 0 $.  

Examples include:  
- $ \sqrt{2} $ (non-perfect square roots)  
- $ \pi $ (ratio of circumference to diameter of a circle)  
- Non-repeating, non-terminating decimals like $ 0.10110111011110\ldots $  

### 3.2 Historical Context  
> The Pythagoreans discovered irrational numbers around 400 BC. They believed all numbers could be expressed as ratios, but Hippacus of Croton proved $ \sqrt{2} $ was irrational—a revelation that disrupted their worldview.  

### 3.3 Decimal Expansions  
> Irrational numbers have **non-repeating, non-terminating decimal expansions**. Unlike rationals, their decimals never settle into a repeating pattern.  

### 3.4 Proof of Irrationality (Example: $ \sqrt{2} $)  
Assume $ \sqrt{2} = \frac{p}{q} $, where $ p $ and $ q $ are coprime integers. Squaring both sides gives:

$$
2 = \frac{p^2}{q^2} \implies p^2 = 2q^2

$$  
This implies $ p^2 $ is even, so $ p $ must be even. Let $ p = 2k $. Substituting:

$$
(2k)^2 = 2q^2 \implies 4k^2 = 2q^2 \implies 2k^2 = q^2

$$  
Now $ q^2 $ is even, so $ q $ is even. But this contradicts the assumption that $ p $ and $ q $ are coprime. Hence, $ \sqrt{2} $ is irrational.  

### 3.5 Role in the Number Line  
> When combined with rational numbers, irrational numbers **completely fill the number line**. This means every point on the number line corresponds to a real number (either rational or irrational).

## 4. Solved Examples  

**Example 1:** Determine if $ \sqrt{5} $ is irrational.  
**Solution:**  
Assume $ \sqrt{5} = \frac{p}{q} $, where $ p $ and $ q $ are coprime integers. Squaring:

$$
5 = \frac{p^2}{q^2} \implies p^2 = 5q^2

$$  
This implies $ p^2 $ is divisible by 5, so $ p $ is divisible by 5. Let $ p = 5k $. Substituting:

$$
(5k)^2 = 5q^2 \implies 25k^2 = 5q^2 \implies 5k^2 = q^2

$$  
Thus, $ q^2 $ is divisible by 5, meaning $ q $ is divisible by 5. Contradiction! Therefore, $ \sqrt{5} $ is irrational.  

**Example 2:** Is $ 0.\overline{3} $ irrational?  
**Solution:**  
The decimal $ 0.\overline{3} $ is a **repeating decimal**, which can be expressed as $ \frac{1}{3} $. Since it’s rational, the answer is **no**.  

**Example 3:** Why is $ \pi $ irrational?  
**Solution:**  
$ \pi $ represents the ratio of a circle’s circumference to its diameter. Though widely used, it was proven irrational by Lambert in 1768. Its decimal expansion is **non-repeating and non-terminating**:

$$
\pi \approx 3.141592653589793\ldots

$$  
No finite fraction can exactly represent $ \pi $.  

**Example 4:** Identify the irrational number(s) among $ 0.25, \sqrt{9}, \sqrt{11}, \frac{22}{7} $.  
**Solution:**  
- $ 0.25 = \frac{1}{4} $ → Rational  
- $ \sqrt{9} = 3 $ → Rational  
- $ \sqrt{11} $ → Irrational (non-perfect square)  
- $ \frac{22}{7} $ → Rational  
Only $ \sqrt{11} $ is irrational.  

**Example 5:** Prove $ \sqrt{17} $ is irrational.  
**Solution:**  
Assume $ \sqrt{17} = \frac{p}{q} $, with $ p $ and $ q $ coprime. Squaring:

$$
17 = \frac{p^2}{q^2} \implies p^2 = 17q^2

$$  
This implies $ p $ is divisible by 17. Let $ p = 17k $. Substituting:

$$
(17k)^2 = 17q^2 \implies 289k^2 = 17q^2 \implies 17k^2 = q^2

$$  
Thus, $ q $ is divisible by 17, contradicting the coprimality of $ p $ and $ q $. Hence, $ \sqrt{17} $ is irrational.

## 5. Practice Problems  

🟢 **Easy:**  
1. Is $ \sqrt{16} $ irrational?  
2. Which of these is irrational: $ 0.333\ldots $, $ \sqrt{2} $, $ \frac{5}{11} $?  

🟡 **Medium:**  
3. Prove that $ \sqrt{12} $ is irrational.  
4. Compare $ \sqrt{5} $ and $ \sqrt{7} $: which is larger?  

🔴 **Hard:**  
5. Show that $ 0.10110111011110\ldots $ is irrational.  

**Answers:**  

1. **Answer:** No. $ \sqrt{16} = 4 $, which is rational.  
2. **Answer:** $ \sqrt{2} $ is irrational. $ 0.333\ldots = \frac{1}{3} $, and $ \frac{5}{11} $ is rational.  

3. **Answer:** Assume $ \sqrt{12} = \frac{p}{q} $. Squaring: $ 12 = \frac{p^2}{q^2} \implies p^2 = 12q^2 $. Thus, $ p $ is divisible by 2 and 3, implying $ q $ is too. Contradiction. Hence, $ \sqrt{12} $ is irrational.  

4. **Answer:** $ \sqrt{7} > \sqrt{5} $ because the square root function increases with the input.  

5. **Answer:** The decimal $ 0.10110111011110\ldots $ has a non-repeating, non-terminating pattern. Since it cannot be expressed as $ \frac{p}{q} $, it is irrational.

## 6. Summary  
- An **irrational number** cannot be written as $ \frac{p}{q} $, where $ p $ and $ q $ are integers.  
- Examples include $ \sqrt{2} $, $ \pi $, and non-repeating decimals.  
- Irrationals have **non-repeating, non-terminating decimal expansions**.  
- Their discovery challenged ancient beliefs about numbers.  
- Combining rationals and irrationals fills the number line completely.

## 8. Student Corner  

### Common Mistakes  
- ❌ Confusing **all non-repeating decimals** with irrationals: Only non-repeating, **non-terminating** decimals are irrational.  
- ❌ Assuming all square roots are irrational: Perfect squares like $ \sqrt{16} = 4 $ are rational.  

### Exam Tips  
- Memorize key examples: $ \sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}, \pi $.  
- Practice proofs for irrationality using contradiction.  

### Memory Aid  
**IRRATIONAL** = **I**t can’t be **R**epresented as a **R**atio of integers.
