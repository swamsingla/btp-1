## 2. Introduction  
Have you ever wondered why some numbers have repeating decimals while others terminate? For example, $ \frac{1}{2} = 0.5 $ ends, but $ \frac{1}{3} = 0.\overline{3} $ repeats infinitely. In this topic, we explore **decimal expansions of real numbers**, learning how to distinguish **rational** (terminating or repeating) from **irrational** (non-repeating, non-terminating) numbers. By analyzing remainders during division, we uncover patterns that reveal the nature of real numbers. This builds on your understanding of fractions and prepares you for advanced topics like irrational numbers and operations on real numbers.  

---

## 3. Explanation  

### 3.1 Definitions  
> **Terminating Decimal**: A decimal expansion that ends after a finite number of digits. Example: $ \frac{7}{8} = 0.875 $.  
> **Non-Terminating Recurring Decimal**: A decimal expansion that repeats indefinitely. Example: $ \frac{1}{3} = 0.\overline{3} $.  
> **Repeating Block**: A sequence of digits that repeats in a non-terminating decimal. Example: In $ \frac{1}{7} = 0.\overline{142857} $, the repeating block is "142857".  

### 3.2 Key Concepts  
> When dividing a rational number $ \frac{p}{q} $ (where $ q \neq 0 $), two outcomes occur:  
> 1. **Case 1**: The remainder eventually becomes **zero** → Decimal expansion is **terminating**.  
> 2. **Case 2**: The remainder **repeats** → Decimal expansion is **non-terminating recurring**.  
>  
> The **number of digits in the repeating block** is always **less than the divisor**. For example, in $ \frac{1}{7} $, the repeating block has 6 digits, and the divisor is 7.  

### 3.3 Proof of the Theorem  
> **Theorem**: Any rational number $ \frac{p}{q} $ (with $ q \neq 0 $) has either a terminating or non-terminating recurring decimal expansion.  
>  
> **Proof**:  
> 1. Perform the division $ p \div q $.  
> 2. At each step, the remainder is less than the divisor $ q $.  
> 3. There are only finitely many possible remainders (from 0 to $ q-1 $).  
> 4. If a remainder **repeats**, the sequence of digits in the quotient will also repeat.  
> 5. Thus, the decimal expansion must either terminate (if a remainder becomes zero) or recur (if a remainder repeats).

## 4. Solved Examples  

### Example 1:  
**Find the decimal expansion of $ \frac{10}{3} $.**  
**Solution**:  
1. Divide 10 by 3:  
   - 3 × 3 = 9, remainder 1.  
   - Bring down a 0: 10 ÷ 3 = 3, remainder 1.  
   - Repeat: Remainder 1 → Quotient digit 3, new remainder 1.  
   - This cycle continues indefinitely.  
2. Result: $ \frac{10}{3} = 3.\overline{3} $.  

### Example 2:  
**Determine whether $ \frac{7}{8} $ is terminating or recurring.**  
**Solution**:  
1. Divide 7 by 8:  
   - 8 × 0 = 0, remainder 7.  
   - Bring down a 0: 70 ÷ 8 = 8, remainder 6.  
   - Bring down a 0: 60 ÷ 8 = 7, remainder 4.  
   - Bring down a 0: 40 ÷ 8 = 5, remainder 0.  
2. Since the remainder becomes zero, the decimal terminates: $ \frac{7}{8} = 0.875 $.  

### Example 3:  
**Analyze the decimal expansion of $ \frac{1}{7} $.**  
**Solution**:  
1. Divide 1 by 7:  
   - Remainders: 1, 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1 (repeat).  
   - Quotient digits: 1, 4, 2, 8, 5, 7 (repeat).  
2. Result: $ \frac{1}{7} = 0.\overline{142857} $.

## 5. Practice Problems  

### 🟢 Easy  
**Problem 1**: Convert $ \frac{5}{2} $ to a decimal and classify its expansion.  
**Answer**:  
- Division: $ 5 \div 2 = 2.5 $.  
- Remainder becomes zero → **Terminating decimal**.  

### 🟡 Medium  
**Problem 2**: Determine if $ \frac{13}{6} $ is terminating or recurring.  
**Answer**:  
- Division steps:  
  - 13 ÷ 6 = 2, remainder 1.  
  - Bring down 0: 10 ÷ 6 = 1, remainder 4.  
  - Bring down 0: 40 ÷ 6 = 6, remainder 4.  
  - Remainder 4 repeats → **Non-terminating recurring decimal**: $ 2.\overline{16} $.  

### 🔴 Hard  
**Problem 3**: Prove that $ \frac{1}{13} $ has a repeating block of 6 digits.  
**Answer**:  
- Perform division $ 1 \div 13 $:  
  - Remainders: 1, 10, 9, 12, 3, 4, 1 (repeat).  
  - Quotient digits: 0, 7, 6, 9, 2, 3 (repeat).  
- Since the repeating block has 6 digits (1, 10, 9, 12, 3, 4), the decimal expansion is $ 0.\overline{076923} $.

## 6. Summary  
- **Decimal expansions** of rational numbers are either terminating or non-terminating recurring.  
- **Terminating decimals** occur when division results in a remainder of zero.  
- **Non-terminating recurring decimals** arise when remainders repeat, forming a repeating block.  
- The **length of the repeating block** is always less than the divisor.  
- Rational numbers have **finite or infinite periodic decimal expansions**, while irrational numbers have **non-repeating, non-terminating decimals**.  
- The **division algorithm** ensures that remainders eventually repeat or terminate.

## 8. Student Corner  

### Common Mistakes  
1. **Confusing terminating and recurring decimals**:  
   - ❌ Mistake: Thinking $ \frac{1}{3} = 0.333 $ instead of $ 0.\overline{3} $.  
   - ✅ Correction: Always use the overline (or ellipsis) to denote repetition.  

2. **Ignoring remainders**:  
   - ❌ Mistake: Skipping remainders during division leads to incorrect classifications.  
   - ✅ Correction: Track remainders to determine if the decimal terminates or recurs.  

### Exam Tips  
- **Memorize** the decimal expansions of common fractions (e.g., $ \frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{6}, \frac{1}{7} $) for quick recognition.  
- **Practice long division** with denominators like 3, 7, 11 to identify repeating patterns.  

### Memory Aid  
**"Rational Numbers Have Patterns!"**  
- Remember: Rational numbers always have **terminating** or **repeating** decimals (patterns).  
- Irrationals have **no pattern** (like π or √2).
