## 1. Prerequisites  
Before diving into the laws of exponents for real numbers, ensure you are familiar with:  
- **Basic exponent rules**: Multiplying powers with the same base, raising a power to another power, and dividing powers.  
- **Negative exponents**: Understanding that $ a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n} $.  
- **Rational exponents**: Recognizing that $ a^{m/n} $ represents the $ n $ th root of $ a^m $.  

These foundational skills will enable you to grasp how these laws extend to real numbers and rational exponents.

Today, we’ll explore how these laws apply to **real numbers**, including **negative exponents** and **rational exponents**. By the end of this lesson, you’ll be able to simplify complex expressions involving real numbers raised to various powers confidently.  

This builds on your prior knowledge of integer exponents while expanding it to handle more advanced scenarios, such as fractional exponents and negative exponents.

## 3. Explanation  

### 3.1 Definition of Exponents  
Let $ a > 0 $ be a real number and $ n $ a positive integer. We define:

$$
a^n = a \times a \times \cdots \times a \quad (\text{n times})

$$  
Here, $ a $ is the **base**, and $ n $ is the **exponent**. This definition extends to **negative exponents** and **rational exponents** as described below.  

> **Definition**: For a real number $ a > 0 $, $ a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n} $, where $ n $ is a positive integer.  
> **Definition**: For a real number $ a > 0 $ and integers $ m, n $, $ a^{m/n} = \left( a^{1/n} \right)^m = \left( \sqrt[n]{a} \right)^m $.  

### 3.2 Extended Laws of Exponents  
The following laws hold for **positive real numbers** $ a $ and rational numbers $ p, q $:  
> **Law 1**: $ a^p \cdot a^q = a^{p+q} $  
> **Law 2**: $ (a^p)^q = a^{pq} $  
> **Law 3**: $ \frac{a^p}{a^q} = a^{p-q} $ (equivalent to $ a^p \cdot a^{-q} $)  
> **Law 4**: $ (ab)^p = a^p \cdot b^p $  

#### Proof of Law 1:  
Let $ a^p \cdot a^q $. Express both terms as repeated multiplication:

$$
a^p = a \times a \times \cdots \times a \quad (\text{p times})

$$

$$
a^q = a \times a \times \cdots \times a \quad (\text{q times})

$$
Multiplying them gives $ a $ multiplied $ p + q $ times, i.e., $ a^{p+q} $.  

#### Proof of Law 2:  
Start with $ (a^p)^q $. This means multiplying $ a^p $ by itself $ q $ times:

$$
(a^p)^q = a^p \cdot a^p \cdot \cdots \cdot a^p \quad (\text{q times}) = a^{p+ p + \cdots + p} = a^{pq}

$$

#### Proof of Law 3:  
Using Law 1:

$$
\frac{a^p}{a^q} = a^p \cdot a^{-q} = a^{p - q}

$$

#### Proof of Law 4:  
Expand $ (ab)^p $ as $ ab \cdot ab \cdot \cdots \cdot ab $ ($ p $ times):

$$
(ab)^p = a^p \cdot b^p

$$

### 3.3 Application to Negative and Rational Exponents  
The laws also apply when exponents are negative or rational:  
- **Negative exponents**: $ a^{-p} = \frac{1}{a^p} $, so $ a^{-p} \cdot a^{-q} = a^{-p - q} $.  
- **Rational exponents**: For $ a^{m/n} $, interpret $ a^{1/n} $ as the $ n $ th root of $ a $, then raise it to the power $ m $.  

> **Example**: $ 64^{2/3} = \left( \sqrt[3]{64} \right)^2 = 4^2 = 16 $.

## 4. Solved Examples  

### **Example 1:** Simplify $ 2^3 \cdot 2^4 $.  
**Solution**:  
Using Law 1:

$$
2^3 \cdot 2^4 = 2^{3+4} = 2^7

$$  
**Answer**: $ 2^7 $  

### **Example 2:** Simplify $ (3^2)^5 $.  
**Solution**:  
Using Law 2:

$$
(3^2)^5 = 3^{2 \cdot 5} = 3^{10}

$$  
**Answer**: $ 3^{10} $  

### **Example 3:** Simplify $ \frac{5^7}{5^3} $.  
**Solution**:  
Using Law 3:

$$
\frac{5^7}{5^3} = 5^{7 - 3} = 5^4

$$  
**Answer**: $ 5^4 $  

### **Example 4:** Simplify $ (2 \cdot 3)^4 $.  
**Solution**:  
Using Law 4:

$$
(2 \cdot 3)^4 = 2^4 \cdot 3^4

$$  
**Answer**: $ 2^4 \cdot 3^4 $  

### **Example 5:** Simplify $ 7^{-2} \cdot 7^{-3} $.  
**Solution**:  
Using Law 1 and negative exponents:

$$
7^{-2} \cdot 7^{-3} = 7^{-2 + (-3)} = 7^{-5} = \frac{1}{7^5}

$$  
**Answer**: $ \frac{1}{7^5} $

## 5. Practice Problems  

### 🟢 Easy  
1. Simplify $ 5^2 \cdot 5^3 $.  
2. Simplify $ (2^4)^3 $.  
3. Simplify $ \frac{10^6}{10^2} $.  

### 🟡 Medium  
4. Simplify $ (3 \cdot 4)^2 $.  
5. Simplify $ 8^{-1} \cdot 8^{-2} $.  

### 🔴 Hard  
6. Simplify $ 16^{3/4} \cdot 16^{1/2} $.  

---

### Answers:  

1. **Solution**: Using Law 1:

$$
5^2 \cdot 5^3 = 5^{2+3} = 5^5

$$  
**Answer**: $ 5^5 $  

2. **Solution**: Using Law 2:

$$
(2^4)^3 = 2^{4 \cdot 3} = 2^{12}

$$  
**Answer**: $ 2^{12} $  

3. **Solution**: Using Law 3:

$$
\frac{10^6}{10^2} = 10^{6 - 2} = 10^4

$$  
**Answer**: $ 10^4 $  

4. **Solution**: Using Law 4:

$$
(3 \cdot 4)^2 = 3^2 \cdot 4^2 = 9 \cdot 16 = 144

$$  
**Answer**: $ 144 $  

5. **Solution**: Using Law 1 and negative exponents:

$$
8^{-1} \cdot 8^{-2} = 8^{-1 + (-2)} = 8^{-3} = \frac{1}{8^3} = \frac{1}{512}

$$  
**Answer**: $ \frac{1}{512} $  

6. **Solution**: Simplify each term separately:  
- $ 16^{3/4} = \left( \sqrt[4]{16} \right)^3 = 2^3 = 8 $  
- $ 16^{1/2} = \sqrt{16} = 4 $  
Multiply results:

$$
8 \cdot 4 = 32

$$  
**Answer**: $ 32 $

## 6. Summary  
- **Law 1**: $ a^p \cdot a^q = a^{p+q} $  
- **Law 2**: $ (a^p)^q = a^{pq} $  
- **Law 3**: $ \frac{a^p}{a^q} = a^{p-q} $  
- **Law 4**: $ (ab)^p = a^p \cdot b^p $  
- Negative exponents: $ a^{-p} = \frac{1}{a^p} $  
- Rational exponents: $ a^{m/n} = \left( \sqrt[n]{a} \right)^m $  

These laws allow simplification of expressions involving real numbers raised to any rational power.  

---  

## 7. Key Formulas  

| Formula | Description |  
|--------|-------------|  
| $ a^p \cdot a^q = a^{p+q} $ | Product of powers with the same base |  
| $ (a^p)^q = a^{pq} $ | Power of a power |  
| $ \frac{a^p}{a^q} = a^{p-q} $ | Quotient of powers |  
| $ (ab)^p = a^p \cdot b^p $ | Power of a product |  
| $ a^{-p} = \frac{1}{a^p} $ | Negative exponents |  
| $ a^{m/n} = \left( \sqrt[n]{a} \right)^m $ | Rational exponents |

## 8. Student Corner  

### **Common Mistakes**  
- **Mistake 1**: Confusing $ a^p \cdot a^q = a^{p+q} $ with $ (a^p)^q = a^{p^q} $.  
  **Correction**: Remember, multiplication adds exponents; raising a power multiplies them.  
- **Mistake 2**: Forgetting to convert negative exponents to fractions.  
  **Correction**: Always rewrite $ a^{-n} $ as $ \frac{1}{a^n} $.  

### **Exam Tips**  
- **Tip 1**: Memorize the four laws of exponents and practice applying them to mixed operations.  
- **Tip 2**: When dealing with rational exponents, simplify radicals first before applying exponents.  

### **Memory Aid**  
Use the acronym **LEAP** to recall the four laws:  
- **L**aw 1: Multiply → Add exponents  
- **E**xponent Rule: Raise a power → Multiply exponents  
- **A**ddition/Subtraction: Divide → Subtract exponents  
- **P**roduct Rule: Multiply bases → Multiply individual powers
