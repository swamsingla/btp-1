## 1. Prerequisites  
Before diving into operations on real numbers, ensure you understand:  
- **Rational Numbers**: Numbers expressible as $ \frac{p}{q} $, where $ p $ and $ q $ are integers and $ q \neq 0 $.  
- **Decimal Expansions**: Terminating decimals (e.g., $ 0.25 $) or repeating decimals (e.g., $ 0.\overline{3} $) indicate rational numbers.  
- **Closure Property**: Rational numbers are closed under addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division (except by zero).

## 2. Introduction  
Have you ever wondered why $ \sqrt{2} + 2 $ is irrational? Or why multiplying $ \sqrt{5} $ by itself gives a whole number? This topic explores how operations like addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division affect **real numbers**, including both rational and irrational numbers. By the end of this section, you’ll understand:  
- How combining rational and irrational numbers behaves.  
- When the result of such operations remains irrational or becomes rational.  
- Practical methods to simplify expressions involving radicals.  

This builds on your prior knowledge of decimal expansions and irrational numbers, preparing you for advanced topics like exponent laws.  

---

## 3. Explanation  

### 3.1 Definitions & Properties  
> **Real Numbers** include all rational and irrational numbers. They can be represented on the number line.  
> **Rational Numbers** are numbers that can be expressed as $ \frac{p}{q} $, where $ p $ and $ q $ are integers and $ q \neq 0 $.  
> **Irrational Numbers** cannot be expressed as $ \frac{p}{q} $; their decimal expansions are non-terminating and non-repeating.  

#### Key Theorems  
> **Theorem 1**: The sum or difference of a **rational number** and an **irrational number** is **always irrational**.  
**Proof**: Suppose $ r $ is rational and $ i $ is irrational. Assume $ r + i = q $, where $ q $ is rational. Then $ i = q - r $. Since $ q - r $ is rational (difference of rationals), this contradicts $ i $ being irrational. Hence, $ r + i $ must be irrational.  

> **Theorem 2**: The product or quotient of a **non-zero rational number** and an **irrational number** is **always irrational**.  
**Proof**: Let $ r \neq 0 $ be rational and $ i $ irrational. Assume $ r \cdot i = q $, where $ q $ is rational. Then $ i = \frac{q}{r} $, which is rational (since $ q/r $ is rational). Contradiction. Thus, $ r \cdot i $ is irrational.  

> **Theorem 3**: Adding, subtracting, multiplying, or dividing two **irrational numbers** can yield either a **rational** or **irrational** result.  
**Example**: $ \sqrt{2} + (-\sqrt{2}) = 0 $ (rational), while $ \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} $ is irrational.  

### 3.2 Operations on Radicals  
When simplifying expressions with radicals:  
1. **Addition/Subtraction**: Combine like terms (same radicand).  
   - Example: $ 2\sqrt{3} + 3\sqrt{3} = 5\sqrt{3} $.  
2. **Multiplication**: Multiply coefficients and radicals separately.  
   - Example: $ 6\sqrt{5} \times 2\sqrt{5} = 12 \times (\sqrt{5})^2 = 12 \times 5 = 60 $.  
3. **Division**: Simplify the fraction under the radical.  
   - Example: $ \frac{8\sqrt{15}}{2\sqrt{3}} = 4 \times \sqrt{\frac{15}{3}} = 4\sqrt{5} $.

## 4. Solved Examples  

### Example 1:  
**Check if $ 7\sqrt{5} $, $ \frac{7}{\sqrt{5}} $, $ \sqrt{2} + 21 $, and $ \pi - 2 $ are irrational.**  
**Solution**:  
- $ \sqrt{5} = 2.236... $ → $ 7\sqrt{5} = 15.652... $ (non-terminating, non-repeating).  
- $ \frac{7}{\sqrt{5}} = \frac{7\sqrt{5}}{5} = 3.1304... $ (non-terminating, non-repeating).  
- $ \sqrt{2} = 1.4142... $ → $ \sqrt{2} + 21 = 22.4142... $ (non-terminating, non-repeating).  
- $ \pi = 3.1415... $ → $ \pi - 2 = 1.1415... $ (non-terminating, non-repeating).  
**Conclusion**: All are irrational.  

### Example 2:  
**Add $ 2\sqrt{2} + 3\sqrt{3} $ and $ -3\sqrt{3} $.**  
**Solution**:

$$
(2\sqrt{2} + 3\sqrt{3}) + (-3\sqrt{3}) = 2\sqrt{2} + (3\sqrt{3} - 3\sqrt{3}) = 2\sqrt{2}

$$  

### Example 3:  
**Multiply $ 6\sqrt{5} $ by $ 2\sqrt{5} $.**  
**Solution**:

$$
6\sqrt{5} \times 2\sqrt{5} = (6 \times 2) \times (\sqrt{5} \times \sqrt{5}) = 12 \times 5 = 60

$$  

### Example 4:  
**Divide $ 8\sqrt{15} $ by $ 2\sqrt{3} $.**  
**Solution**:

$$
\frac{8\sqrt{15}}{2\sqrt{3}} = \frac{8}{2} \times \frac{\sqrt{15}}{\sqrt{3}} = 4 \times \sqrt{\frac{15}{3}} = 4\sqrt{5}

$$  

### Example 5:  
**Verify $ (\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b})(\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b}) = a - b $.**  
**Solution**:  
Using identity $ (x+y)(x-y)=x^2 - y^2 $:

$$
(\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b})(\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b}) = (\sqrt{a})^2 - (\sqrt{b})^2 = a - b

$$

## 5. Practice Problems  

### 🟢 Easy  
1. Is $ \sqrt{9} + \frac{1}{2} $ rational? Justify.  
2. Simplify $ 5\sqrt{7} + 2\sqrt{7} $.  

### 🟡 Medium  
3. Prove that $ \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} $ is irrational.  
4. Multiply $ 3\sqrt{6} $ by $ 4\sqrt{6} $.  

### 🔴 Hard  
5. Show that $ \frac{\sqrt{12}}{\sqrt{3}} $ is rational.  

**Answers**:  
1. **Answer**: Yes. $ \sqrt{9} = 3 $, so $ 3 + \frac{1}{2} = \frac{7}{2} $, which is rational.  
2. **Answer**: $ 5\sqrt{7} + 2\sqrt{7} = 7\sqrt{7} $.  
3. **Answer**: Assume $ \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} = r $ (rational). Squaring: $ 2 + 3 + 2\sqrt{6} = r^2 $ → $ 5 + 2\sqrt{6} = r^2 $. Rearrange: $ 2\sqrt{6} = r^2 - 5 $. Since $ r^2 - 5 $ is rational, $ \sqrt{6} $ would be rational, contradiction.  
4. **Answer**: $ 3\sqrt{6} \times 4\sqrt{6} = 12 \times 6 = 72 $.  
5. **Answer**: $ \frac{\sqrt{12}}{\sqrt{3}} = \sqrt{\frac{12}{3}} = \sqrt{4} = 2 $, which is rational.

## 6. Summary  
- **Sum/Difference of rational and irrational**: Always irrational.  
- **Product/Quotient of non-zero rational and irrational**: Always irrational.  
- **Operations on irrationals**: May yield rational or irrational results.  
- **Radical simplification**: Combine like terms, multiply/divide coefficients and radicals.  
- **Square roots**: $ \sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{a} = a $.  
- **Examples**: $ 2\sqrt{3} + 3\sqrt{3} = 5\sqrt{3} $, $ \sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{2} = 2 $.

## 7. Key Formulas  

| Formula | Description |  
|--------|-------------|  
| $ a + b = b + a $ | Commutative law for addition |  
| $ ab = ba $ | Commutative law for multiplication |  
| $ (a + b) + c = a + (b + c) $ | Associative law for addition |  
| $ (ab)c = a(bc) $ | Associative law for multiplication |  
| $ a(b + c) = ab + ac $ | Distributive law |  
| $ \sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{a} = a $ | Square root property |  
| $ \frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}} = \sqrt{\frac{a}{b}} $ | Division of radicals |

## 8. Student Corner  

### Common Mistakes  
- ❌ Assuming $ \sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b} = \sqrt{a+b} $: **Correct**: $ \sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b} \neq \sqrt{a+b} $.  
- ❌ Forgetting to rationalize denominators: **Fix**: Multiply numerator and denominator by $ \sqrt{c} $ to eliminate radicals in the denominator.  

### Exam Tips  
- **Always simplify radicals** before comparing or operating.  
- **Use theorems** to quickly determine if results are rational or irrational.  

### Memory Aid  
**"Rational + Irrational = Irrational"** (Remember: R + I = I).
