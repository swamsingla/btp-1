## 1. Prerequisites  
Before diving into rays, you should already understand:  
- **Points**: A location in space represented by a dot (e.g., point $ A $).  
- **Lines**: Straight paths extending infinitely in both directions (denoted by $ \overleftrightarrow{AB} $).  
- **Line Segments**: Finite portions of a line between two endpoints (denoted by $ \overline{AB} $).  

These foundational ideas will help you grasp how rays differ from lines and line segments.

## 2. Introduction  
Have you ever watched sunlight spread out from a window or seen a laser pointer shine across a room? These are **rays**! In this lesson, you’ll learn how to recognize, name, and work with rays. By the end, you’ll be able to:  
- Identify rays in real-world situations.  
- Understand how rays are different from lines and line segments.  
- Solve problems involving ray notation and diagrams.  

This builds on your existing knowledge of points and lines, making it easier to visualize shapes and angles later!

## 3. Explanation  

### 3.1 Definition of a Ray  
A **ray** is a part of a line that starts at a specific point (called the **starting point** or **initial point**) and extends **indefinitely** in one direction.  
> **Definition**: A ray is a straight path that begins at a point and continues forever in one direction.  

**Visual Example**: Imagine a flashlight shining light. The beam starts at the flashlight’s bulb and spreads outward—this is a ray!  

### 3.2 Notation and Diagrams  
To name a ray:  
1. Identify the **starting point** (always written first).  
2. Choose any other point on the ray (written second).  
3. Use an arrowhead to indicate direction.  

**Example**: The ray in Figure 2.3 starts at point $ A $ and passes through $ P $. It is written as $ \overrightarrow{AP} $.  

**Important Rule**: The **starting point** cannot be changed. For instance, $ \overrightarrow{AP} $ is **not** the same as $ \overrightarrow{PA} $.  

### 3.3 Real-Life Models  
The source text provides three real-life examples of rays:  
1. **Beam of light from a lighthouse**  
2. **Ray of light from a torch**  
3. **Sun rays**  

These examples show how rays model natural phenomena where energy or light travels in one direction from a fixed origin.

## 4. Solved Examples  

### Example 1: Identifying Rays  
**Problem**: In Figure 2.7, can the ray $ \overrightarrow{OA} $ also be named $ \overrightarrow{OB} $?  
**Solution**:  
1. A ray is defined by its starting point and direction.  
2. Both $ A $ and $ B $ lie on the same ray, but the direction from $ O $ differs.  
3. Thus, $ \overrightarrow{OA} $ and $ \overrightarrow{OB} $ are **distinct rays**.  

**Answer**: No, because the direction from the starting point changes.  

### Example 2: Naming Rays  
**Problem**: In Figure 2.5, list all possible rays starting from point $ T $.  
**Solution**:  
1. Identify points on the ray: $ A, N, B $.  
2. Rays starting at $ T $: $ \overrightarrow{TA}, \overrightarrow{TN}, \overrightarrow{TB} $.  

**Answer**: $ \overrightarrow{TA}, \overrightarrow{TN}, \overrightarrow{TB} $.  

### Example 3: Intersections of Rays  
**Problem**: If rays $ \overrightarrow{OP} $ and $ \overrightarrow{OQ} $ intersect at point $ O $, what does this imply?  
**Solution**:  
1. The intersection implies both rays share the same starting point $ O $.  
2. This means $ O $ is the **common endpoint** of both rays.  

**Answer**: The rays originate from the same point $ O $.  

### Example 4: Misconception Correction  
**Problem**: Is a ray $ \overrightarrow{AB} $ the same as $ \overrightarrow{BA} $?  
**Solution**:  
1. $ \overrightarrow{AB} $ starts at $ A $ and goes toward $ B $.  
2. $ \overrightarrow{BA} $ starts at $ B $ and goes toward $ A $.  
3. They are **opposite rays** and represent different directions.  

**Answer**: No, they are opposite rays with different starting points.  

### Example 5: Practical Application  
**Problem**: A lighthouse emits a beam of light. If the light starts at the top of the lighthouse and shines toward the sea, what geometric shape represents this?  
**Solution**:  
1. The beam has a **fixed starting point** (the lighthouse).  
2. It extends infinitely in one direction (toward the sea).  
3. This matches the definition of a **ray**.  

**Answer**: A ray.

## 6. Summary  
- A **ray** starts at a point and extends infinitely in one direction.  
- Rays are denoted by $ \overrightarrow{AB} $, where $ A $ is the starting point.  
- Unlike lines, rays have **only one endpoint**.  
- Real-life examples include sunlight and flashlight beams.  
- Rays can be confused with lines, but they are distinct due to directionality.  

---  

## 7. Key Formulas  

| Formula | Description |  
|--------|------------|  
| $ \overrightarrow{AB} $ | Ray starting at $ A $, passing through $ B $. |  
| $ \overleftrightarrow{CD} $ | Line extending infinitely in both directions. |  
| $ \overline{EF} $ | Line segment between points $ E $ and $ F $. |  
| $ \text{Starts at } A \text{, goes through } B $ | Defines a ray $ \overrightarrow{AB} $. |  
| $ \text{Two distinct points define one line} $ | Theorem: Only one line passes through two points. |

## 8. Student Corner  

### Common Mistakes  
1. **Error**: Confusing rays with lines.  
   **Correction**: A line has no endpoints, while a ray has one.  
2. **Error**: Writing $ \overrightarrow{BA} $ instead of $ \overrightarrow{AB} $.  
   **Correction**: Always write the starting point first.  

### Exam Tips  
- Label diagrams clearly, using arrows to show direction.  
- Double-check ray names to ensure the starting point is correct.  

### Memory Aid  
**Rhyme**: *"Ray starts at a point, never ends, it's a beam of light from the sun!"*
