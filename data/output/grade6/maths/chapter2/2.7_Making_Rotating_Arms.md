## Making Rotating Arms
### Importance Score: 3/5

Have you ever seen a spinning top or a merry-go-round? They both involve rotating arms or circles that seem to move in different directions! In this topic, we'll explore how to create "rotating arms" using everyday objects like paper straws and a paper clip. We'll also learn about the relationship between angles and whether they can pass through certain slits.

### Introduction

### Explanation
#### What are Rotating Arms?
A **rotating arm** is a straight line that rotates around another fixed point. Think of it like a seesaw – when one side moves down, the other side moves up!

We can create rotating arms using two paper straws and a paper clip. Here's how:

1. Take two paper straws and a paper clip.
2. Insert the straws into the arms of the paper clip.
3. Your rotating arm is ready!

#### Comparing Angles
When we compare two angles, we want to know which one is larger or smaller. To do this, we use **superimposition**, where we overlap the angles to see which one covers more area.

$$

\text{Angle A} < \text{Angle B} \iff \text{Area of } \angle A + \text{Area of } \angle A = \text{Area of } \angle B

$$

In our case, we'll use the paper clips to create different angles and then arrange them in order from smallest to largest.

### Summary
Here's a quick summary of the key concepts:

• **Rotating arms** are straight lines that rotate around a fixed point.
• When comparing angles, use **superimposition** to determine which one is larger or smaller.
• If $ m\angle A = m\angle B $, then the arm will pass through the slit.

### Common Mistakes
Be careful not to confuse superimposition with addition! Also, remember that the measure of an angle determines whether it can pass through a slit.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Rotating arm**: a straight line that rotates around a fixed point.
- **Superimposition**: overlapping two or more angles to compare their sizes.
- **Angle**: a measure of the amount of turn between a straight line and a nearby vertical line.
