## 2. Introduction  
Have you ever noticed how numbers can create surprising patterns? In this lesson, we’ll explore how **number sequences** like odd numbers or counting numbers are connected to **square numbers**. You’ll discover how adding specific sequences can lead to perfect squares—and even visualize these relationships! Let’s dive in!  

---  

## 3. Explanation  

### 3.1 Odd Numbers and Square Numbers  
**Odd numbers** are numbers like 1, 3, 5, 7, etc., which are not divisible by 2. When you add the first few odd numbers, you get a fascinating pattern:

$$
1 = 1 \\
1 + 3 = 4 \\
1 + 3 + 5 = 9 \\
1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16 \\

$$  

> This pattern shows that the sum of the first $n $ odd numbers equals $ n^2 $. For example:  
>

$$
> \text{Sum of first } 3 \text{ odd numbers} = 1 + 3 + 5 = 9 = 3^2
>

$$  

#### Why does this work?  
Imagine building a square grid of dots. Each new layer adds an odd number of dots:  
- First layer: 1 dot → $1^2 $  
- Second layer: 3 dots → total $ 1 + 3 = 4 = 2^2 $  
- Third layer: 5 dots → total $ 1 + 3 + 5 = 9 = 3^2 $  

This visual approach explains why the sum of odd numbers always forms a perfect square.  

### 3.2 Counting Numbers Added Up and Down  
Another pattern involves adding counting numbers **up and down**:

$$
1 = 1 \\
1 + 2 + 1 = 4 \\
1 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 9 \\
1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 16 \\

$$  

> Here, the sum of the first $n $ counting numbers added up and down equals $ n^2 $. For example:  
>

$$
> \text{Sum of } 1 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 9 = 3^2
>

$$  

This works because the sequence mirrors the layers of a square grid. Adding numbers up to $n $ and then back down creates a symmetrical shape that fills the grid perfectly.  

### 3.3 General Rule for Summing Sequences  
Two key relationships emerge:  
1. **Odd Numbers**:

$$
   \text{Sum of first } n \text{ odd numbers} = n^2

$$  
2. **Counting Numbers (Up and Down)**:

$$
   \text{Sum of } 1 + 2 + \dots + n + \dots + 2 + 1 = n^2

$$  

These patterns show how different sequences can generate the same result through unique arrangements.

## 4. Solved Examples  

### Example 1: Sum of First 10 Odd Numbers  
**Question**: What is the sum of the first 10 odd numbers?  
**Solution**:  
Using the formula for the sum of odd numbers:

$$
\text{Sum} = n^2 \quad \text{where } n = 10

$$

$$
\text{Sum} = 10^2 = 100

$$  
**Answer**: $100 $  

### Example 2: Sum of First 100 Odd Numbers  
**Question**: Calculate the sum of the first 100 odd numbers.  
**Solution**:  
Apply the same formula:

$$
\text{Sum} = n^2 = 100^2 = 10,\!000

$$  
**Answer**: $10,\!000 $  

### Example 3: Sum of Up-and-Down Sequence  
**Question**: Find the value of $ 1 + 2 + 3 + \dots + 99 + 100 + 99 + \dots + 3 + 2 + 1 $.  
**Solution**:  
This follows the up-and-down pattern. Since the largest number is $ 100 $:

$$
\text{Sum} = 100^2 = 10,\!000

$$  
**Answer**: $10,\!000 $  

### Example 4: Triangular Numbers and Their Sums  
**Question**: What happens when you add pairs of consecutive triangular numbers?  
**Solution**:  
Triangular numbers are $ 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, \dots $. Adding consecutive pairs:

$$
1 + 3 = 4 \quad (\text{which is } 2^2) \\
3 + 6 = 9 \quad (\text{which is } 3^2) \\
6 + 10 = 16 \quad (\text{which is } 4^2)

$$  
**Pattern**: The sum of two consecutive triangular numbers is a perfect square.

## 5. Practice Problems  

🟢 **Easy**:  
1. What is the sum of the first 5 odd numbers?  
2. Calculate $1 + 2 + 1 $.  

🟡 **Medium**:  
3. Find the sum of the first 15 odd numbers.  
4. What is the value of $ 1 + 2 + 3 + \dots + 10 + 9 + \dots + 2 + 1 $?  

🔴 **Hard**:  
5. Prove that the sum of the first $ n $ odd numbers is always $ n^2 $.  
6. Add the first three triangular numbers and verify if their sum is a square number.  

**Answers**:  
1. **Solution**: $ 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 = 25 = 5^2 $.  
2. **Solution**: $ 1 + 2 + 1 = 4 = 2^2 $.  
3. **Solution**: $ 15^2 = 225 $.  
4. **Solution**: $ 10^2 = 100 $.  
5. **Solution**: Use induction or the visual grid method to prove $ 1 + 3 + 5 + \dots + (2n-1) = n^2 $.  
6. **Solution**: Triangular numbers: $ 1 + 3 + 6 = 10 $, which is not a square. Wait—the question may have an error. Correction: The sum of **consecutive** triangular numbers (e.g., $ 3 + 6 = 9 $) is a square.

## 6. Summary  
- The sum of the first $ n $ odd numbers equals $ n^2 $.  
- Adding counting numbers up and down also results in $ n^2 $.  
- Visualizing sequences as square grids clarifies these patterns.  
- Consecutive triangular numbers sum to square numbers.

## 8. Student Corner  

### Common Mistakes  
- Confusing square numbers with triangular numbers (e.g., $ 1 + 3 = 4 $ vs. $ 1 + 2 = 3 $).  
- Forgetting to square the number when applying the odd-number rule.  

### Memory Aid  
**"Odd numbers build squares, up-and-down counts fill grids!"**  

### Tip  
Always draw a grid or diagram to visualize how sequences form shapes. This helps avoid calculation errors!
