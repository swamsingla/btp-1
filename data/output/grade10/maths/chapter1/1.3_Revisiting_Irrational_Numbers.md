## 2. Introduction  
Have you ever wondered why certain numbers like $ \sqrt{2} $ or $ \pi $ can’t be written as simple fractions? While you’ve encountered irrational numbers in earlier classes, this section dives deeper—proving that numbers like $ \sqrt{p} $ (where $ p $ is a prime) are indeed irrational. By revisiting this concept, you’ll explore how the **Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic** helps establish this truth. This journey will sharpen your analytical skills and deepen your understanding of number systems.  

---  

## 3. Explanation  

### 3.1 Definitions and Key Concepts  
> **Irrational number**: A number that **cannot** be expressed in the form $ \frac{p}{q} $, where $ p $ and $ q $ are integers and $ q \neq 0 $. Examples include $ \sqrt{2}, \pi, e $, and non-repeating, non-terminating decimals.  

> **Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic**: Every composite number can be uniquely expressed as a product of prime numbers, disregarding the order of multiplication.  

> **Theorem 1.2**: Let $ p $ be a prime number. If $ p $ divides $ a^2 $, then $ p $ divides $ a $, where $ a $ is a positive integer.  

> **Theorem 1.3**: $ \sqrt{2} $ is irrational.  

### 3.2 Proof of Theorem 1.2  
Let $ a $ be a positive integer with prime factorization:

$$
a = p_1 p_2 \dots p_n

$$  
Then,

$$
a^2 = (p_1 p_2 \dots p_n)^2 = p_1^2 p_2^2 \dots p_n^2

$$  
If a prime $ p $ divides $ a^2 $, then $ p $ must appear in the prime factorization of $ a^2 $. By uniqueness of prime factorization, $ p $ must divide $ a $. Hence, proved.  

### 3.3 Proof of Theorem 1.3: $ \sqrt{2} $ is Irrational  
Assume, to the contrary, that $ \sqrt{2} $ is rational. Then, it can be written as:

$$
\sqrt{2} = \frac{r}{s}

$$  
where $ r $ and $ s $ are coprime integers ($ \gcd(r, s) = 1 $) and $ s \neq 0 $. Squaring both sides:

$$
2 = \frac{r^2}{s^2} \implies 2s^2 = r^2

$$  
From Theorem 1.2, since 2 divides $ r^2 $, it must divide $ r $. Let $ r = 2c $:

$$
2s^2 = (2c)^2 = 4c^2 \implies s^2 = 2c^2

$$  
Again, applying Theorem 1.2, 2 divides $ s $. Thus, both $ r $ and $ s $ are divisible by 2, contradicting the assumption that they are coprime. Hence, $ \sqrt{2} $ is irrational.  

### 3.4 Generalizing to Other Primes  
Using identical logic, we can prove that $ \sqrt{p} $ is irrational for any prime $ p $. Assume $ \sqrt{p} = \frac{r}{s} $, leading to $ p s^2 = r^2 $. By Theorem 1.2, $ p $ divides $ r $, implying $ r = pc $, and substituting yields $ p s^2 = p^2 c^2 \implies s^2 = p c^2 $. Again, $ p $ divides $ s $, contradicting coprimality. Thus, $ \sqrt{p} $ is irrational.

## 4. Solved Examples  

### Example 1: Prove $ \sqrt{3} $ is irrational  
**Solution:**  
Assume $ \sqrt{3} = \frac{r}{s} $, with $ \gcd(r, s) = 1 $. Squaring:

$$
3s^2 = r^2

$$  
By Theorem 1.2, 3 divides $ r $. Let $ r = 3c $:

$$
3s^2 = 9c^2 \implies s^2 = 3c^2

$$  
Thus, 3 divides $ s $, contradicting coprimality. Hence, $ \sqrt{3} $ is irrational.  

### Example 2: Prove $ \sqrt{5} $ is irrational  
**Solution:**  
Assume $ \sqrt{5} = \frac{r}{s} $, $ \gcd(r, s) = 1 $. Squaring:

$$
5s^2 = r^2

$$  
By Theorem 1.2, 5 divides $ r $. Let $ r = 5c $:

$$
5s^2 = 25c^2 \implies s^2 = 5c^2

$$  
Hence, 5 divides $ s $, contradicting coprimality. Therefore, $ \sqrt{5} $ is irrational.  

### Example 3: Is $ \sqrt{4} $ irrational?  
**Solution:**

$$
\sqrt{4} = 2 = \frac{2}{1}

$$  
Since it’s expressible as a fraction, $ \sqrt{4} $ is rational.  

### Example 4: Prove $ \sqrt{17} $ is irrational  
**Solution:**  
Assume $ \sqrt{17} = \frac{r}{s} $, $ \gcd(r, s) = 1 $. Squaring:

$$
17s^2 = r^2

$$  
By Theorem 1.2, 17 divides $ r $. Let $ r = 17c $:

$$
17s^2 = 289c^2 \implies s^2 = 17c^2

$$  
Thus, 17 divides $ s $, contradicting coprimality. Hence, $ \sqrt{17} $ is irrational.  

### Example 5: Why isn’t $ \sqrt{9} $ irrational?  
**Solution:**

$$
\sqrt{9} = 3 = \frac{3}{1}

$$  
It is rational because 9 is a perfect square.

## 5. Practice Problems  

### 🟢 Easy: Prove $ \sqrt{7} $ is irrational  
**Answer:**  
Assume $ \sqrt{7} = \frac{r}{s} $, $ \gcd(r, s) = 1 $. Squaring:

$$
7s^2 = r^2 \implies 7 \mid r \implies r = 7c \implies s^2 = 7c^2 \implies 7 \mid s

$$  
Contradiction; thus, $ \sqrt{7} $ is irrational.  

### 🟡 Medium: Prove $ \sqrt{11} $ is irrational  
**Answer:**  
Assume $ \sqrt{11} = \frac{r}{s} $, $ \gcd(r, s) = 1 $. Squaring:

$$
11s^2 = r^2 \implies 11 \mid r \implies r = 11c \implies s^2 = 11c^2 \implies 11 \mid s

$$  
Contradiction; hence, $ \sqrt{11} $ is irrational.  

### 🔴 Hard: Prove $ \sqrt{p} $ is irrational for prime $ p $  
**Answer:**  
Assume $ \sqrt{p} = \frac{r}{s} $, $ \gcd(r, s) = 1 $. Squaring:

$$
p s^2 = r^2 \implies p \mid r \implies r = p c \implies s^2 = p c^2 \implies p \mid s

$$  
Contradiction; therefore, $ \sqrt{p} $ is irrational.

## 6. Summary  
- **Irrational numbers** cannot be expressed as $ \frac{p}{q} $ with integers $ p $ and $ q $.  
- **Theorem 1.2**: If a prime $ p $ divides $ a^2 $, then $ p $ divides $ a $.  
- **Theorem 1.3**: $ \sqrt{2} $ is irrational via **proof by contradiction**.  
- The same method applies to prove $ \sqrt{p} $ is irrational for any prime $ p $.  
- Rational numbers are closed under addition/multiplication, while irrational numbers are not.

## 8. Student Corner  

### Common Mistakes  
- **Mistake**: Assuming all square roots are irrational.  
  **Correction**: Only square roots of **non-square** numbers are irrational (e.g., $ \sqrt{2} $, $ \sqrt{3} $), while perfect squares like $ \sqrt{4} $ are rational.  

- **Mistake**: Skipping the coprimality step in proofs.  
  **Correction**: Always assume $ \gcd(r, s) = 1 $ and show contradictions arising from divisibility.  

### Exam Tips  
- **Memorize the structure** of the proof for $ \sqrt{2} $, as similar methods apply to other primes.  
- **Highlight contradictions** explicitly in your proofs to score full marks.  

### Memory Aid  
Use the acronym **P.R.O.C.** to remember the steps in the proof:  
- **P**roblem: Assume $ \sqrt{p} $ is rational.  
- **R**epresent: $ \sqrt{p} = \frac{r}{s} $.  
- **O**perate: Derive $ p s^2 = r^2 $.  
- **C**ontradict: Find a common divisor, invalidating the initial assumption.
