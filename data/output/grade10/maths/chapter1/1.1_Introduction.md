## 3. Explanation  
### 3.1 Euclid’s Division Algorithm  
Euclid’s Division Algorithm states that for any two positive integers *a* and *b*, there exist unique integers *q* (quotient) and *r* (remainder) such that:

$$
a = bq + r \quad \text{where } 0 \leq r < b

$$  
This mirrors the long division process you’ve learned earlier. The algorithm is particularly useful for computing the **Highest Common Factor (HCF)** of two numbers.  

### 3.2 Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic  
This theorem asserts that every composite number greater than 1 can be expressed as a product of prime numbers in a **unique way**, disregarding the order of the primes. Mathematically, for any composite number *n*:

$$
n = p_1^{e_1} \cdot p_2^{e_2} \cdot \ldots \cdot p_k^{e_k}

$$  
where $p_1, p_2, ..., p_k $ are prime numbers and $ e_1, e_2, ..., e_k $ are positive integers.  

### 3.3 Applications  
- **Proving Irrationality**: The theorem helps demonstrate why numbers like √2, √3, and √5 are irrational.  
- **Decimal Expansions**: It explains whether a rational number $ \frac{p}{q} $ (where $ q ≠ 0 $) has a terminating or non-terminating repeating decimal expansion by examining the prime factorization of *q*.  

> **Important Theorem**: The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic guarantees the **uniqueness** of prime factorization, forming the backbone of number theory.

## 4. Solved Examples  
### Example 1: Using Euclid’s Algorithm  
Find the HCF of 48 and 18.  
**Solution**:  
1. Apply Euclid’s algorithm:

$$
   48 = 18 \times 2 + 12 \quad (\text{remainder } 12)

$$  
2. Now take 18 and 12:

$$
   18 = 12 \times 1 + 6 \quad (\text{remainder } 6)

$$  
3. Next, take 12 and 6:

$$
   12 = 6 \times 2 + 0 \quad (\text{remainder } 0)

$$  
Since the remainder is now 0, the HCF is **6**.  

### Example 2: Decimal Expansion Analysis  
Determine if $ \frac{13}{30} $ has a terminating or non-terminating decimal expansion.  
**Solution**:  
Factorize the denominator:

$$
30 = 2 \times 3 \times 5

$$  
The prime factors include 3, which is not 2 or 5. Hence, the decimal expansion is **non-terminating and repeating**.

## 5. Practice Problems  
🟢 **Easy**: Use Euclid’s algorithm to find the HCF of 56 and 12.  
🟡 **Medium**: Determine if $ \frac{17}{20} $ has a terminating decimal expansion.  
🔴 **Hard**: Prove that $ \sqrt{5} $ is irrational using the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic.  

**Answers**:  
1. **Easy**:

$$
   56 = 12 \times 4 + 8 \\
   12 = 8 \times 1 + 4 \\
   8 = 4 \times 2 + 0

$$  
   HCF = **4**.  

2. **Medium**:  
   Factorize 20:

$$
   20 = 2^2 \times 5

$$  
   Only primes 2 and 5 are present → **Terminating** decimal.  

3. **Hard**:  
   Assume $ \sqrt{5} = \frac{a}{b} $, where *a* and *b* are coprime integers. Squaring gives $ 5b^2 = a^2 $. By the Fundamental Theorem, 5 divides *a*, leading to contradictions. Thus, $ \sqrt{5} $ is irrational.
