## 1. Prerequisites  
Before diving into this topic, ensure you understand:  
- **Prime numbers**: Numbers greater than 1 divisible only by 1 and themselves (e.g., 2, 3, 5, 7).  
- **Factorization**: Breaking down a number into smaller numbers that multiply to form it (e.g., 12 = 2 × 6 or 3 × 4).  
- **Natural numbers**: Positive integers starting from 1 (1, 2, 3, ...).  

These concepts form the foundation for exploring how numbers can be uniquely expressed as products of primes.

## 2. Introduction  
Imagine you have a box containing different colored marbles. Each marble represents a prime number, and combining them creates new colors (numbers). Can you create *every* color using just these primes? This is the core idea behind the **Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic**, which tells us about the unique way numbers can be built from primes.  

In this section, you’ll learn:  
- How to break down natural numbers into prime factors.  
- Why prime factorization is unique (up to rearrangement of factors).  
- The historical significance of this theorem, attributed to Carl Friedrich Gauss.  

This builds on your existing knowledge of primes and connects to future topics like irrational numbers.  

---  

## 3. Explanation  

### 3.1 Prime Factorization  
Every **natural number greater than 1** can be expressed as a product of **prime numbers**. These primes are called the **prime factors** of the number.  
> **Definition**: The **prime factorization** of a number is its decomposition into prime numbers multiplied together. For example, 12 = 2 × 2 × 3.  

### 3.2 Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic  
> **Theorem**: Every composite number can be uniquely expressed as a product of prime numbers, disregarding the order of the factors.  
> **Proof Sketch**:  
1. **Existence**: Any number greater than 1 can be broken down into primes via repeated division by primes.  
2. **Uniqueness**: Assume two different prime factorizations exist for a number. By the **Euclidean algorithm**, we can show that one factor must divide the other, leading to a contradiction unless both factorizations are identical.  

### 3.3 Example: Factor Tree Method  
To find prime factors, use a **factor tree**:  
1. Divide the number by the smallest prime (starting with 2).  
2. Repeat the process for the quotient until all factors are prime.  
For example, factorizing 32760:

$$
32760 = 2 \times 16380 \\
= 2 \times 2 \times 8190 \\
= 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 4095 \\
= 2^3 \times 3 \times 1365 \\
= 2^3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 455 \\
= 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5 \times 91 \\
= 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5 \times 7 \times 13 \\

$$  
Thus, $ 32760 = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5 \times 7 \times 13 $.  

### 3.4 Historical Context  
The theorem’s roots trace back to Euclid’s *Elements* (Book IX, Proposition 14), but **Carl Friedrich Gauss** provided the first rigorous proof in *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae*. Gauss’s work laid the groundwork for modern number theory.

## 4. Solved Examples  

### **Example 1:**  
Find the prime factorization of 123456789.  
**Solution:**  
Start dividing by small primes:

$$
123456789 ÷ 3 = 41152263 \\
41152263 ÷ 3 = 13717421 \\

$$  
Check if 13717421 is prime. Testing divisibility by 7, 11, 13,... eventually confirms it’s prime. Thus:

$$
123456789 = 3^2 \times 3803 \times 3607

$$  

### **Example 2:**  
Verify the uniqueness of prime factorization for 60.  
**Solution:**  
Possible factorizations:  
- $ 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 5 $  
- $ 2 \times 3 \times 2 \times 5 $ (same primes, reordered)  
- $ 5 \times 2 \times 3 \times 2 $ (same primes, reordered)  
Since primes are unordered, all representations are equivalent.  

### **Example 3:**  
Factorize 222222 using a factor tree.  
**Solution:**

$$
222222 = 2 \times 111111 \\
111111 = 3 \times 37037 \\
37037 = 7 \times 5291 \\
5291 = 11 \times 481 \\
481 = 13 \times 37

$$  
Final factorization: $ 2 \times 3 \times 7 \times 11 \times 13 \times 37 $.  

### **Example 4:**  
Prove that 42 cannot be expressed as a product of primes other than 2, 3, and 7.  
**Solution:**  
Divide 42 by 2: $ 42 = 2 \times 21 $.  
Divide 21 by 3: $ 21 = 3 \times 7 $.  
Both 2, 3, and 7 are primes. No other primes divide 42.  

### **Example 5:**  
Use the theorem to resolve conflicting factorizations.  
**Solution:**  
Suppose someone claims $ 36 = 2^2 \times 3^2 $ and $ 36 = 3^2 \times 2^2 $. Both are valid because multiplication is commutative. The theorem guarantees the **set** of prime factors is the same, regardless of order.

## 5. Practice Problems  

### 🟢 Easy  
1. Find the prime factors of 72.  
2. Verify that 105 = 3 × 5 × 7.  
3. Express 256 as a power of a prime.  

### 🟡 Medium  
4. Prove that 120 = 2³ × 3 × 5 is the unique prime factorization.  
5. Factorize 987654 using the factor tree method.  
6. Show that 1001 = 7 × 11 × 13.  

### 🔴 Hard  
7. Explain why the number 1 is excluded from the theorem.  
8. Suppose a number N has prime factors p₁, p₂, ..., pₙ. Prove that any other factorization of N must consist of the same primes.  
9. Use the theorem to argue that there are infinitely many primes.  

**Answers:**  
1. $ 72 = 2^3 \times 3^2 $.  
2. $ 105 = 3 \times 5 \times 7 $.  
3. $ 256 = 2^8 $.  
4. Divide 120 by 2 repeatedly: $ 120 = 2^3 \times 3 \times 5 $. No other combination works.  
5. $ 987654 = 2 \times 3 \times 7 \times 11 \times 13 \times 37 $.  
6. $ 1001 ÷ 7 = 143 $; $ 143 ÷ 11 = 13 $. All factors are prime.  
7. The theorem applies to numbers >1. Since 1 has no prime factors, it’s excluded.  
8. Assume two factorizations exist. By the Euclidean algorithm, primes must match.  
9. If there were finitely many primes, their product +1 would be a new prime, contradicting finiteness.

## 6. Summary  
- **Prime factorization** breaks numbers into primes (e.g., 60 = 2² × 3 × 5).  
- The **Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic** states that this factorization is unique (ignoring order).  
- **Examples**:  
  - $ 32760 = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5 \times 7 \times 13 $  
  - $ 123456789 = 3^2 \times 3803 \times 3607 $  
- **Historical Note**: Gauss formalized the theorem in the 19th century.

## 7. Key Formulas  
| Formula | Description |  
|--------|-------------|  
| $ n = p_1^{k_1} \times p_2^{k_2} \times \dots \times p_m^{k_m} $ | Prime factorization of a number $ n $. |  
| $ \text{If } a \mid b \text{ and } b \mid c, \text{ then } a \mid c $ | Divisibility property. |  
| $ \text{Unique factorization: } n = \prod_{i=1}^m p_i^{k_i} $ | Uniqueness up to ordering of primes. |

## 8. Student Corner  

### **Common Mistakes**  
- ❌ **Error**: Missing a prime factor (e.g., forgetting 3 in 12 = 2 × 6).  
  ✅ **Fix**: Always check divisibility by primes up to $ \sqrt{n} $.  
- ❌ **Error**: Confusing prime and composite numbers (e.g., thinking 1 is prime).  
  ✅ **Fix**: Remember 1 is neither prime nor composite.  

### **Exam Tips**  
- **Tip 1**: Practice factor trees systematically to avoid missing primes.  
- **Tip 2**: For difficult numbers, test divisibility by small primes first (2, 3, 5, 7).  

### **Memory Aid**  
**"Prime Factors Are Unique!"**  
Recall the theorem with the phrase: *"Primes are always the building blocks, and they never change!"*
