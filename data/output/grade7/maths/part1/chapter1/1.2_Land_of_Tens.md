## 3. Explanation  

### 3.1 Special Calculators in the Land of Tens  
In this chapter, we encounter three unique calculators:  
- **Thoughtful Thousands**: Only has a **+1000** button.  
- **Tedious Tens**: Only has a **+10** button.  
- **Handy Hundreds**: Only has a **+100** button.  

Each calculator uses its single button repeatedly to build numbers. For example:  
- Pressing **+1000** three times gives $1000 \times 3 = 3000 $.  
- Pressing **+10** fifty times gives $ 10 \times 50 = 500 $.  

> **Definition**: A **button press** refers to activating a button once, and the total number of presses determines the resulting number.  

### 3.2 Building Numbers with Repeated Addition  
When a calculator adds the same number repeatedly, it’s essentially performing **multiplication**. For example:  
- To make 5000 using the **+1000** button:

$$
  1000 \times x = 5000 \quad \Rightarrow \quad x = \frac{5000}{1000} = 5 \text{ presses}.

$$  

Similarly, using the **+10** button:  
- To make 700:

$$
  10 \times x = 700 \quad \Rightarrow \quad x = \frac{700}{10} = 70 \text{ presses}.

$$  

> **Property**: The number of button presses needed to form a number is equal to the number divided by the button’s value.  

### 3.3 Creative Chitti: Exploring Multiple Strategies  
The **Creative Chitti** calculator has buttons for all powers of ten (**+1**, **+10**, **+100**, ..., **+1,000,000**). It allows multiple ways to build the same number. For instance:  
- To make **321**:  
  - $32 \times 10 + 1 = 320 + 1 = 321 $  
  - $ 2 \times 100 + 12 \times 10 + 1 = 200 + 120 + 1 = 321 $  

> **Theorem**: Any number can be expressed as a sum of multiples of powers of ten.  

#### Proof:  
Take a number $ N $. Break it into its digits:

$$
N = d_n \times 10^n + d_{n-1} \times 10^{n-1} + \dots + d_1 \times 10 + d_0,

$$  
where $d_i $ represents the digit at position $ i $. This is the standard base-10 representation, confirming that multiple strategies exist to form $ N $.

## 4. Solved Examples  

### Example 1: Thoughtful Thousands Button  
**Question**: How many times should the **+1000** button be pressed to show **90,000**?  

**Solution**:  
We divide 90,000 by 1000:

$$
\frac{90,\!000}{1000} = 90 \text{ presses}.

$$  
Thus, **90 presses** are needed.  

### Example 2: Tedious Tens Button  
**Question**: How many times should the **+10** button be pressed to show **3700**?  

**Solution**:  
Divide 3700 by 10:

$$
\frac{3700}{10} = 370 \text{ presses}.

$$  
So, **370 presses** are required.  

### Example 3: Handy Hundreds Button  
**Question**: How many times should the **+100** button be pressed to show **1,00,000**?  

**Solution**:  
Divide 1,00,000 by 100:

$$
\frac{1,\!00,\!000}{100} = 1000 \text{ presses}.

$$  
Answer: **1000 presses**.  

### Example 4: Creative Chitti Strategy  
**Question**: Find a different way to express **5072** using the buttons **+1000**, **+100**, and **+1**.  

**Solution**:  
Start with the largest power of ten less than 5072:  
- $5 \times 1000 = 5000 $  
- Remaining: $ 5072 - 5000 = 72 $  
- $ 72 = 7 \times 10 + 2 \times 1 $  

Thus, the expression is:

$$
(5 \times 1000) + (7 \times 10) + (2 \times 1) = 5072.

$$  

### Example 5: Maximum Number with 30 Presses  
**Question**: Using exactly 30 button presses, what is the largest and smallest 3-digit number you can make?  

**Solution**:  
- **Largest number**: Use the highest-value button (**+1000**) as much as possible. However, since we need a 3-digit number, use **+100** instead:

$$
  30 \times 100 = 3000 \quad (\text{Too big!})

$$  
  Adjust to use **+100** for 29 presses and **+1** for 1 press:

$$
  29 \times 100 + 1 = 2900 + 1 = 2901 \quad (\text{Still 4 digits!})

$$  
  Instead, use **+100** for 9 presses and **+10** for 21 presses:

$$
  9 \times 100 + 21 \times 10 = 900 + 210 = 1110 \quad (\text{Still 4 digits!})

$$  
  Finally, use **+10** for 30 presses:

$$
  30 \times 10 = 300 \quad (\text{Valid 3-digit number}).

$$  
  So, **largest is 300**.  

- **Smallest number**: Use **+1** for all 30 presses:

$$
  30 \times 1 = 30 \quad (\text{Valid 2-digit number}).

$$  
  Wait! We need a 3-digit number. Use **+100** once and **+1** 29 times:

$$
  1 \times 100 + 29 \times 1 = 100 + 29 = 129.

$$  
  Answer: **Smallest is 129**.

## 6. Summary  
- **Button presses** are used to build numbers by adding the same value repeatedly.  
- The number of presses equals the target number divided by the button’s value.  
- Numbers can be represented in multiple ways using different combinations of buttons.  
- **Creative Chitti** demonstrates that flexibility exists in forming numbers.  
- Larger numbers require more presses, especially for smaller buttons like **+10**.  
- Understanding place value is essential for interpreting these strategies.

## 7. Key Formulas  
| Formula | Description |  
|--------|-------------|  
| $ \text{Presses} = \frac{\text{Target Number}}{\text{Button Value}} $ | Calculates the number of presses needed. |  
| $ N = d_n \times 10^n + d_{n-1} \times 10^{n-1} + \dots + d_0 $ | Standard base-10 representation of a number. |  
| $ \text{Total Buttons Used} = \sum (\text{Number of Presses} \times \text{Button Value}) $ | Sum of contributions from each button press. |

## 8. Student Corner  

### Common Mistakes  
- **Mistake**: Confusing the number of presses for **+1000** with **+100**.  
  **Correction**: Always divide the target number by the button’s value (e.g., $ 10,\!000 / 1000 = 10 $).  

- **Mistake**: Not accounting for all digits in a number (e.g., missing zeros).  
  **Correction**: Break the number into its place values systematically.  

### Exam Tips  
- Practice converting numbers into expressions using different button combinations.  
- Verify your answers by reversing the calculation (e.g., multiply presses by button value).
