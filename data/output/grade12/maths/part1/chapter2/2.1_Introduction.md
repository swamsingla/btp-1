## 2. Introduction  
Inverse trigonometric functions are essential tools in mathematics, enabling us to reverse operations performed by trigonometric functions. However, most trigonometric functions are not one-one or onto over their entire domains, making their inverses undefined. This chapter explores how restricting domains and ranges can restore invertibility, while also highlighting their significance in calculus, physics, and engineering. By the end of this section, you’ll understand the foundational concepts behind inverse trigonometric functions and their practical relevance.  

---  

## 3. Explanation  
### 3.1 Invertibility of Functions  
A function $ f: A \to B $ is invertible if and only if it is **one-one** (injective) and **onto** (surjective).  
> **Definition**: A function $ f $ is **one-one** if $ f(a_1) = f(a_2) \implies a_1 = a_2 $ for all $ a_1, a_2 \in A $. It is **onto** if for every $ b \in B $, there exists an $ a \in A $ such that $ f(a) = b $.  

Trigonometric functions like $ \sin x $, $ \cos x $, and $ \tan x $ fail to satisfy these conditions over their natural domains. For example:  
- $ \sin x $ is periodic and not injective over $ \mathbb{R} $.  
- $ \cos x $ is neither injective nor surjective over $ \mathbb{R} $ unless restricted.  

To define inverses, domains and ranges must be appropriately constrained.  

### 3.2 Domain and Range Restrictions  
For a trigonometric function to have an inverse, its domain must be limited to an interval where it is one-one, and its range must match the codomain of the inverse function.  
> **Property**: The domain of $ \arcsin x $ is $ [-1, 1] $, and its range is $ \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] $, ensuring it is bijective.  
> **Property**: The domain of $ \arccos x $ is $ [-1, 1] $, and its range is $ [0, \pi] $, guaranteeing bijectivity.  

These restrictions allow the inverse functions to exist and behave predictably. Graphically, the inverse of a function is obtained by reflecting the original function over the line $ y = x $.  

### 3.3 Applications of Inverse Trigonometric Functions  
Inverse trigonometric functions are crucial in:  
- Calculus: Solving integrals involving trigonometric expressions.  
- Science and Engineering: Modeling relationships between angles and distances in physical systems.  

They also serve as building blocks for more complex mathematical models and algorithms.

## 4. Solved Examples  
### Example 1: Determining Invertibility  
Determine whether $ f(x) = \sin x $ is invertible over its natural domain.  
**Solution**:  
- $ \sin x $ is periodic with period $ 2\pi $, meaning multiple values of $ x $ yield the same output. For example, $ \sin(0) = \sin(\pi) = 0 $.  
- Since $ \sin x $ is not one-one over $ \mathbb{R} $, it fails the invertibility criterion.  
**Answer**: $ f(x) = \sin x $ is not invertible over its natural domain.  

### Example 2: Restricting Domain for Invertibility  
Find the domain restriction for $ \cos x $ to make it invertible.  
**Solution**:  
- The range of $ \cos x $ is $ [-1, 1] $. To ensure bijectivity, restrict the domain to $ [0, \pi] $, where $ \cos x $ is decreasing and covers all values in $ [-1, 1] $.  
**Answer**: The restricted domain is $ [0, \pi] $, and the inverse function is $ \arccos x $.  

### Example 3: Graphical Interpretation  
Sketch the graph of $ y = \arcsin x $ and describe its behavior.  
**Solution**:  
- The domain of $ \arcsin x $ is $ [-1, 1] $, and the range is $ \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] $.  
- The graph passes through $ (-1, -\frac{\pi}{2}) $, $ (0, 0) $, and $ (1, \frac{\pi}{2}) $.  
- It is increasing and continuous over its domain.  
**Answer**: See the described graph and behavior.

## 5. Practice Problems  
🟢 **Easy**: Determine whether $ f(x) = \tan x $ is invertible over its natural domain.  
🟡 **Medium**: Find the domain restriction for $ \sin x $ to ensure invertibility.  
🔴 **Hard**: Solve $ \arcsin(x) + \arccos(x) = \frac{\pi}{2} $ and verify the result.  

**Answers**:  
**Easy**:  
- $ \tan x $ is periodic and not one-one over $ \mathbb{R} $, so it is not invertible.  

**Medium**:  
- Restrict the domain of $ \sin x $ to $ \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] $, where it is bijective.  

**Hard**:  
- The identity $ \arcsin(x) + \arccos(x) = \frac{\pi}{2} $ holds for all $ x \in [-1, 1] $. Verification involves substituting $ x = 0 $, $ x = 1 $, and using derivative properties.

## 6. Summary  
- Inverse functions require the original function to be one-one and onto.  
- Trigonometric functions are inherently non-invertible over their full domains due to periodicity and lack of surjectivity.  
- Domain and range restrictions are critical to define valid inverse trigonometric functions.  
- Inverse trigonometric functions are indispensable in calculus and applied sciences.

## 8. Student Corner  
**Common Mistakes**:  
- Confusing one-one with onto; remember both are required for invertibility.  
- Misapplying domain restrictions (e.g., using $ [0, \pi] $ for arcsin instead of $ [-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}] $).  

**Exam Tip**: Always sketch graphs of inverse trigonometric functions to visualize their domains and ranges.
