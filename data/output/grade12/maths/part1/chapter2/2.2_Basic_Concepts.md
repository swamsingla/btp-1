## 1. Prerequisites  
Before diving into inverse trigonometric functions, students should be familiar with:  
- **Functions and their properties**: Understanding of injective (one-to-one), surjective (onto), and bijective mappings.  
- **Domain and range**: Ability to identify the domain and range of standard trigonometric functions like sine, cosine, and tangent.  
- **Inverse functions**: Knowledge of how to determine the inverse of a function, including the requirement for the function to be bijective.  

These foundational concepts are critical for grasping how inverse trigonometric functions are defined and applied.  

---  

## 2. Introduction  
Why do we need inverse trigonometric functions? Imagine trying to find an angle whose sine is 0.5—this is a classic problem where traditional trigonometric functions fall short. While trigonometric functions map angles to values, their inverses allow us to reverse this process. In this topic, we explore how to define inverse trigonometric functions by restricting the domains of standard trigonometric functions to ensure they are bijective. This enables us to assign unique outputs to inputs, making the inverse operations valid. By the end of this section, you’ll understand how to define inverse sine, cosine, and tangent functions, along with their principal value branches.  

---  

## 3. Explanation  

### 3.1 Definition of Inverse Trigonometric Functions  
> **An inverse trigonometric function** is a function that reverses the operation of a trigonometric function. To define such a function, the original trigonometric function must be **bijective**, meaning it is both **injective (one-to-one)** and **surjective (onto)**.  

For example, consider the sine function:  
- **Original function**: $ f: \mathbb{R} \rightarrow [-1, 1] $  
- However, $ f $ is **not injective** over $ \mathbb{R} $, as $ \sin(\theta) = \sin(\theta + 2\pi) $.  

To make it bijective, we **restrict its domain** to an interval where it is one-to-one and covers the entire range $ [-1, 1] $. The most commonly used interval is $ \left[ -\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2} \right] $, which is called the **principal value branch**.  

> **Definition**: The inverse sine function $ \sin^{-1}: [-1, 1] \rightarrow \left[ -\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2} \right] $ is defined such that $ y = \sin^{-1}(x) $ implies $ \sin(y) = x $.  

### 3.2 Domain and Range Restrictions  
> **Principal Value Branch**: The inverse trigonometric functions are defined with specific domain and range restrictions to ensure uniqueness.  
- **Sine Function**:  
  - Domain: $ [-1, 1] $  
  - Range: $ \left[ -\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2} \right] $  
- **Cosine Function**:  
  - Domain: $ [-1, 1] $  
  - Range: $ [0, \pi] $  
- **Tangent Function**:  
  - Domain: $ (-1, 1) $  
  - Range: $ \left( -\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2} \right) $  

These restrictions ensure that each input maps to exactly one output, satisfying the criteria for an inverse function.  

### 3.3 Properties of Inverse Functions  
> **Property 1**: If $ y = \sin^{-1}(x) $, then $ \sin(y) = x $, and $ y \in \left[ -\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2} \right] $.  
> **Property 2**: $ \sin(\sin^{-1}(x)) = x $ for $ x \in [-1, 1] $.  
> **Property 3**: $ \sin^{-1}(\sin(x)) = x $ **only if** $ x \in \left[ -\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2} \right] $. Otherwise, adjustments are needed to fit the principal value range.  

### 3.4 Graphical Interpretation  
> **Graph of $ \sin^{-1}(x) $**: The graph of the inverse sine function is the reflection of the sine function’s graph over the line $ y = x $. It passes through key points like $ (-1, -\frac{\pi}{2}) $, $ (0, 0) $, and $ (1, \frac{\pi}{2}) $.  

This symmetry between a function and its inverse is a fundamental concept in mathematics.

## 4. Solved Examples  

### Example 1:  
**Evaluate $ \sin^{-1}\left( \frac{1}{2} \right) $.**  
**Solution**:  
We know that $ \sin\left( \frac{\pi}{6} \right) = \frac{1}{2} $, and $ \frac{\pi}{6} $ lies within the principal value range $ \left[ -\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2} \right] $.  
Hence, $ \sin^{-1}\left( \frac{1}{2} \right) = \frac{\pi}{6} $.  

### Example 2:  
**Find $ \sin\left( \sin^{-1}\left( \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \right) \right) $.**  
**Solution**:  
By **Property 2**, $ \sin(\sin^{-1}(x)) = x $ for $ x \in [-1, 1] $. Since $ \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 0.707 $ is within the valid range:

$$
\sin\left( \sin^{-1}\left( \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \right) \right) = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}

$$  

### Example 3:  
**Simplify $ \sin^{-1}\left( \sin\left( \frac{5\pi}{6} \right) \right) $.**  
**Solution**:  
First compute $ \sin\left( \frac{5\pi}{6} \right) = \frac{1}{2} $. Now, $ \frac{5\pi}{6} $ is outside the principal value range $ \left[ -\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2} \right] $. The equivalent angle within the range is $ \frac{\pi}{6} $, since $ \sin\left( \frac{\pi}{6} \right) = \frac{1}{2} $.  
Thus:

$$
\sin^{-1}\left( \sin\left( \frac{5\pi}{6} \right) \right) = \frac{\pi}{6}

$$  

### Example 4:  
**Prove that $ \sin^{-1}(x) + \cos^{-1}(x) = \frac{\pi}{2} $ for $ x \in [-1, 1] $.**  
**Solution**:  
Let $ \theta = \sin^{-1}(x) $. Then $ \sin(\theta) = x $, and $ \theta \in \left[ -\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2} \right] $.  
We know that $ \cos\left( \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta \right) = \sin(\theta) = x $, so:

$$
\cos^{-1}(x) = \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta = \frac{\pi}{2} - \sin^{-1}(x)

$$  
Adding $ \sin^{-1}(x) $ to both sides:

$$
\sin^{-1}(x) + \cos^{-1}(x) = \frac{\pi}{2}

$$  
This identity holds for all $ x \in [-1, 1] $.  

### Example 5:  
**Find the domain of $ f(x) = \tan^{-1}(x) $.**  
**Solution**:  
The domain of $ \tan^{-1}(x) $ is all real numbers, since $ \tan(\theta) $ takes all real values as $ \theta $ varies over $ \left( -\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2} \right) $. Therefore:

$$
\text{Domain of } f(x) = \mathbb{R}

$$

## 5. Practice Problems  

### 🟢 Easy  
**Problem 1**: Evaluate $ \cos^{-1}\left( \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \right) $.  
**Problem 2**: Simplify $ \cos\left( \cos^{-1}\left( \frac{1}{2} \right) \right) $.  

### 🟡 Medium  
**Problem 3**: Find $ \sin^{-1}\left( \sin\left( \frac{3\pi}{4} \right) \right) $.  
**Problem 4**: Prove that $ \tan^{-1}(x) + \cot^{-1}(x) = \frac{\pi}{2} $ for $ x > 0 $.  

### 🔴 Hard  
**Problem 5**: Solve for $ x $ in $ \sin^{-1}(x) + \sin^{-1}(1 - x) = \frac{\pi}{2} $.  

---  

**Answers:**  

**Problem 1**:

$$
\cos^{-1}\left( \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \right) = \frac{\pi}{6}

$$  
**Reasoning**: $ \cos\left( \frac{\pi}{6} \right) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} $, and $ \frac{\pi}{6} \in [0, \pi] $.  

**Problem 2**:

$$
\cos\left( \cos^{-1}\left( \frac{1}{2} \right) \right) = \frac{1}{2}

$$  
**Reasoning**: Apply **Property 2** directly.  

**Problem 3**:

$$
\sin^{-1}\left( \sin\left( \frac{3\pi}{4} \right) \right) = \frac{\pi}{4}

$$  
**Reasoning**: $ \sin\left( \frac{3\pi}{4} \right) = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} $, but $ \frac{3\pi}{4} \notin \left[ -\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2} \right] $. The equivalent angle is $ \frac{\pi}{4} $.  

**Problem 4**:  
**Proof**: Let $ \theta = \tan^{-1}(x) $. Then $ \tan(\theta) = x $, and $ \theta \in \left( -\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2} \right) $.  
We know $ \cot\left( \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta \right) = \tan(\theta) = x $, so:

$$
\cot^{-1}(x) = \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta = \frac{\pi}{2} - \tan^{-1}(x)

$$  
Adding $ \tan^{-1}(x) $ to both sides:

$$
\tan^{-1}(x) + \cot^{-1}(x) = \frac{\pi}{2}

$$  
**Note**: Valid for $ x > 0 $, as $ \cot^{-1}(x) $ is defined for $ x > 0 $.  

**Problem 5**:  
**Solution**:  
Let $ \alpha = \sin^{-1}(x) $, $ \beta = \sin^{-1}(1 - x) $. Given $ \alpha + \beta = \frac{\pi}{2} $, so $ \beta = \frac{\pi}{2} - \alpha $.  
Take sine of both sides:

$$
\sin(\beta) = \sin\left( \frac{\pi}{2} - \alpha \right) = \cos(\alpha)

$$  
But $ \sin(\beta) = 1 - x $, and $ \cos(\alpha) = \sqrt{1 - x^2} $ (since $ \alpha \in [-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}] $).  
Thus:

$$
1 - x = \sqrt{1 - x^2}

$$  
Square both sides:

$$
(1 - x)^2 = 1 - x^2 \implies 1 - 2x + x^2 = 1 - x^2 \implies -2x + x^2 = -x^2 \implies 2x^2 - 2x = 0 \implies 2x(x - 1) = 0

$$  
So $ x = 0 $ or $ x = 1 $. Check validity:  
- If $ x = 0 $, then $ \sin^{-1}(0) + \sin^{-1}(1) = 0 + \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2} $. ✔️  
- If $ x = 1 $, then $ \sin^{-1}(1) + \sin^{-1}(0) = \frac{\pi}{2} + 0 = \frac{\pi}{2} $. ✔️  

**Final Answer**: $ x = 0 $ or $ x = 1 $.

## 6. Summary  
- **Inverse trigonometric functions** are defined by restricting the domains of standard trigonometric functions to make them bijective.  
- The **principal value branch** ensures unique outputs for each input, with specific domain-range pairs:  
  - $ \sin^{-1}: [-1, 1] \rightarrow \left[ -\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2} \right] $  
  - $ \cos^{-1}: [-1, 1] \rightarrow [0, \pi] $  
  - $ \tan^{-1}: \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \left( -\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2} \right) $  
- Key properties include:  
  - $ \sin(\sin^{-1}(x)) = x $ for $ x \in [-1, 1] $.  
  - $ \sin^{-1}(\sin(x)) = x $ **only if** $ x \in \left[ -\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2} \right] $.  
- These functions are critical for solving equations involving trigonometric relationships.

| Formula | Description |

## 8. Student Corner  

### Common Mistakes  
- ❌ **Confusing domain and range**: Students often mix up the domain of the inverse function and the range of the original function.  
  ✅ **Correction**: Always recall that the domain of $ \sin^{-1}(x) $ is $ [-1, 1] $, while its range is $ \left[ -\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2} \right] $.  

- ❌ **Ignoring principal value constraints**: Forgetting to adjust angles to lie within the principal value range leads to incorrect results.  
  ✅ **Correction**: Use identities like $ \sin^{-1}(\sin(x)) = x $ **only if** $ x \in \left[ -\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2} \right] $. Adjust angles accordingly.  

### Exam Tips  
- 🔍 **Memorize principal value ranges**: Quick recall of ranges for inverse trigonometric functions saves time during exams.  
- 🧠 **Use identities strategically**: Combine properties like $ \sin^{-1}(x) + \cos^{-1}(x) = \frac{\pi}{2} $ to simplify complex expressions.  

### Memory Aid  
**"Sin Cos Tan, Pi Over Two!"**  
Remember the principal value ranges:  
- $ \sin^{-1} $: $ -\frac{\pi}{2} $ to $ \frac{\pi}{2} $  
- $ \cos^{-1} $: $ 0 $ to $ \pi $  
- $ \tan^{-1} $: $ -\frac{\pi}{2} $ to $ \frac{\pi}{2} $
