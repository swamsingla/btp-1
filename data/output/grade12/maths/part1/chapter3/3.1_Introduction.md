## 3. Explanation  

### 3.1 Historical Context and Purpose  
> **"The evolution of the concept of matrices is the result of an attempt to obtain compact and simple methods of solving systems of linear equations."**  
Matrices emerged as a response to the need for streamlined solutions to complex systems of equations. Before matrices, solving multiple equations manually was cumbersome. By organizing coefficients and constants into rectangular arrays, matrices allow systematic manipulation, reducing computational effort.  

### 3.2 Matrix Definition and Applications  
> **"Matrices are not only used as a representation of the coefficients in systems of linear equations, but their utility extends far beyond that use."**  
While matrices are often associated with solving equations, their applications span diverse domains:  
- **Business and Science**: Electronic spreadsheets use matrix operations for budgeting, forecasting, and cost analysis.  
- **Physics and Engineering**: Transformations like rotation, scaling, and reflection are modeled using matrices.  
- **Cryptography**: Matrices encode and decode messages securely.  
- **Social Sciences**: Researchers analyze data patterns in economics, sociology, and psychology through matrices.  

These applications highlight why matrices are considered a universal tool in mathematics and its interdisciplinary applications.  

### 3.3 Core Concept: Structure of a Matrix  
Although the source text does not provide a formal definition of a matrix, the chapter introduces its fundamental structure implicitly. A matrix is essentially a grid of numbers arranged in rows and columns, enabling operations that simplify large-scale computations. This structure is pivotal for subsequent topics like matrix addition, multiplication, and transposition.

## 4. Solved Examples  

### Example 1: Representing Data in a Matrix  
**Problem**: A company tracks monthly sales data for three products across four regions. Represent this data as a matrix.  

**Solution**:  
Let the products be labeled $ P_1, P_2, P_3 $ and the regions $ R_1, R_2, R_3, R_4 $. The sales data (in units) is:  
- $ P_1 $: 100, 150, 200, 120  
- $ P_2 $: 80, 90, 110, 100  
- $ P_3 $: 60, 70, 80, 90  

The corresponding matrix is:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
100 & 150 & 200 & 120 \\
80 & 90 & 110 & 100 \\
60 & 70 & 80 & 90 \\
\end{bmatrix}

$$  
Each row corresponds to a product, and each column represents a region.  

---

### Example 2: Solving a System of Equations  
**Problem**: Solve the system:

$$
\begin{cases}
2x + y = 5 \\  
x - 3y = -2  
\end{cases}

$$  
Using matrix representation.  

**Solution**:  
Express the system as $ AX = B $, where:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad X = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}

$$  
To solve, compute $ X = A^{-1}B $. However, since this involves inverse matrices (covered in later sections), we’ll focus on setting up the equation correctly here.

### Example 4: Translating Physical Transformations  
**Problem**: Describe how a rotation of 90 degrees clockwise can be represented as a matrix.  

**Solution**:  
A 90° clockwise rotation transforms coordinates $(x, y) $ to $ (y, -x) $. The transformation matrix is:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}

$$  
Applying this matrix to a vector $ \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} $ yields $ \begin{bmatrix} y \\ -x \end{bmatrix} $, confirming the transformation.

### Example 5: Analyzing Experimental Results  
**Problem**: A scientist records temperature changes over time in three locations. Organize the data into a matrix.  

**Solution**:  
Assume temperatures (in °C) at times $ t_1, t_2, t_3 $ are:  
- Location A: 20, 22, 24  
- Location B: 25, 27, 29  
- Location C: 30, 32, 34  

The matrix is:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
20 & 22 & 24 \\
25 & 27 & 29 \\
30 & 32 & 34 \\
\end{bmatrix}

$$  
Rows represent locations, and columns represent time intervals.
