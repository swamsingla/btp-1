## 3. Explanation  

### 3.1 Powers of 10  
Powers of 10 are numbers like $ 10^0 = 1 $, $ 10^1 = 10 $, $ 10^2 = 100 $, and so on. They are used to represent numbers in a compact form. For example:  
- $ 10^3 = 1000 $ (three zeros)  
- $ 10^{-1} = 0.1 $ (one-tenth)  
- $ 10^{-3} = 0.001 $ (one-thousandth)  

> **Definition**: A power of 10 is a number obtained by multiplying 10 by itself a certain number of times. It can be positive (for large numbers) or negative (for small numbers).  

### 3.2 Scientific Notation  
Scientific notation expresses numbers as $ x \times 10^n $, where $ 1 \leq x < 10 $ and $ n $ is an integer. This is useful for simplifying large or small numbers.  

> **Definition**: Scientific notation is a way to write numbers as the product of a number between 1 and 10 and a power of 10.  

#### Example from Source Text  
The number **5900** can be rewritten in multiple ways using powers of 10:

$$
5900 = 59 \times 10^2 = 5.9 \times 10^3

$$  
Here, $5.9 $ is the coefficient (between 1 and 10), and $ 3 $ is the exponent.  

### 3.3 Converting Decimals to Powers of 10  
Decimals like **561.903** can also be broken down using powers of 10:

$$
561.903 = (5 \times 10^2) + (6 \times 10^1) + (1 \times 10^0) + (9 \times 10^{-1}) + (0 \times 10^{-2}) + (3 \times 10^{-3})

$$  
Each digit corresponds to a specific power of 10, depending on its position.  

### 3.4 Large Numbers in Scientific Notation  
Large numbers like the distance to the Sun (**30,00,00,00,00,00,00,00,00,000 m**) are simplified using scientific notation:

$$
30,00,00,00,00,00,00,00,00,000 = 3 \times 10^{21}

$$  
Here, the coefficient is $3 $, and the exponent $ 21 $ tells us how many places the decimal point was moved.  

### 3.5 Importance of Exponents  
The exponent in scientific notation often carries more weight than the coefficient. For instance:  
- Changing the exponent from $ 3 $ to $ 4 $ in $ 5.9 \times 10^3 $ increases the number by **10 times** (to $ 5.9 \times 10^4 $).  
- Changing the coefficient from $ 5.9 $ to $ 2.08 $ in $ 2.08 \times 10^4 $ changes the number by only **less than half**.  

> **Tip**: Always check whether the exponent reflects the scale of the number, not just the magnitude of the coefficient.

## 4. Solved Examples  

### Example 1: Express 172 Using Powers of 10  
**Solution**:  
Break down 172 into its place values:

$$
172 = (1 \times 10^2) + (7 \times 10^1) + (2 \times 10^0)

$$  
So, $172 = 1 \times 10^2 + 7 \times 10^1 + 2 \times 10^0 $.  

### Example 2: Convert 561.903 to Powers of 10  
**Solution**:  
Identify each digit’s place value:

$$
561.903 = (5 \times 10^2) + (6 \times 10^1) + (1 \times 10^0) + (9 \times 10^{-1}) + (0 \times 10^{-2}) + (3 \times 10^{-3})

$$  
This shows how decimals work with negative exponents.  

### Example 3: Rewrite 5900 in Scientific Notation  
**Solution**:  
Move the decimal point three places left to get a number between 1 and 10:

$$
5900 = 5.9 \times 10^3

$$  
Here, $5.9 $ is the coefficient, and $ 3 $ is the exponent.  

### Example 4: Express 80,00,000 in Scientific Notation  
**Solution**:  
Move the decimal point seven places left:

$$
80,00,000 = 8 \times 10^6

$$  
This highlights how the exponent determines the scale.  

### Example 5: Compare Exponents vs. Coefficients  
**Solution**:  
Consider $2 \times 10^7 $ (Mumbai’s population):  
- If the exponent increases by 1 ($ 2 \times 10^8 $), the population becomes **10 times larger** (20 crores).  
- If the coefficient increases by 1 ($ 3 \times 10^7 $), the population grows by **only 50%** (1.5 crores).

## 6. Summary  
- **Powers of 10** allow us to write large or small numbers concisely.  
- **Scientific Notation** takes the form $ x \times 10^n $, where $ 1 \leq x < 10 $.  
- **Positive exponents** represent large numbers; **negative exponents** represent small numbers.  
- The **exponent** is crucial as it defines the number’s scale.  
- **Examples**:

$$
  5900 = 5.9 \times 10^3,\quad 0.001 = 1 \times 10^{-3},\quad 561.903 = 5 \times 10^2 + 6 \times 10^1 + \dots

$$  

---  

## 7. Key Formulas  
| Formula | Description |  
|--------|-------------|  
| $10^n $ | Represents 1 followed by $ n $ zeros (e.g., $ 10^3 = 1000 $) |  
| $ 10^{-n} $ | Represents $ 1/(10^n) $ (e.g., $ 10^{-3} = 0.001 $) |  
| $ x \times 10^n $ | Scientific notation, where $ 1 \leq x < 10 $ |  
| $ N = a \times 10^n $ | Any number $ N $ can be expressed as a product of a coefficient $ a $ and a power of 10 |

## 8. Student Corner  

### Common Mistakes  
- ❌ **Incorrect Placement of Decimal**: Forgetting to move the decimal point enough places.  
  ✅ Fix: Count the number of digits moved to determine the exponent.  
- ❌ **Mixing Up Positive/Negative Exponents**: Confusing $ 10^3 $ with $ 10^{-3} $.  
  ✅ Fix: Remember $ 10^3 = 1000 $, while $ 10^{-3} = 0.001 $.  

### Exam Tips  
- Focus on the **significance of the exponent**, as it defines the number’s scale.  
- Practice converting numbers repeatedly to build confidence.  

### Memory Aid  
**"Big numbers go up, small ones go down"**: Use $+$ for large numbers ($ 10^3 $) and $-$ for small numbers ($ 10^{-3} $).
