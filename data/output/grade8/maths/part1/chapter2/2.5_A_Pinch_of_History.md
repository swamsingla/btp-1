## 2. Introduction  
Ever wondered how ancient civilizations named unimaginably large numbers? This section explores the fascinating history of how people from India and beyond described powers of ten, from religious texts to modern currency! You’ll discover how numbers like *googol* and *googolplex* got their names—and even learn about the world’s largest ever printed money!  

---  

## 3. Explanation  
### 3.1 Historical Naming Systems  
Ancient texts used unique names for large powers of ten. For example:  
- **Ayuta** = $ 10^9 $ (100 crore)  
- **Niyuta** = $ 10^{11} $ (100 ayutas)  
- **Kankara** = $ 10^{13} $ (100 niyutas)  
This pattern continued up to $ 10^{53} $ in Buddhist texts like *Lalitavistara*. Jain and Hindu scriptures extended this further, naming values up to $ 10^{96} $ and even $ 10^{140} $!  

### 3.2 Modern Naming Conventions  
Today, we use:  
- **Million** = $ 10^6 $  
- **Billion** = $ 10^9 $  
- **Trillion** = $ 10^{12} $  
- ...and so on, with each new term representing $ 10^3 $ times the previous one.  
For example:

$$
10^6 \times 10^3 = 10^9 \quad (\text{million} \times 1000 = \text{billion})

$$  

### 3.3 Googol and Googolplex  
- **Googol** = $10^{100} $  
- **Googolplex** = $ 10^{\text{googol}} $ (a number with $ 10^{100} $ zeros!)  
These terms were coined in the 1930s and reflect humanity’s growing fascination with enormous quantities.  

### 3.4 Historical Currency Notes  
- **Hungary (1946)**: Printed a 1 sextillion pengő note ($ 10^{21} $) but never issued it.  
- **Zimbabwe (2009)**: Issued a 100 trillion ($ 10^{14} $) dollar note due to hyperinflation.

## 4. Solved Examples  
### Example 1: Convert Names to Exponents  
**Problem**: What is the value of *tallakshana*?  
**Solution**:  
From the text:  
- *Vibhutangama* = $ 10^{51} $  
- *Tallakshana* = $ 100 \times \text{vibhutangama} = 10^2 \times 10^{51} = 10^{53} $  
**Answer**: $ 10^{53} $  

### Example 2: Calculate Zeros in Googolplex  
**Problem**: How many zeros are in a googolplex?  
**Solution**:  
- Googol = $ 10^{100} $ → 100 zeros  
- Googolplex = $ 10^{\text{googol}} = 10^{10^{100}} $  
Thus, it contains $ 10^{100} $ zeros!  
**Answer**: $ 10^{100} $ zeros  

### Example 3: Compare Historical Values  
**Problem**: Which is larger—a *sarvajna* ($ 10^{49} $) or a *neel* ($ 10^{13} $)?  
**Solution**:  
Since $ 10^{49} > 10^{13} $, *sarvajna* is much larger.  
**Answer**: *Sarvajna* is larger

## 5. Practice Problems  
🟢 **Easy**: Identify the exponent for *maha shankh* (given: *shankh* = $ 10^{17} $)  
🟡 **Medium**: Calculate the number of zeros in a *googol*  
🔴 **Hard**: Compare $ 10^{96} $ (Jain *dasha-ananta*) and $ 10^{140} $ (Pali *asaṅkhyeya*)  

**Answers**:  
🟢 *Maha shankh* = $ 100 \times 10^{17} = 10^{19} $  
🟡 Googol = $ 10^{100} $ → 100 zeros  
🔴 $ 10^{140} > 10^{96} $

## 6. Summary  
- Ancient texts named powers of ten using terms like *ayuta* and *sarvajna*.  
- Modern terms like *million*, *billion*, and *trillion* follow a $ 10^3 $ progression.  
- A **googol** is $ 10^{100} $, and a **googolplex** is $ 10^{\text{googol}} $.  
- Historical currency notes included values like $ 10^{21} $ and $ 10^{14} $.
