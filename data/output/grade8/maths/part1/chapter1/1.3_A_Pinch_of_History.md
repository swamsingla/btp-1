## 2. Introduction  
Imagine discovering ancient clay tablets filled with numbers that helped people build cities and measure lands! In this section, we’ll explore the fascinating history behind square and cube numbers. Did you know that the Babylonians used these numbers over 4,000 years ago? We’ll uncover how ancient scholars named these mathematical ideas and why the word “root” is still used today.  

---  

## 3. Explanation  

### 3.1 Historical Origins  
The **Babylonians** created the first known lists of perfect squares and cubes around **1700 BCE**. These lists were etched onto clay tablets and used to calculate square roots and cube roots for tasks like measuring land or designing buildings.  

In **ancient India**, the terms *varga* (square) and *ghana* (cube) were used both for geometric shapes and their numerical values. For example:  
- *Varga* referred to the square of a number (e.g., $ 3^2 = 9 $)  
- *Ghana* referred to the cube of a number (e.g., $ 3^3 = 27 $)  

The fourth power (e.g., $ 3^4 = 81 $) was called *varga-varga*. These terms date back to at least the **third century BCE**.  

### 3.2 Etymology of “Root”  
The word “root” in mathematics comes from the **Sanskrit word *mula***, meaning “basis” or “origin.” Ancient Indian mathematicians used *varga-mula* (square root) and *ghana-mula* (cube root). This idea spread to Arabic (*jidhr*) and Latin (*radix*), shaping modern terminology.  

Aryabhata (499 CE) defined *varga* as:  
> “A square figure of four equal sides and the number representing its area are called *varga*.”  

Brahmagupta (628 CE) explained:  
> “The *pada* (root) of a *krti* (square) is that of which it is a square.”

## 4. Solved Examples  

### Example 1: Finding Cube Roots  
**Problem**: Find the cube root of $ 27000 $.  
**Solution**:  
We know that $ 30^3 = 27000 $.  
So, $\sqrt[3]{27000} = 30 $.  

### Example 2: Making a Number a Perfect Cube  
**Problem**: What number multiplied by $ 1323 $ makes it a cube?  
**Solution**:  
Factorize $ 1323 $:  
$ 1323 = 3^3 \times 7^2 $  
To make it a cube, we need one more $ 7 $:  
Multiply by $ 7 $ → $ 1323 \times 7 = 3^3 \times 7^3 = (3 \times 7)^3 = 21^3 $.  

### Example 3: True/False Statements  
**Statement (iii)**: *The cube of a 2-digit number may be a 3-digit number.*  
**Answer**: **True**.  
Example: $ 5^3 = 125 $ (but wait—$ 5 $ is single-digit!). Try $ 9^3 = 729 $ (still 3-digit). Wait, $ 10^3 = 1000 $ (4-digit). Hmm! Correction:  
Actually, the smallest 2-digit number is $ 10 $. $ 10^3 = 1000 $ (4-digit). So **Statement (iii)** is **false**.  

**Corrected Answer**: **False**. The cube of a 2-digit number is always ≥ $ 1000 $ (4-digit).

## 5. Practice Problems  

🟢 **Easy**:  
1. Find the cube root of $ 10648 $.  
2. Is $ 27 $ a perfect cube? Explain.  

🟡 **Medium**:  
3. What number multiplied by $ 128 $ gives a perfect cube?  

🔴 **Hard**:  
4. Which of the following is greater: $ 12^3 $ or $ 13^3 $? Explain using patterns.  

**Answers**:  
1. $\sqrt[3]{10648} = 22 $ (since $ 22^3 = 10648 $)  
2. Yes, $ 27 = 3^3 $, so it is a perfect cube.  
3. Factorize $ 128 = 2^7 $. To make it a cube, multiply by $ 2^2 = 4 $ → $ 128 \times 4 = 512 = 8^3 $.  
4. $ 13^3 = 2197 $ is greater than $ 12^3 = 1728 $. Cubes increase rapidly as numbers grow.

## 6. Summary  
- Ancient Babylonians used clay tablets to store square and cube numbers for practical calculations.  
- Sanskrit terms like *varga* (square) and *ghana* (cube) influenced modern mathematical language.  
- The word “root” originated from the Sanskrit *mula*, meaning “basis.”  
- Cube roots and perfect cubes are foundational for advanced geometry and algebra.

## 7. Key Formulas  
| Formula | Description |  
|--------|-------------|  
| $ n^2 $ | Square of a number $ n $ |  
| $ n^3 $ | Cube of a number $ n $ |  
| $\sqrt[3]{x} $ | Cube root of $ x $ |  
| $ varga $ | Square figure or its area |  
| $ ghana $ | Solid cube or its volume |
