## 2. Introduction  
**Have you ever noticed how tiles fit perfectly in a square shape?** Imagine a floor made of small square tiles—each tile’s side length determines the total area. This leads us to *square numbers*! In this topic, you’ll learn how numbers like 1, 4, 9, and 16 are special because they represent the area of squares. You’ll discover patterns, properties, and even how to identify squares just by looking at their last digit!  

---  

## 3. Explanation  

### 3.1 Definition of Square Numbers  
> **A square number** is the result of multiplying a number by itself. Mathematically, this is written as $ n \times n = n^2 $. For example:

$$
1 \times 1 = 1^2 = 1 \\
2 \times 2 = 2^2 = 4 \\
3 \times 3 = 3^2 = 9

$$  
These numbers (1, 4, 9, 16, ...) are called **perfect squares** because they form perfect squares geometrically.  

### 3.2 Units Digit Patterns  
> **Perfect squares** never end with certain digits. Study the table below:  

| Side Length | Area (Units) | Ends With |  
|-------------|--------------|-----------|  
| 1           | 1            | 1         |  
| 2           | 4            | 4         |  
| 3           | 9            | 9         |  
| 4           | 16           | 6         |  
| 5           | 25           | 5         |  
| 10          | 100          | 0         |  

**Observation**:  
- Perfect squares always end with **0, 1, 4, 5, 6, or 9**.  
- If a number ends with **2, 3, 7, or 8**, it **cannot** be a perfect square.  

### 3.3 Decimal and Fractional Squares  
Even numbers like $2.5 $ or $\frac{3}{5} $ can be squared:

$$
(2.5)^2 = 2.5 \times 2.5 = 6.25 \\
\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)^2 = \frac{3}{5} \times \frac{3}{5} = \frac{9}{25}

$$  
This shows that square numbers aren’t limited to whole numbers—they apply to all real numbers!  

### 3.4 Patterns in Squares  
Explore the table of squares for the first 30 natural numbers:

$$
\begin{align*}
1^2 &= 1 & 11^2 &= 121 \\
2^2 &= 4 & 12^2 &= 144 \\
3^2 &= 9 & 13^2 &= 169 \\
4^2 &= 16 & 14^2 &= 196 \\
5^2 &= 25 & 15^2 &= 225 \\
\end{align*}

$$  
**Pattern**:  
- Squares ending in **1** or **9** often have a **1** in the units place. For example, $11^2 = 121 $, $ 19^2 = 361 $.  
- Squares ending in **6** (like $ 4^2 = 16 $, $ 6^2 = 36 $) follow a predictable pattern.

## 4. Solved Examples  

### Example 1: Calculating a Square  
**Problem**: Find $ 7.5^2 $.  
**Solution**:

$$
7.5 \times 7.5 = ?

$$  
Break it down:

$$
(7 + 0.5)^2 = 7^2 + 2 \times 7 \times 0.5 + 0.5^2 = 49 + 7 + 0.25 = 56.25

$$  
✅ Final Answer: $56.25 $  

### Example 2: Identifying Non-Square Numbers  
**Problem**: Is 26 a perfect square?  
**Solution**:  
Check the units digit: 26 ends with **6**, which is allowed. However, test divisibility:

$$
\text{Try } 5^2 = 25, \quad 6^2 = 36.

$$  
Since 26 lies between 25 and 36, it isn’t a perfect square.  
✅ Final Answer: No, 26 is not a perfect square.  

### Example 3: Using Units Digit Rules  
**Problem**: Which of these numbers cannot be squares?  
**(i)** 382 **(ii)** 342 **(iii)** 462 **(iv)** 562 **(v)** 742 **(vi)** 822  
**Solution**:  
Check the units digit:  
- **382**: Ends with 2 ❌  
- **342**: Ends with 2 ❌  
- **462**: Ends with 2 ❌  
- **562**: Ends with 2 ❌  
- **742**: Ends with 2 ❌  
- **822**: Ends with 2 ❌  
✅ Final Answer: All six numbers are **not** perfect squares.  

### Example 4: Predicting Square Patterns  
**Problem**: What is the next square after $19^2 = 361 $?  
**Solution**:  
The next number is $ 20 $:

$$
20^2 = 20 \times 20 = 400

$$  
✅ Final Answer: $400 $  

### Example 5: Fraction Squares  
**Problem**: Calculate $\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^2 $.  
**Solution**:  
Multiply numerator and denominator:

$$
\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{2}{3} = \frac{4}{9}

$$  
✅ Final Answer: $\frac{4}{9} $

## 5. Practice Problems  

### 🟢 Easy  
1. What is $ 6.5^2 $?  
2. Identify whether 81 is a perfect square.  
3. List three numbers ending with 6 that are perfect squares.  

### 🟡 Medium  
4. Find the square of $\frac{5}{2} $.  
5. Why can’t 122 be a perfect square?  

### 🔴 Hard  
6. Prove that numbers ending with 5 always have squares ending with 25.  

**Answers**:  

1. **Solution**:

$$
6.5^2 = (6 + 0.5)^2 = 6^2 + 2 \times 6 \times 0.5 + 0.5^2 = 36 + 6 + 0.25 = 42.25

$$  
✅ Answer: $42.25 $  

2. **Solution**:  
Check $ 9^2 = 81 $.  
✅ Answer: Yes, 81 is a perfect square.  

3. **Solution**:  
Examples: $ 5^2 = 25 $, $ 15^2 = 225 $, $ 25^2 = 625 $.  
✅ Answer: 25, 225, 625  

4. **Solution**:

$$
\left(\frac{5}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{25}{4}

$$  
✅ Answer: $\frac{25}{4} $  

5. **Solution**:  
Numbers ending with 5: $ 5^2 = 25 $, $ 15^2 = 225 $. 122 ends with 2, which is not allowed.  
✅ Answer: 122 cannot be a perfect square.  

6. **Solution**:  
Take a number ending with 5: $ n = 10k + 5 $.

$$
n^2 = (10k + 5)^2 = 100k^2 + 100k + 25 = 100(k^2 + k) + 25

$$  
Thus, the square ends with **25**.  
✅ Proof complete.

## 6. Summary  
- **Square numbers** are formed by $n \times n = n^2 $.  
- **Perfect squares** include 1, 4, 9, 16, ...  
- Squares **never** end with 2, 3, 7, or 8.  
- Decimals and fractions can be squared (e.g., $ 2.5^2 = 6.25 $).  
- Patterns exist for squares ending with 1, 4, 5, 6, or 9.

| Formula | Description |  
|--------|------------|

| $ (a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2 $ | Expansion of squared binomials |

## 8. Student Corner  

### Common Mistakes  
- **Mistake**: Confusing $ n^2 $ with $ 2n $.  
  **Fix**: Remember $ n^2 = n \times n $, not $ 2 \times n $.  
- **Mistake**: Assuming all numbers ending with 6 are squares.  
  **Fix**: Test with examples like $ 26 $ (not a square) vs. $ 16 $ (a square).  

### Exam Tips  
- Always check the **last digit** first to eliminate non-squares quickly.  
- Practice expanding squares of decimals and fractions to build confidence.  

### Memory Aid  
**Rhyme**: *"Square ends with 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, 9—others are not!"*
