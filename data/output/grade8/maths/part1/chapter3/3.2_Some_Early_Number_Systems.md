## 3. Explanation  

### 3.1 Use of Body Parts  
Many ancient cultures used their **hands and body parts** as tools for counting. For example, in Papua New Guinea, people used fingers and toes to represent numbers. This method is simple and intuitive, relying on physical gestures to communicate quantities.  

> **Definition**: *Body-part counting* refers to the practice of using fingers, toes, or other body parts to represent numerical values.  

**Example**: If someone holds up three fingers, they are showing the number **3**. This system is still used today in informal settings, like counting money or items quickly.  

---

### 3.2 Tally Marks on Bones and Surfaces  
One of the earliest known methods of recording numbers is **tally marks**, which are small notches carved into bones, stones, or walls. These marks served as a way to count objects or events.  

> **Definition**: *Tally marks* are simple notches made on surfaces to represent quantities. Each notch corresponds to one item being counted.  

**Properties of Tally Marks**:  
- A single mark represents **1**.  
- Groups of five are often marked together (e.g., four vertical lines and a diagonal line crossing them).  
- Archaeologists have found bones with tally marks over **20,000 years old**, such as the **Ishango bone** (Democratic Republic of Congo) and the **Lebombo bone** (South Africa).  

**Fun Fact**: The Ishango bone has notches arranged in columns, suggesting early use of **calendrical systems** (tracking time, like months or seasons).  

---

### 3.3 Number Names Based on Counting in Twos  
Some Indigenous groups, like the **Gumulgal** in Australia and the **Bushmen** in South Africa, developed number systems based on counting in twos. Their number names combine smaller units to form larger numbers.  

> **Definition**: *Base-2 counting* involves grouping numbers in twos, where each new number is built by adding multiples of 2.  

**Example**:  
- 1 = *urapon*  
- 2 = *ukasar*  
- 3 = *ukasar-urapon* (2 + 1)  
- 4 = *ukasar-ukasar* (2 + 2)  
- 5 = *ukasar-ukasar-urapon* (2 + 2 + 1)  
- 6 = *ukasar-ukasar-ukasar* (2 + 2 + 2)  

**Key Insight**: This system shows how numbers can be constructed using repeated addition. It’s similar to how we use place value in base-10 (e.g., 10 = 1 × 10 + 0).  

**Challenge Question**: Why did the Gumulgal call numbers greater than 6 “ras”?

## 4. Solved Examples  

### Example 1: Calculating Age Difference Between Bones  
The **Lebombo bone** is **44,000 years old**, while the **Ishango bone** is **20,000 to 35,000 years old**. Find the age difference between the two.  

**Solution**:  
1. Subtract the younger bone’s age from the older one:

$$
   44,000 - 35,000 = 9,000 \text{ years}

$$  
2. Answer: The Lebombo bone is **9,000 years older** than the Ishango bone.  

---

### Example 2: Converting Numbers Using Base-2 Logic  
If the Bushmen counted in twos, how would they express the number **7**?  

**Solution**:  
1. Break down 7 using multiples of 2:

$$
   7 = 2 + 2 + 2 + 1

$$  
2. Apply the Bushmen’s naming convention:  
   - 2 = *ahage*  
   - 1 = *tokale*  
   - 7 = *ahage-ahage-ahage-tokale*  

Answer: **ahage-ahage-ahage-tokale**  

---

### Example 3: Analyzing Tally Mark Patterns  
A bone has 5 rows of tally marks, with 4 notches per row. How many notches are there in total?  

**Solution**:  
1. Multiply the number of rows by notches per row:

$$
   5 \times 4 = 20 \text{ notches}

$$  
Answer: There are **20 notches** in total.

## 5. Practice Problems  

### 🟢 Easy: Matching Notches to Numbers  
A bone has 3 rows of tally marks, with 3 notches per row. How many notches does it have in total?  

**Answer**:

$$
3 \times 3 = 9 \text{ notches}

$$  

---

### 🟡 Medium: Comparing Bone Ages  
The Lebombo bone is **44,000 years old**. The Ishango bone is **15,000 years younger**. What is the Ishango bone’s age?  

**Answer**:

$$
44,000 - 15,000 = 29,000 \text{ years}

$$

### 🔴 Hard: Building a Base-2 Number System  
Using the Gumulgal pattern, create the number names for 7 through 10.  

**Answer**:  
- 7 = *ukasar-ukasar-ukasar-urapon* (2+2+2+1)  
- 8 = *ukasar-ukasar-ukasar-ukasar* (2+2+2+2)  
- 9 = *ukasar-ukasar-ukasar-ukasar-urapon* (2+2+2+2+1)  
- 10 = *ukasar-ukasar-ukasar-ukasar-ukasar* (2+2+2+2+2)

## 6. Summary  
- Ancient cultures used **body parts** and **tally marks** for counting.  
- The **Lebombo bone** (44,000 years old) and **Ishango bone** (20,000–35,000 years old) are among the oldest known tally systems.  
- The **Gumulgal** and **Bushmen** used **base-2 counting**, combining pairs to form numbers.  
- Tally marks are grouped in fives for easier reading (e.g., 4 vertical lines + 1 diagonal line).  
- Understanding these systems helps us appreciate the evolution of **numerical representation**.

## 8. Student Corner  

### Common Mistakes  
- ❌ Confusing tally marks with other symbols (e.g., Roman numerals).  
  ✅ Fix: Remember tally marks are simple notches, not letters or shapes.  
- ❌ Miscounting notches in groups of five.  
  ✅ Fix: Always count 4 vertical lines + 1 diagonal line as 5.  

### Exam Tips  
- Focus on **examples from the text** (like the Ishango and Lebombo bones) during exams.  
- Practice converting numbers into base-2 patterns (e.g., 7 = 2+2+2+1).  

### Memory Aid  
**"Two, Two, One—Make Seven!"**  
Remember that base-2 systems build numbers by adding 2s and 1s.
