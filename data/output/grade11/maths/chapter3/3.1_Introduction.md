## 1. Prerequisites  
Before diving into this topic, students should be familiar with:

## 2. Introduction  
**Have you ever wondered how sailors navigate vast oceans or architects design towering structures?**  
Trigonometry, derived from the Greek words *trigonon* (triangle) and *metron* (measure), has been a cornerstone of mathematics for centuries. Originally used to solve geometric problems involving triangles, it now plays a vital role in fields like engineering, physics, and computer science.  

In this chapter, you’ll explore how trigonometric ratios of acute angles (studied in earlier grades) are extended to **trigonometric functions**, enabling analysis of angles beyond 90°. This generalization allows us to model periodic phenomena such as sound waves, light waves, and even the motion of planets! By the end of this section, you’ll understand the significance of trigonometric functions and their relevance to both theoretical and applied mathematics.  

---  

## 3. Explanation  
### 3.1 Origin and Scope of Trigonometry  
> **Trigonometry** is the branch of mathematics that studies relationships between angles and sides of triangles, particularly right-angled triangles. Its origins trace back to ancient civilizations like Greece, India, and Egypt, where it was used for astronomical calculations, construction, and navigation.  

Modern applications of trigonometry include:  
- Calculating the height of mountains using angles of elevation.  
- Modeling wave behavior in physics and acoustics.  
- Designing electrical circuits and analyzing data patterns.  

### 3.2 Generalization of Trigonometric Ratios  
> **Trigonometric functions** extend the concept of trigonometric ratios to **any real number**, not just acute angles. While earlier studies focused on ratios of sides in triangles, this chapter introduces functions defined for all angles measured in degrees or radians.  

This generalization is crucial because:  
- It allows modeling **periodic phenomena** (e.g., oscillations, rotations).  
- It provides tools for solving complex problems in calculus and higher mathematics.  

### 3.3 Historical Context  
> **Aryabhata**, a renowned Indian mathematician (circa 476–550 CE), contributed significantly to trigonometry. His work laid the foundation for systematic study of trigonometric functions, though the modern framework emerged through contributions from Islamic scholars and European mathematicians.

## 4. Solved Examples  
**Example 1:**  
Convert $ 60^\circ $ to radians.  
**Solution:**  
To convert degrees to radians, use the formula:

$$
\text{Radians} = \text{Degrees} \times \frac{\pi}{180}

$$  
Substituting $ 60^\circ $:

$$
60^\circ \times \frac{\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{3} \text{ radians}

$$  
**Answer:** $ \frac{\pi}{3} $ radians  

**Example 2:**  
Evaluate $ \sin(30^\circ) $ and $ \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) $.  
**Solution:**  
- $ \sin(30^\circ) = \frac{1}{2} $ (standard angle).  
- $ \frac{\pi}{6} $ radians equals $ 30^\circ $, so $ \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \frac{1}{2} $.  
**Answer:** Both values are $ \frac{1}{2} $.  

**Example 3:**  
Prove that $ \tan(\theta) = \frac{\sin(\theta)}{\cos(\theta)} $.  
**Solution:**  
By definition:

$$
\tan(\theta) = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}, \quad \sin(\theta) = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}, \quad \cos(\theta) = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}

$$  
Dividing $ \sin(\theta) $ by $ \cos(\theta) $:

$$
\frac{\sin(\theta)}{\cos(\theta)} = \frac{\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}}{\frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}} = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}} = \tan(\theta)

$$  
**Answer:** Proven identity.  

**Example 4:**  
Find the domain and range of $ f(x) = \sin(x) $.  
**Solution:**  
- **Domain:** All real numbers, $ x \in \mathbb{R} $.  
- **Range:** $ [-1, 1] $, since the sine function oscillates between -1 and 1.  
**Answer:** Domain: $ \mathbb{R} $; Range: $ [-1, 1] $.  

**Example 5:**  
State the relationship between degrees and radians.  
**Solution:**  
- $ 180^\circ = \pi $ radians.  
- Therefore, $ 1^\circ = \frac{\pi}{180} $ radians and $ 1 $ radian $ = \frac{180}{\pi} $ degrees.  
**Answer:** Direct proportionality with conversion factor $ \frac{\pi}{180} $.

## 5. Practice Problems  
🟢 **Easy:**  
1. Convert $ 90^\circ $ to radians.  
2. Evaluate $ \cos(0^\circ) $.  

🟡 **Medium:**  
3. Find the domain and range of $ f(x) = \cos(x) $.  
4. Prove that $ \cot(\theta) = \frac{\cos(\theta)}{\sin(\theta)} $.  

🔴 **Hard:**  
5. If $ \alpha = 45^\circ $ and $ \beta = \frac{\pi}{4} $, show that $ \alpha $ and $ \beta $ represent the same angle.  

**Answers:**  
1. $ \frac{\pi}{2} $ radians.  
2. $ \cos(0^\circ) = 1 $.  
3. Domain: $ \mathbb{R} $; Range: $ [-1, 1] $.  
4. By definition: $ \cot(\theta) = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{opposite}} $, which equals $ \frac{\cos(\theta)}{\sin(\theta)} $.  
5. Since $ \frac{\pi}{4} $ radians $ = 45^\circ $, both $ \alpha $ and $ \beta $ are equivalent.

## 6. Summary  
- **Trigonometry** originates from Greek and has evolved to analyze angles and periodic phenomena.  
- **Trigonometric functions** generalize ratios to all real numbers, enabling deeper mathematical exploration.  
- **Conversion between degrees and radians** is essential for working with trigonometric functions.  
- **Standard angles** (e.g., $ 30^\circ, 45^\circ, 60^\circ $) have known sine, cosine, and tangent values.  
- **Domain and range** of basic trigonometric functions are critical for graphing and problem-solving.

## 7. Key Formulas  
| Formula | Description |  
|--------|-------------|  
| $ \text{Radians} = \text{Degrees} \times \frac{\pi}{180} $ | Conversion from degrees to radians. |  
| $ \text{Degrees} = \text{Radians} \times \frac{180}{\pi} $ | Conversion from radians to degrees. |

| $ \tan(\theta) = \frac{\sin(\theta)}{\cos(\theta)} $ | Tangent as sine over cosine. |  
| $ \cot(\theta) = \frac{\cos(\theta)}{\sin(\theta)} $ | Cotangent as cosine over sine. |

## 8. Student Corner  
### Common Mistakes  
- **Confusing degrees and radians:** Always specify the unit when working with angles.  
- **Misapplying identities:** Verify whether the identity applies to all angles or only specific cases (e.g., acute angles).  
- **Ignoring domain restrictions:** For example, $ \tan(\theta) $ is undefined at $ \theta = \frac{\pi}{2} + k\pi $.  

### Exam Tips  
- Memorize standard angle values (e.g., $ \sin(30^\circ) = \frac{1}{2} $) to save time during exams.  
- Use unit circle diagrams to visualize relationships between angles and trigonometric functions.

- **S**in = **O**pposite / **H**ypotenuse  
- **C**os = **A**djacent / **H**ypotenuse  
- **T**an = **O**pposite / **A**djacent
