## 1. Prerequisites  
Before studying this topic, students should already understand:  
- Basic set theory concepts like **elements**, **membership** ($\in $), and **subsets** ($\subset $)  
- The definition and representation of **real numbers** ($\mathbb{R} $)  
- Fundamental operations involving **integers** and **fractions**

## 2. Introduction  
Real numbers form a vast set containing both rational and irrational numbers. This section explores important **subsets** of $\mathbb{R} $, including natural numbers, integers, rational numbers, and irrational numbers. By understanding these subsets, students will gain clarity on how different types of numbers relate within the broader framework of real numbers.  

---  

## 3. Explanation  

### 3.1 Definitions of Key Subsets  
**Natural numbers** ($\mathbb{N} $):

$$

\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, \dots\}

$$  
These are the counting numbers starting from 1.  

**Integers** ($\mathbb{Z} $):

$$

\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\}

$$  
This set includes all positive and negative whole numbers along with zero.  

**Rational numbers** ($\mathbb{Q} $):

$$

\mathbb{Q} = \left\{ x : x = \frac{p}{q},\ p, q \in \mathbb{Z}\ \text{and}\ q \neq 0 \right\}

$$  
A rational number can always be expressed as a fraction of two integers. Examples include $-\frac{5}{1} $, $\frac{7}{5} $, and $\frac{1}{2} $.  

**Irrational numbers** ($ T $):

$$

T = \{x : x \in \mathbb{R}\ \text{and}\ x \notin \mathbb{Q}\}

$$  
These are real numbers that cannot be expressed as fractions of integers. Examples include $\sqrt{2} $, $\pi $, and $ e $.  

> **Important Note**: The set of real numbers ($\mathbb{R} $) includes both $\mathbb{Q} $ and $ T $. Thus, $\mathbb{Q} \cup T = \mathbb{R} $.  

### 3.2 Subset Relationships  
From the source text, we derive the following hierarchical relationships:  
- $\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} $  
- $\mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R} $  
- $ T \subset \mathbb{R} $  
- $\mathbb{N} \not\subset T $ (natural numbers and irrational numbers are disjoint sets)  

> **Proof Sketch for $\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} $**:  
Every natural number is a positive integer. Since $\mathbb{Z} $ contains all integers, including positive ones, $\mathbb{N} $ is entirely contained within $\mathbb{Z} $.  

> **Proof Sketch for $\mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R} $**:  
By definition, $\mathbb{Q} $ is a subset of $\mathbb{R} $ because all rational numbers are real numbers.  

### 3.3 Intervals as Subsets of $\mathbb{R} $  
Although the source text truncates here, intervals are defined as subsets of $\mathbb{R} $ based on inequalities. For example:  
- **Closed interval**: $ [a, b] = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : a \leq x \leq b\} $  
- **Open interval**: $ (a, b) = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : a < x < b\} $  
- **Half-open intervals**: $ [a, b) $ or $ (a, b] $

## 4. Solved Examples  

### Example 1:  
**Determine whether $-\frac{3}{2} $ belongs to $\mathbb{Q} $**.  

**Solution:**  
- $-\frac{3}{2} $ is a fraction where $ p = -3 $ and $ q = 2 $, both integers with $ q \neq 0 $.  
- By definition, $\mathbb{Q} $ includes all such fractions.  
**Conclusion**: $-\frac{3}{2} \in \mathbb{Q} $.  

### Example 2:  
**Verify the relationship $\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} $**.  

**Solution:**  
- Take any element $ x \in \mathbb{N} $. By definition, $ x $ is a positive integer.  
- Since $\mathbb{Z} $ contains all integers, $ x $ is also in $\mathbb{Z} $.  
**Conclusion**: Every natural number is an integer, hence $\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} $.  

### Example 3:  
**Prove that $\sqrt{2} \in T $**.  

**Solution:**  
- Assume $\sqrt{2} $ is rational: $\sqrt{2} = \frac{p}{q} $, where $ p, q \in \mathbb{Z} $ and $ q \neq 0 $.  
- Squaring both sides: $ 2 = \frac{p^2}{q^2} $ ⇒ $ p^2 = 2q^2 $.  
- This implies $ p^2 $ is even, so $ p $ must be even. Let $ p = 2k $:

$$

(2k)^2 = 2q^2 \Rightarrow 4k^2 = 2q^2 \Rightarrow 2k^2 = q^2

$$  
- Now $q^2 $ is even ⇒ $ q $ is even. Contradiction: $ p $ and $ q $ share a common factor of 2.  
**Conclusion**: $\sqrt{2} $ is irrational ⇒ $\sqrt{2} \in T $.

## 5. Practice Problems  

🟢 **Easy**:  
1. Is $-\frac{5}{1} $ a member of $\mathbb{Q} $? Justify.  
2. Identify the subset of $\mathbb{R} $ containing all positive integers.  

🟡 **Medium**:  
3. Prove that $\mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} $.  
4. Show that $ T $ and $\mathbb{Q} $ are disjoint sets.  

🔴 **Hard**:  
5. Given $ a = 2 $ and $ b = 5 $, describe the closed interval $ [a, b] $ and classify its elements.  

**Answers:**  

1. **Answer**: Yes. $-\frac{5}{1} = -5 $ is an integer, and all integers are rational. Hence, $-\frac{5}{1} \in \mathbb{Q} $.  
2. **Answer**: The subset is $\mathbb{N} $, as it contains all positive integers.  

3. **Answer**: Any integer $ x \in \mathbb{Z} $ can be written as $\frac{x}{1} $, satisfying the definition of $\mathbb{Q} $. Thus, $\mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} $.  
4. **Answer**: By definition, $ T $ contains numbers not in $\mathbb{Q} $. Therefore, $ T \cap \mathbb{Q} = \emptyset $.  

5. **Answer**: The closed interval $ [2, 5] $ includes all real numbers $ x $ such that $ 2 \leq x \leq 5 $. Its elements are:  
   - Rational numbers (e.g., 2, 3, 4, 2.5)  
   - Irrational numbers (e.g., $\sqrt{5} $, $\pi $)  
   - Both are subsets of $\mathbb{R} $.

## 6. Summary  
- $\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\} $ (natural numbers)  
- $\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \dots\} $ (integers)  
- $\mathbb{Q} = \{x : x = \frac{p}{q},\ p, q \in \mathbb{Z},\ q \neq 0\} $ (rational numbers)  
- $ T = \{x : x \in \mathbb{R}\ \text{and}\ x \notin \mathbb{Q}\} $ (irrational numbers)  
- Subset relationships: $\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R} $, $ T \subset \mathbb{R} $  
- Intervals are special subsets defined by inequalities

## 8. Student Corner  

**Common Mistakes**:  
- Confusing $\mathbb{Q} $ with $\mathbb{Z} $ (e.g., thinking $ 0.333\ldots $ is not rational).  
- Misapplying subset symbols ($\subset $ vs. $\subseteq $).  

**Exam Tip**: Remember the hierarchy: $\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R} $, and $ T $ is complementary to $\mathbb{Q} $ in $\mathbb{R} $.
