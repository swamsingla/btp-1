## 1. Prerequisites  
Before diving into **Relations**, students should already be familiar with:  
- **Set notation** and **element membership** (e.g., $a \in A $ means *a* is an element of set *A*).  
- **Cartesian products** of sets (e.g., $ P \times Q $ creates ordered pairs from sets *P* and *Q*).  
- Basic **subsets** and **ordered pairs** (e.g., $ (x, y) $ represents an ordered pair where *x* and *y* belong to respective sets).  

These foundational concepts are critical for understanding how relations are constructed from Cartesian products.

## 2. Introduction  
Have you ever wondered how relationships between entities are described mathematically? For instance, consider a classroom where students are paired with their favorite subjects. This pairing is essentially a **relation**!  

In this topic, we explore **relations** as subsets of Cartesian products. We’ll learn how to define relations, identify their **domain**, **range**, and **codomain**, and represent them using **arrow diagrams** or **set-builder notation**. By the end, you’ll be able to analyze and construct relations systematically, much like analyzing connections in real-world scenarios.  

This builds directly on your prior knowledge of Cartesian products (Section 2.2) and prepares you for the study of **functions** (Section 2.4).

## 3. Explanation  

### 3.1 Definition of a Relation  
> **Definition 2**: A **relation** $ R $ from a non-empty set $ A $ to a non-empty set $ B $ is a **subset** of the Cartesian product $ A \times B $. To form $ R $, we describe a relationship between the **first element** (*x*) and the **second element** (*y*) of each ordered pair $ (x, y) $ in $ A \times B $.  

**Key Vocabulary**:  
- **Ordered pair**: A pair $ (x, y) $ where the position matters (e.g., $ (a, b) \neq (b, a) $ unless $ a = b $).  
- **Subset**: A collection of elements from a larger set (e.g., $ R \subset A \times B $).  

### 3.2 Domain, Range, and Codomain  
> **Definition 3**: The **domain** of a relation $ R $ is the set of all **first elements** of the ordered pairs in $ R $.  
> **Definition 4**: The **range** of $ R $ is the set of all **second elements** in $ R $. The **codomain** is the entire set $ B $, and it satisfies $ \text{range} \subset \text{codomain} $.  

**Important Note**:  
The **range** is not necessarily equal to the **codomain**. For example, in the relation $ R = \{(a, \text{Ali}), (b, \text{Bhanu})\} $, the codomain is $ Q = \{\text{Ali}, \text{Bhanu}, \text{Binoy}, \text{Chandra}, \text{Divya}\} $, but the range is $\{\text{Ali}, \text{Bhanu}\} $.  

### 3.3 Representation Methods  
> **Remark (i)**: A relation can be expressed using:  
- **Roster method**: Listing all ordered pairs explicitly (e.g., $ R = \{(a, \text{Ali}), (b, \text{Bhanu})\} $).  
- **Set-builder method**: Describing the rule that defines the relation (e.g., $ R = \{(x, y): x \text{ is the first letter of } y, x \in P, y \in Q\} $).  

> **Remark (ii)**: **Arrow diagrams** visually depict relations by drawing arrows from elements of $ A $ to corresponding elements of $ B $.  

### 3.4 Example-Based Understanding  
Let’s revisit the example from the source text:  
- $ P = \{a, b, c\} $, $ Q = \{\text{Ali}, \text{Bhanu}, \text{Binoy}, \text{Chandra}, \text{Divya}\} $  
- $ R = \{(x, y): x \text{ is the first letter of } y, x \in P, y \in Q\} $  
Thus, $ R = \{(a, \text{Ali}), (b, \text{Bhanu}), (b, \text{Binoy}), (c, \text{Chandra})\} $.  

**Domain**: $\{a, b, c\} $  
**Range**: $\{\text{Ali}, \text{Bhanu}, \text{Binoy}, \text{Chandra}\} $  
**Codomain**: $ Q = \{\text{Ali}, \text{Bhanu}, \text{Binoy}, \text{Chandra}, \text{Divya}\} $  

This illustrates how relations filter Cartesian products based on specific conditions.

## 4. Solved Examples  

### Example 1:  
Let $ A = \{1, 2, 3\} $ and $ B = \{4, 5\} $. Define a relation $ R $ such that $ R = \{(x, y): y = x + 3\} $.  
**Solution**:  
- Compute all possible ordered pairs in $ A \times B $:  
  $ A \times B = \{(1, 4), (1, 5), (2, 4), (2, 5), (3, 4), (3, 5)\} $.  
- Apply the condition $ y = x + 3 $:  
  - For $ x = 1 $: $ y = 4 $ → $ (1, 4) \in R $  
  - For $ x = 2 $: $ y = 5 $ → $ (2, 5) \in R $  
  - For $ x = 3 $: $ y = 6 $ (not in $ B $) → excluded  
- Final relation: $ R = \{(1, 4), (2, 5)\} $  
- **Domain**: $\{1, 2\} $  
- **Range**: $\{4, 5\} $  
- **Codomain**: $ B = \{4, 5\} $  

### Example 2:  
Define $ R $ from $ A = \{1, 2, 3, 4\} $ to $ A $ as $ R = \{(x, y): y < x\} $.  
**Solution**:  
- List all ordered pairs where the second element is less than the first:  
  - $ x = 1 $: No valid $ y $ (since $ y < 1 $ is impossible)  
  - $ x = 2 $: $ y = 1 $ → $ (2, 1) \in R $  
  - $ x = 3 $: $ y = 1, 2 $ → $ (3, 1), (3, 2) $  
  - $ x = 4 $: $ y = 1, 2, 3 $ → $ (4, 1), (4, 2), (4, 3) $  
- Final relation: $ R = \{(2, 1), (3, 1), (3, 2), (4, 1), (4, 2), (4, 3)\} $  
- **Domain**: $\{2, 3, 4\} $  
- **Range**: $\{1, 2, 3\} $  
- **Codomain**: $ A = \{1, 2, 3, 4\} $  

### Example 3:  
Given $ P = \{4, 9, 25\} $ and $ Q = \{-5, -3, -2, 2, 3, 5\} $, define $ R = \{(x, y): x = y^2\} $.  
**Solution**:  
- Find all $ y \in Q $ such that $ y^2 \in P $:  
  - $ y = -5 $: $ (-5)^2 = 25 \in P $ → $ (25, -5) $  
  - $ y = -3 $: $ (-3)^2 = 9 \in P $ → $ (9, -3) $  
  - $ y = -2 $: $ (-2)^2 = 4 \in P $ → $ (4, -2) $  
  - $ y = 2 $: $ 2^2 = 4 \in P $ → $ (4, 2) $  
  - $ y = 3 $: $ 3^2 = 9 \in P $ → $ (9, 3) $  
  - $ y = 5 $: $ 5^2 = 25 \in P $ → $ (25, 5) $  
- Final relation: $ R = \{(4, -2), (4, 2), (9, -3), (9, 3), (25, -5), (25, 5)\} $  
- **Domain**: $\{4, 9, 25\} $  
- **Range**: $\{-5, -3, -2, 2, 3, 5\} $  
- **Codomain**: $ Q = \{-5, -3, -2, 2, 3, 5\} $  

### Example 4:  
Represent the relation $ R = \{(1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 4)\} $ using an **arrow diagram**.  
**Solution**:  
- Draw three circles labeled $ 1, 2, 3 $ (domain) and $ 2, 3, 4 $ (codomain).  
- Draw arrows from $ 1 \rightarrow 2 $, $ 2 \rightarrow 3 $, and $ 3 \rightarrow 4 $.  
- This visually shows the mapping of each element in the domain to its corresponding image in the codomain.

## 5. Practice Problems  

### 🟢 Easy  
**Problem 1**: Let $ A = \{1, 2, 3\} $ and $ B = \{4, 5\} $. Define $ R = \{(x, y): y = x + 4\} $.  
- What is the **domain** of $ R $?  
- What is the **range** of $ R $?  

### 🟡 Medium  
**Problem 2**: Given $ C = \{2, 4, 6\} $ and $ D = \{1, 3, 5\} $, define $ R = \{(x, y): y = x - 1\} $.  
- Express $ R $ in **roster form**.  
- Identify the **codomain** of $ R $.  

### 🔴 Hard  
**Problem 3**: Let $ E = \{1, 2, 3, 4\} $ and $ F = \{2, 4, 6, 8\} $. Define $ R = \{(x, y): y = 2x\} $.  
- Write $ R $ in **set-builder form**.  
- Determine the **range** of $ R $.  

**Answers**:  
**Problem 1**:  
- Domain: $\{1, 2, 3\} $ (all elements of $ A $ satisfy $ y = x + 4 $ within $ B $).  
- Range: $\{4, 5\} $ (since $ 1 + 4 = 5 $ and $ 2 + 4 = 6 $ [invalid], but only $ x = 1 $ maps to $ y = 5 $; wait, correction: $ x = 1 $ gives $ y = 5 $, $ x = 2 $ gives $ y = 6 $ [not in $ B $], so actual range is $\{5\} $).  

**Problem 2**:  
- Roster form: $ R = \{(2, 1), (4, 3)\} $.  
- Codomain: $ D = \{1, 3, 5\} $.  

**Problem 3**:  
- Set-builder form: $ R = \{(x, y): y = 2x, x \in E, y \in F\} $.  
- Range: $\{4, 8\} $ (since $ 2 \times 2 = 4 $ and $ 4 \times 2 = 8 $; $ x = 3 $ gives $ y = 6 $ [not in $ F $], $ x = 4 $ gives $ y = 8 $).

## 6. Summary  
- A **relation** is a subset of the Cartesian product $ A \times B $.  
- The **domain** consists of all first elements of ordered pairs in $ R $.  
- The **range** contains all second elements of ordered pairs in $ R $.  
- The **codomain** is the entire set $ B $, with $\text{range} \subset \text{codomain} $.  
- Relations can be expressed via **roster method**, **set-builder method**, or **arrow diagrams**.  
- Always specify the **rule** defining the relation (e.g., $ y = x + 1 $) to avoid ambiguity.  

---  

## 7. Key Formulas  

| Formula | Description |  
|--------|-------------|  
| $ R \subset A \times B $ | A relation $ R $ is a subset of the Cartesian product. |  
| $\text{Domain}(R) = \{x \mid \exists y \in B, (x, y) \in R\} $ | The set of all first elements in $ R $. |  
| $\text{Range}(R) = \{y \mid \exists x \in A, (x, y) \in R\} $ | The set of all second elements in $ R $. |  
| $\text{Codomain}(R) = B $ | The entire set $ B $ is the codomain. |  
| $ R = \{(x, y): \text{condition relating } x \text{ and } y\} $ | Set-builder form of a relation. |

## 8. Student Corner  

### Common Mistakes  
1. **Confusing domain and codomain**:  
   - ❌ Error: Assuming the domain equals the codomain.  
   - ✅ Fix: Remember the domain is derived from $ R $, while the codomain is the entire set $ B $.  

2. **Incorrect set-builder notation**:  
   - ❌ Error: Omitting conditions (e.g., $ R = \{(x, y): x \in A\} $).  
   - ✅ Fix: Always specify the relationship (e.g., $ R = \{(x, y): y = x + 1\} $).  

### Exam Tips  
- When asked to find the **range**, explicitly list all second elements of ordered pairs in $ R $.  
- For **arrow diagrams**, draw arrows only for pairs explicitly included in $ R $, not for all possible pairs.  

### Memory Aid  
**"DRY" Mnemonic**:  
- **D**omain: First elements of ordered pairs.  
- **R**ange: Second elements of ordered pairs.  
- **Y**ou must define the relation’s rule to determine both.
