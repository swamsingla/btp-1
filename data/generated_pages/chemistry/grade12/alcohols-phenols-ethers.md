# Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers

Alcohols, phenols, and ethers are fundamental classes of organic compounds that contain a hydroxyl (-OH) group bonded to an alkyl or aryl carbon atom. They are essential building blocks in the chemical industry, serving as solvents, antiseptics, and precursors to polymers like plastics and synthetic fibers. Understanding how to prepare them in the laboratory, predict their physical properties like boiling points and solubility, and manipulate their chemical reactivity is a cornerstone of organic chemistry.

## Preparation of Alcohols

In the laboratory, alcohols can be synthesized through several reliable chemical routes, each suited to different starting materials and desired products.

### From Alkenes
Alcohols can be prepared by reacting alkenes with water in the presence of an acid catalyst, a process known as acid-catalyzed hydration. When the alkene is unsymmetrical, the addition of water follows Markovnikov's rule, meaning the hydroxyl group attaches to the more substituted carbon atom. The mechanism proceeds in three steps: first, an electrophilic attack by a hydronium ion ($H_3O^+$) protonates the alkene to form a carbocation. Second, a water molecule acts as a nucleophile and attacks this carbocation. Finally, deprotonation of the resulting oxonium ion yields the alcohol.

As an alternative to acid-catalyzed hydration, hydroboration-oxidation can be used. In this two-step process, diborane ($(BH_3)_2$) adds across the carbon-carbon double bond. The boron atom attaches to the $sp^2$ carbon carrying the greater number of hydrogen atoms. This intermediate is then oxidized using hydrogen peroxide in the presence of aqueous sodium hydroxide. This method is highly valuable because it produces an alcohol that appears to have formed by the addition of water in a manner opposite to Markovnikov's rule, and it typically yields the product in excellent yield.

### From Carbonyl Compounds
Alcohols are readily produced by reducing aldehydes and ketones. Catalytic hydrogenation, which involves adding hydrogen in the presence of a finely divided metal catalyst such as platinum, palladium, or nickel, converts aldehydes into primary alcohols and ketones into secondary alcohols. Alternatively, these carbonyl compounds can be treated with reducing agents like sodium borohydride ($NaBH_4$) or lithium aluminium hydride ($LiAlH_4$) to yield the corresponding alcohols.

Carboxylic acids can also be reduced to primary alcohols. While $LiAlH_4$ is a strong reducing agent that accomplishes this in excellent yields, it is quite expensive and generally reserved for preparing special chemicals. Commercially, it is more economical to first convert the carboxylic acid into an ester, and then reduce the ester using hydrogen in the presence of a catalyst to obtain the primary alcohol.

### From Grignard Reagents
Alcohols can be synthesized by reacting Grignard reagents with aldehydes and ketones. The reaction begins with the nucleophilic addition of the Grignard reagent to the carbonyl group, forming an intermediate adduct. Subsequent hydrolysis of this adduct yields the final alcohol product.

The specific type of alcohol produced depends entirely on the carbonyl compound used. When methanal is used, the reaction yields a primary alcohol. If other aldehydes are used, secondary alcohols are produced. Finally, when ketones are reacted with Grignard reagents, the outcome is a tertiary alcohol.

## Physical Properties of Alcohols and Phenols

The chemical and physical behavior of alcohols and phenols is dictated by the presence of the hydroxyl (-OH) group, though the nature of the attached alkyl or aryl group modifies these properties.

### Boiling Points
The boiling points of both alcohols and phenols increase as the number of carbon atoms increases, which is due to a corresponding increase in van der Waals forces. Conversely, within alcohols, the boiling points decrease as the carbon chain becomes more branched, because branching reduces the surface area and thus weakens the van der Waals forces.

A defining characteristic of alcohols and phenols is their ability to form intermolecular hydrogen bonds. This strong attractive force significantly raises their boiling points compared to other classes of compounds with comparable molecular masses, such as hydrocarbons, ethers, and haloalkanes. For instance, ethanol and propane have very similar molecular masses, yet ethanol has a much higher boiling point.

### Solubility
Alcohols and phenols are soluble in water because they can form hydrogen bonds with water molecules. However, as the size of the alkyl or aryl group increases, the solubility in water decreases. This is because the larger alkyl group is hydrophobic, or water-fearing, and disrupts the hydrogen bonding network of the water. Several lower molecular mass alcohols are completely miscible with water in all proportions.

## Preparation of Phenols

While alcohols are derived from aliphatic compounds, phenols are aromatic compounds containing a hydroxyl group directly attached to a benzene ring. Phenol, also known as carbolic acid, was first isolated in the early nineteenth century from coal tar. Today, it is predominantly produced synthetically.

### From Haloarenes
Chlorobenzene can be converted to phenol by fusing it with sodium hydroxide at a high temperature of 623 K and a pressure of 320 atmospheres. This reaction produces sodium phenoxide, which must then be acidified to yield phenol.

### From Benzenesulphonic Acid
Phenol can also be prepared by first sulphonating benzene with oleum to produce benzene sulphonic acid. This acid is then heated with molten sodium hydroxide to form sodium phenoxide, which is subsequently acidified to give phenol.

### From Diazonium Salts
Another laboratory method involves the use of diazonium salts. An aromatic primary amine is treated with nitrous acid, generated in situ from sodium nitrite and hydrochloric acid at a temperature between 273 and 278 K, to form a diazonium salt. This salt is then hydrolyzed to produce phenol by warming it with water or by treating it with dilute acids.

### From Cumene
On an industrial scale, the vast majority of the world's phenol is manufactured from the hydrocarbon cumene (isopropylbenzene). Cumene is oxidized in the presence of air to form cumene hydroperoxide. This hydroperoxide is then treated with dilute acid, which cleaves the molecule to yield phenol and acetone. The acetone produced is a valuable by-product that is also obtained in large quantities through this specific reaction pathway.

## Acidity of Phenols

Phenols exhibit a distinct chemical property compared to alcohols: they are significantly more acidic. While alcohols are generally neutral, phenols can donate the hydrogen atom of their hydroxyl group to form a phenoxide ion ($C_6H_5O^-$).

This enhanced acidity arises from the ability of the phenoxide ion to stabilize the negative charge through resonance. The lone pair of electrons on the oxygen atom is delocalized into the aromatic benzene ring. This resonance stabilization makes it energetically favorable for the phenol to lose a proton. In contrast, the alkoxide ion formed from an alcohol lacks this extensive resonance stabilization, making alcohols far less acidic.

## Worked example

To illustrate the preparation of alcohols from alkenes, consider the acid-catalyzed hydration of propene. If propene, $\mathrm{CH_3-CH=CH_2}$, is reacted with water in the presence of a dilute sulphuric acid catalyst, the addition of water will follow Markovnikov's rule. The hydroxyl group will attach to the central carbon atom, yielding propan-2-ol.

## Key points

* Alcohols are prepared by acid-catalyzed hydration, hydroboration-oxidation, reduction of carbonyl compounds, or reactions with Grignard reagents.
* Acid-catalyzed hydration of unsymmetrical alkenes follows Markovnikov's rule, whereas hydroboration-oxidation follows anti-Markovnikov addition.
* Grignard reagents react with methanal to yield primary alcohols, with other aldehydes to yield secondary alcohols, and with ketones to yield tertiary alcohols.
* Phenols are prepared from haloarenes, benzenesulphonic acid, diazonium salts, or industrially from cumene.
* The acidity of phenols is greater than that of alcohols due to resonance stabilization of the resulting phenoxide ion.
