# Properties of Acids and Bases

Acids and bases are fundamental substances in chemistry. They interact with metals, carbonates, and each other in predictable ways, which helps us identify them and understand their behavior in everyday life and industry. Knowing these properties allows us to distinguish between acidic and basic materials safely.

## Reaction with Metals

When acids react with certain metals, they produce hydrogen gas. This is a classic chemical property that helps us identify acids in a laboratory setting. For example, if you add dilute sulphuric acid to zinc granules, the zinc displaces hydrogen atoms from the acid. This reaction releases hydrogen gas, which can be collected and tested. 

To test the gas, you can pass it through a soap solution to form bubbles. If you bring a burning candle near these bubbles, they will pop with a characteristic squeaky sound, confirming the presence of hydrogen. This same observation occurs with other acids like hydrochloric acid and ethanoic acid.

The general rule for this reaction is that an acid plus a metal produces a salt and hydrogen gas. You can write this as:
Acid + Metal → Salt + Hydrogen gas

For example, dilute sulphuric acid and zinc give zinc sulphate and hydrogen: $\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{SO}_4(\mathrm{aq}) + \mathrm{Zn(s)} \rightarrow \mathrm{ZnSO}_4(\mathrm{aq}) + \mathrm{H}_2(\mathrm{g})$.

Some bases also react with metals. For instance, when you add sodium hydroxide solution to zinc and warm the mixture, hydrogen gas is produced. The reaction is:
$2 \mathrm{NaOH(aq)} + \mathrm{Zn(s)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Na} _ {2} \mathrm{ZnO} _ {2} (\mathrm{s}) + \mathrm{H} _ {2} (\mathrm{g})$
This shows that some bases can also react with metals to release hydrogen, though this is not possible with all metals.

## Reaction with Carbonates

Acids also react vigorously with metal carbonates and metal hydrogencarbonates. If you place sodium carbonate in a test tube and add dilute hydrochloric acid, you will observe effervescence, meaning bubbles of gas are released. The same happens with sodium hydrogencarbonate.

To identify the gas, you can pass it through lime water, which is a solution of calcium hydroxide. The carbon dioxide produced turns the lime water milky due to the formation of a white precipitate of calcium carbonate. If you continue to pass excess carbon dioxide through the milky solution, it turns clear again because the precipitate reacts to form soluble calcium hydrogencarbonate.

The general equation for this reaction is:
Metal carbonate/Metal hydrogencarbonate + Acid → Salt + Carbon dioxide + Water

## Neutralisation Reactions

When an acid and a base are mixed, they react to form a salt and water. This process is called neutralisation because the effect of the acid is nullified by the base, and vice versa. 

You can observe this using an acid-base indicator like phenolphthalein. If you add phenolphthalein to a sodium hydroxide solution, it turns pink. As you add dilute hydrochloric acid drop by drop, the pink color disappears, indicating the base has been neutralised by the acid. If you then add a few drops of sodium hydroxide back to the mixture, the pink color reappears, showing the base has taken effect again.

The chemical equation for a neutralisation reaction is:
Base + Acid → Salt + Water

## Conductivity and Ions

Acids and bases conduct electricity when dissolved in water because they contain ions. You can test this by setting up a simple circuit with a battery, a bulb, and two nails in a cork. If you place the nails in a dilute hydrochloric acid solution and switch on the current, the bulb will glow. The same happens with dilute sulphuric acid.

However, if you use glucose or alcohol solutions, the bulb will not glow because these substances do not conduct electricity. The glow indicates a flow of electric current through the solution, which is carried by ions. Acids contain the $H^{+}$ ion as the cation, along with various anions like $Cl^{-}$, $NO_{3}^{-}$, $SO_{4}^{2-}$, or $CH_{3}COO^{-}$. This suggests that acids produce hydrogen ions, $H^{+}(aq)$, in solution, which are responsible for their acidic properties. In the same way, bases that dissolve in water (called alkalis) produce hydroxide ions, $OH^{-}(aq)$, in solution, and these ions are responsible for their basic properties.

## Acidic and Basic Oxides

Metallic oxides and non-metallic oxides also exhibit acidic or basic properties. Metallic oxides, like copper oxide, react with acids to form a salt and water. For example, when copper oxide is added to dilute hydrochloric acid, it dissolves, and the solution turns blue-green due to the formation of copper(II) chloride. The general reaction is:
Metal oxide + Acid → Salt + Water
Because metallic oxides behave like bases in this reaction, they are called basic oxides.

On the other hand, non-metallic oxides are acidic. For example, carbon dioxide, which is a non-metallic oxide, reacts with calcium hydroxide (a base) to produce a salt and water: $\mathrm{Ca(OH)}_2(\mathrm{aq}) + \mathrm{CO}_2(\mathrm{g}) \rightarrow \mathrm{CaCO}_3(\mathrm{s}) + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}(\mathrm{l})$. This behavior is similar to the reaction between a base and an acid, confirming that non-metallic oxides are acidic in nature.

## Worked example

Consider the reaction of sodium carbonate with hydrochloric acid. According to the rules, when a metal carbonate reacts with an acid, it produces a salt, carbon dioxide, and water. The specific reaction is:
$\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{CO}_3(\mathrm{s}) + 2\mathrm{HCl}(\mathrm{aq}) \rightarrow 2\mathrm{NaCl}(\mathrm{aq}) + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}(\mathrm{l}) + \mathrm{CO}_2(\mathrm{g})$
In this equation, the sodium carbonate and hydrochloric acid react to form sodium chloride (the salt), water, and carbon dioxide gas.

Reading the coefficients, 1 mole of sodium carbonate needs 2 moles of hydrochloric acid, and it gives 2 moles of sodium chloride, 1 mole of water and 1 mole of carbon dioxide. So if 3 moles of sodium carbonate react completely, 6 moles of hydrochloric acid are used and 3 moles of carbon dioxide gas are released. Passing this gas through lime water turns it milky, which confirms it is carbon dioxide.

## Key points

- Acid + Metal → Salt + Hydrogen gas; hydrogen burns with a squeaky pop.
- Metal carbonate or hydrogencarbonate + Acid → Salt + Carbon dioxide + Water; carbon dioxide turns lime water milky.
- Acid and base react to form a salt and water in a neutralisation reaction.
- Acids give hydrogen ions, $H^{+}(aq)$, and bases that dissolve in water give hydroxide ions, $OH^{-}(aq)$; both conduct electricity in solution because they contain ions.
- Metallic oxides are basic and react with acids to give a salt and water; non-metallic oxides are acidic and react with bases.
