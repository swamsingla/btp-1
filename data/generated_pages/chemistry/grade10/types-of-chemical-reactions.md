# Types of Chemical Reactions

A chemical reaction is the process where atoms break and form bonds to produce entirely new substances. During this process, the atoms of one element never change into the atoms of another element, nor do they disappear from the mixture or appear from elsewhere. Because of this, the total mass of the elements present in the products of a chemical reaction has to be equal to the total mass of the elements present in the reactants. This means the number of atoms of each element remains the same, before and after a chemical reaction. To keep track of these atoms and ensure the law of conservation of mass is followed, scientists use balanced chemical equations. Understanding the different ways these atoms rearrange themselves is the foundation of all chemistry, from the rusting of a bicycle chain to the burning of fuel in a car engine.

## Combination Reactions

A combination reaction, also known as a synthesis reaction, occurs when two or more simple substances, such as elements or compounds, combine to form a single, more complex product. In these reactions, the reactants join together to create one unified molecule. For example, when carbon in any of its allotropic forms burns in oxygen, it combines to form carbon dioxide. This reaction also releases heat and light. The chemical equation for this is $\mathrm{C} + \mathrm{O}_2\rightarrow \mathrm{CO}_2 + \text{heat and light}$. Another example is quicklime reacting with water to form slaked lime: $\mathrm{CaO(s)} + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O(l)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Ca(OH)}_2\mathrm{(aq)}$, which also releases a large amount of heat. In each of these reactions, several reactants merge into a single product. Burning methane ($\mathrm{CH}_4$) or ethanol is different: it gives two products, carbon dioxide and water, so it is not a combination reaction.

## Decomposition Reactions

A decomposition reaction is the exact opposite of a combination reaction. In a decomposition reaction, a single compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances. This usually happens when a compound is exposed to heat, electricity, or light. There are three common examples.

- Heat: when calcium carbonate (limestone) is heated, it decomposes into calcium oxide (quicklime) and carbon dioxide: $\mathrm{CaCO}_3\mathrm{(s)} \xrightarrow{\text{heat}} \mathrm{CaO(s)} + \mathrm{CO}_2\mathrm{(g)}$.
- Electricity: when an electric current is passed through water, it decomposes into hydrogen and oxygen: $2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O(l)} \xrightarrow{\text{electricity}} 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{(g)} + \mathrm{O}_2\mathrm{(g)}$.
- Light: silver chloride turns grey in sunlight because it decomposes into silver and chlorine: $2\mathrm{AgCl(s)} \xrightarrow{\text{sunlight}} 2\mathrm{Ag(s)} + \mathrm{Cl}_2\mathrm{(g)}$.

In each case a single compound breaks down into simpler substances.

## Displacement Reactions

In a displacement reaction, an element reacts with a compound and replaces one of the elements in that compound. This happens because the incoming element is more reactive than the element it is replacing. A classic example is the reaction between zinc and sulphuric acid. The word equation for this is Zinc + Sulphuric acid $\rightarrow$ Zinc sulphate + Hydrogen. The skeletal chemical equation is $\mathrm{Zn} + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{SO}_4 \rightarrow \mathrm{ZnSO}_4 + \mathrm{H}_2$. By examining the number of atoms on both sides of the arrow, we can see that the equation is already balanced. There is one zinc atom, two hydrogen atoms, one sulphur atom, and four oxygen atoms on both the left-hand side (reactants) and the right-hand side (products).

The reaction between iron and steam is also a displacement reaction, because iron displaces hydrogen from water. The skeletal equation is $\mathrm{Fe} + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \rightarrow \mathrm{Fe}_3\mathrm{O}_4 + \mathrm{H}_2$. By applying the hit-and-trial method, we find that the balanced equation is $3\mathrm{Fe(s)} + 4\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Fe}_3\mathrm{O}_4(\mathrm{s}) + 4\mathrm{H}_2(\mathrm{g})$. Here there are 3 iron atoms, 8 hydrogen atoms and 4 oxygen atoms on each side.

## Double Displacement Reactions

A double displacement reaction occurs when two compounds exchange ions or atoms to form two entirely new compounds. In this type of reaction, the positive ions of the two reactants switch places. For example, when barium chloride reacts with aluminium sulphate, the barium and aluminium swap places. The products formed are barium sulphate and aluminium chloride. This reaction takes place in an aqueous solution, meaning the reactants are dissolved in water. The barium sulphate formed is insoluble and settles as a white precipitate. When a double displacement reaction produces a precipitate, it is also called a precipitation reaction.

To write the balanced chemical equation, we must balance the number of atoms of each element on both sides of the arrow: $3\mathrm{BaCl}_2 + \mathrm{Al}_2(\mathrm{SO}_4)_3 \rightarrow 3\mathrm{BaSO}_4 + 2\mathrm{AlCl}_3$. There are 3 barium atoms, 6 chlorine atoms, 2 aluminium atoms, 3 sulphur atoms and 12 oxygen atoms on each side.

## Redox Reactions

Redox reactions involve the transfer of electrons between reactants, resulting in a change in the oxidation states of the elements involved. In terms of oxygen and hydrogen, a substance is oxidised when it gains oxygen or loses hydrogen, and it is reduced when it loses oxygen or gains hydrogen. Oxidation and reduction always take place together in a redox reaction.

Combustion is a common type of redox reaction. For example, when carbon burns in oxygen, it is oxidised, and the reaction releases a large amount of heat and light. The chemical equation for this is $\mathrm{C} + \mathrm{O}_2\rightarrow \mathrm{CO}_2 + \text{heat and light}$. Similarly, methane and ethanol also undergo combustion, releasing heat and light as they react with oxygen.

Another example is heating copper(II) oxide in a stream of hydrogen: $\mathrm{CuO} + \mathrm{H}_2 \rightarrow \mathrm{Cu} + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$. Copper(II) oxide loses oxygen, so it is reduced to copper. Hydrogen gains oxygen, so it is oxidised to water.

## Worked example

To apply the rules of balancing chemical equations, let us consider a new reaction. Suppose we have a reaction between hydrogen gas and chlorine gas to form hydrogen chloride gas. The skeletal equation is $\mathrm{H}_2 + \mathrm{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \mathrm{HCl}$. To balance this equation, we must ensure that the number of atoms of each element is equal on both sides of the arrow. We can see that there are two hydrogen atoms and two chlorine atoms on the left-hand side, but only one hydrogen atom and one chlorine atom on the right-hand side. To balance the equation, we must put a coefficient of 2 in front of the $\mathrm{HCl}$ on the right-hand side. The balanced equation is $\mathrm{H}_2 + \mathrm{Cl}_2 \rightarrow 2\mathrm{HCl}$.

## Key points

*   Chemical reactions involve the breaking and making of bonds between atoms to produce new substances.
*   The number of atoms of each element remains the same before and after a chemical reaction.
*   Combination reactions occur when two or more reactants combine to form a single product.
*   Decomposition reactions occur when a single compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances.
*   Displacement reactions occur when an element replaces another element in a compound.
*   Double displacement reactions occur when two compounds exchange ions or atoms to form two new compounds.
*   Redox reactions involve the transfer of electrons between reactants, resulting in a change in the oxidation states of the elements involved.
*   A substance that gains oxygen (or loses hydrogen) is oxidised, and a substance that loses oxygen (or gains hydrogen) is reduced; the two always happen together.
