# Covalent Bonding

You may already know that atoms are more stable when they have a completely filled outer shell of electrons, just like the noble gases. However, gaining or losing electrons is not always easy. In covalent bonding, atoms achieve stability by sharing electrons with each other. This sharing allows them to form molecules without creating charged ions, which explains why many carbon compounds are poor conductors of electricity and have low melting and boiling points.

## Why Atoms Share Electrons

Carbon has an atomic number of 6, which means it has four electrons in its outermost shell and needs four more to achieve a noble gas configuration. If carbon were to gain four electrons to form a $C^{4-}$ anion, the nucleus with six protons would struggle to hold onto ten electrons. Conversely, if it were to lose four electrons to form a $C^{4+}$ cation, it would require a large amount of energy to remove them, leaving behind a cation with six protons holding onto just two electrons. To overcome this problem, carbon and many other elements share their valence electrons. The shared electrons belong to the outermost shells of both atoms, allowing both to attain a noble gas configuration.

## Single, Double, and Triple Bonds

The number of electrons shared between two atoms determines the type of bond formed. When two hydrogen atoms share one pair of electrons, they form a single covalent bond, which can be represented by a single line between the atoms. 

In the case of oxygen, each atom has six electrons in its L shell and requires two more to complete its octet. Each oxygen atom shares two electrons with another oxygen atom, giving rise to two shared pairs of electrons. This constitutes a double bond between the two atoms. 

Similarly, in a diatomic molecule of nitrogen, each atom has five valence electrons and needs three more to complete its octet. Each nitrogen atom contributes three electrons, giving rise to three shared pairs of electrons. This constitutes a triple bond between the two nitrogen atoms.

## Drawing Lewis Structures

Lewis structures are diagrams that show the valence electrons of atoms in a molecule using dots. To draw a Lewis structure, you first determine the number of valence electrons each atom has. Then, you connect the atoms with single bonds and distribute the remaining electrons to satisfy the octet rule for each atom.

For example, let us consider the molecule of ammonia, $NH_{3}$. Nitrogen has five valence electrons and hydrogen has one valence electron. To satisfy the octet of nitrogen and the duet of hydrogen, nitrogen shares its electrons with three hydrogen atoms. This results in three single bonds and one lone pair of electrons on the nitrogen atom.

## Properties of Covalent Compounds

Covalently bonded molecules are seen to have strong bonds within the molecule, but intermolecular forces are weak. This gives rise to the low melting and boiling points of these compounds. Since the electrons are shared between atoms and no charged particles are formed, such covalent compounds are generally poor conductors of electricity.

## Worked Example: Drawing the Lewis Structure of Water

Water has the formula $H_{2}O$. Oxygen has six valence electrons and each hydrogen atom has one valence electron. To satisfy the octet of oxygen and the duet of hydrogen, the oxygen atom needs two more electrons to complete its octet and each hydrogen atom needs one more to complete its duet. The oxygen atom therefore shares one of its electrons with each of the two hydrogen atoms, and each hydrogen atom shares its single electron with oxygen. This results in two single bonds, one between the oxygen atom and each hydrogen atom. Oxygen is left with two lone pairs of electrons.

Check: the oxygen atom now has 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 8 electrons around it (two bonding pairs and two lone pairs), and each hydrogen atom has 2 electrons, so every atom has a completely filled outer shell.

## Key points

- In covalent bonding, atoms achieve a filled outer shell by sharing electrons, not by gaining or losing them.
- Carbon cannot easily gain or lose four electrons, so it shares its valence electrons.
- Sharing one, two or three pairs of electrons gives a single, double or triple bond, as in hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen molecules.
- Lewis structures use dots to show valence electrons; ammonia has three single bonds and one lone pair on nitrogen.
- Water has two single bonds, and two lone pairs on the oxygen atom.
- Covalent compounds have strong bonds within molecules but weak intermolecular forces, so they have low melting and boiling points and are generally poor conductors of electricity.
