# Carbon and Its Compounds

Carbon is an element that forms the basis of all known life, and it is found in millions of different compounds that we use every day. From the clothes we wear to the food we eat and the fuel we burn, carbon compounds are everywhere. In fact, chemists have estimated that the number of known carbon compounds is in the millions, which outnumbers the compounds formed by all other elements put together by a very large margin. This incredible abundance is not an accident. It is the direct result of two unique properties of carbon: its tetravalency and its ability to form long chains with itself, a process called catenation. These two features allow carbon to build a vast and complex molecular world, making it the backbone of organic chemistry.

## Tetravalency: The Four-Fold Bond

You may already know that atoms bond by sharing electrons to complete their outer shells. Carbon is no exception. With an atomic number of six, carbon has four electrons in its outer shell. To achieve a stable arrangement, it needs to share four more electrons. This means carbon has a valency of four, allowing it to form up to four covalent bonds with other atoms. Because of this tetravalency, a single carbon atom can connect to four other atoms. These other atoms can be other carbon atoms, or they can be elements like hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, or chlorine. This ability to bond with a wide variety of elements is what allows chemists to create millions of different molecules.

## Catenation: The Chain-Building Ability

While tetravalency explains how carbon connects to different atoms, catenation explains how carbon connects to itself. Catenation is the unique ability of carbon atoms to link together to form long chains, branched chains, or even rings. This self-linking property is incredibly strong because the carbon-carbon bond is very strong, making the resulting compounds exceptionally stable. No other element exhibits catenation to the extent that carbon does. For example, silicon can form chains of up to seven or eight atoms, but these compounds are highly reactive and unstable compared to carbon. Because carbon chains can be long, branched, or cyclic, the number of possible arrangements becomes enormous, leading to the millions of known carbon compounds.

## Saturated and Unsaturated Compounds

When carbon atoms are linked together, they can use different types of bonds. If the carbon atoms are connected only by single bonds, the resulting compound is called a saturated compound. In saturated compounds, all the valencies of the carbon atoms are satisfied. For example, in ethane ($C_{2}H_{6}$), the two carbon atoms are linked by a single bond, and each carbon is also bonded to three hydrogen atoms. Because all the valencies are satisfied by single bonds, these compounds are normally not very reactive.

However, carbon atoms can also be linked by double or triple bonds. When carbon compounds have double or triple bonds between their carbon atoms, they are called unsaturated compounds. For instance, ethene ($C_{2}H_{4}$) has a double bond between the two carbon atoms, and ethyne ($C_{2}H_{2}$) has a triple bond. These unsaturated compounds are more reactive than saturated compounds because the multiple bonds can easily break to form new compounds.

## Naming Carbon Compounds

To manage the millions of carbon compounds, chemists use a systematic naming method. The names of compounds in a homologous series are based on the name of the basic carbon chain, modified by a prefix or a suffix indicating the nature of the functional group. For example, the alcohols with one, two, three and four carbon atoms are called methanol, ethanol, propanol, and butanol.

The method for naming a carbon compound involves the following steps. First, identify the number of carbon atoms in the compound. A compound having three carbon atoms would have the name propane. Second, if a functional group is present, it is indicated in the name of the compound with either a prefix or a suffix. Third, if the name of the functional group is to be given as a suffix, and the suffix of the functional group begins with a vowel a, e, i, o, u, then the name of the carbon chain is modified by deleting the final ‘e’ and adding the appropriate suffix. For example, a three-carbon chain with a ketone group would be named in the following manner – Propane – ‘e’ = propan + ‘one’ = propanone.

If the carbon chain is unsaturated, then the final ‘ane’ in the name of the carbon chain is substituted by ‘ene’ or ‘yne’. For example, a three-carbon chain with a double bond would be called propene, and if it has a triple bond, it would be called propyne.

## Worked Example

Let us apply the rules of nomenclature to a practical example. Suppose we have a compound with a four-carbon chain and a hydroxyl group (-ol) attached to the first carbon. According to the rules, a four-carbon chain would be named butane. Since the functional group is an alcohol, we use the suffix -ol. This suffix begins with the vowel 'o', so we delete the final 'e' of the chain name: butane – 'e' = butan + 'ol' = butanol.

Now, suppose we have a compound with a three-carbon chain and a double bond between the first and second carbon atoms. According to the rules, a three-carbon chain with a double bond would be called propene.

## Key points

- Carbon has a valency of four, allowing it to form four covalent bonds with other atoms.
- Catenation is the unique ability of carbon atoms to link together to form long chains, branched chains, or rings.
- Saturated compounds have only single bonds between carbon atoms and are normally not very reactive.
- Unsaturated compounds have double or triple bonds between carbon atoms and are more reactive than saturated compounds.
- The names of carbon compounds are based on the basic carbon chain, modified by a prefix or a suffix indicating the functional group.
