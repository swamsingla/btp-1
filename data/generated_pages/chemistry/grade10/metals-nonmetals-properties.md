# Properties of Metals and Non-metals

Metals and non-metals are the two broad categories into which most elements are grouped based on their physical and chemical characteristics. Understanding these properties allows us to select the right material for everyday tasks, from wiring a house to manufacturing cooking vessels. This page explores the defining physical traits of metals and non-metals, and examines how they behave chemically when exposed to air, water, and other substances.

## Metallic Lustre and Hardness

The most immediate way to distinguish a metal from a non-metal is by looking at its surface. When you examine a piece of iron, copper, aluminium, or magnesium, you will notice that they possess a shining surface in their pure state. This property is called metallic lustre. You can observe this shine clearly by cleaning the surface of the sample with sandpaper, which removes any dirt or tarnish. However, not all metals are hard. While metals are generally hard, the hardness varies from metal to metal. For instance, alkali metals like sodium and potassium are so soft that they can be cut with a sharp knife. In fact, when you hold a piece of sodium with tongs and press it between the folds of a filter paper to dry it, you can easily slice it with a knife. On the other hand, non-metals typically do not exhibit this shiny appearance and are not as hard.

## Malleability and Ductility

Two of the most useful physical properties of metals are malleability and ductility. Malleability is the property that allows a material to be beaten into thin sheets. If you take pieces of iron, zinc, lead, or copper and strike them on a block of iron with a hammer, you will observe that some metals can be beaten into thin sheets. This property is called malleability. Did you know that gold and silver are the most malleable metals? Similarly, ductility is the ability of a material to be drawn into thin wires. You may have seen wires of copper and aluminium in daily life, and this is because of their ductility. Gold is the most ductile metal. It is quite surprising to learn that a wire of about 2 km length can be drawn from just one gram of gold. It is because of their malleability and ductility that metals can be given different shapes according to our needs.

## Conductivity of Heat and Electricity

Metals are excellent conductors of heat and electricity, which makes them indispensable in both household and industrial applications. To understand this, consider an activity where an aluminium or copper wire is clamped on a stand, with a pin fixed to its free end using wax. When the wire is heated near the clamped end with a spirit lamp or a burner, you will observe that the pin drops off after some time, but the metal wire itself does not melt. This activity shows that metals are good conductors of heat and have high melting points. The best conductors of heat are silver and copper. However, lead and mercury are comparatively poor conductors of heat.

Metals also conduct electricity very well. If you set up an electric circuit and place a metal sample between the terminals, the bulb will glow, indicating that the metal conducts electricity. This is why the wires that carry current in your homes are made of metals. However, you may have noticed that these wires have a coating of polyvinylchloride (PVC) or a rubber-like material. This coating is an insulator and is applied to prevent electric shocks, as the metal core is a good conductor.

## Sonority

Another distinct physical property of metals is sonority, which is the ability to produce a sound when struck against a hard surface. Metals that possess this property are said to be sonorous. This characteristic is the reason why school bells are made of metals rather than non-metals.

## Chemical Properties: Reaction with Air

While physical properties help us group elements, chemical properties provide a clearer distinction between metals and non-metals. When metals are burnt in air, they react with oxygen to form metal oxides. For example, when copper is heated in air, it combines with oxygen to form copper(II) oxide, which is a black oxide. The chemical equation for this reaction is $2 \mathrm{Cu} + \mathrm{O} _ {2} \rightarrow 2 \mathrm{CuO}$. Similarly, aluminium forms aluminium oxide according to the equation $4 \mathrm{Al} \quad + \quad 3 \mathrm{O} _ {2} \rightarrow 2 \mathrm{Al} _ {2} \mathrm{O} _ {3}$.

Almost all metals combine with oxygen to form metal oxides. However, different metals show different reactivities towards oxygen. Metals such as potassium and sodium react so vigorously that they catch fire if kept in the open. Therefore, to protect them and prevent accidental fires, they are kept immersed in kerosene oil. At ordinary temperatures, the surfaces of metals such as magnesium, aluminium, zinc, and lead are covered with a thin layer of oxide. This protective oxide layer prevents the metal from further oxidation. Iron does not burn on heating, but iron filings burn vigorously when sprinkled in the flame of a burner. Copper does not burn, but the hot metal gets coated with a black layer of copper(II) oxide. Silver and gold do not react with oxygen even at high temperatures.

## Chemical Properties: Reaction with Water

Metals also react with water to produce a metal hydroxide or a metal oxide, along with hydrogen gas. The reactivity of a metal with water depends on its position in the reactivity series. Potassium and sodium react violently with cold water. The reaction is so violent and exothermic that the evolved hydrogen immediately catches fire. The chemical equations for these reactions are $2 \mathrm{K(s)} + 2 \mathrm {H_ {2} O(l)} \rightarrow 2 \mathrm{KOH(aq)} + \mathrm {H_ {2} (g)} + \text{heat energy}$ and $2 \mathrm{Na(s)} + 2 \mathrm {H_ {2} O(l)} \rightarrow 2 \mathrm{NaOH(aq)} + \mathrm {H_ {2} (g)} + \text{heat energy}$.

The reaction of calcium with water is less violent, and the heat evolved is not sufficient for the hydrogen to catch fire. The equation for this reaction is $\mathrm{Ca(s)} + 2 \mathrm{H} _ {2} \mathrm{O(l)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Ca(OH)} _ {2} (\mathrm{aq}) + \mathrm{H} _ {2} (\mathrm{g})$. Calcium starts floating because the bubbles of hydrogen gas formed stick to the surface of the metal.

Magnesium does not react with cold water but reacts with hot water to form magnesium hydroxide and hydrogen. It also starts floating due to the bubbles of hydrogen gas sticking to its surface. Metals like aluminium, iron, and zinc do not react with cold or hot water, but they do react with steam to form the metal oxide and hydrogen. The equations for these reactions are $2 \mathrm{Al(s)} + 3 \mathrm {H_ {2} O(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm {Al_ {2} O_ {3} (s)} + 3 \mathrm {H_ {2} (g)}$ and $3 \mathrm{Fe(s)} + 4 \mathrm {H_ {2} O(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm {Fe_ {3} O_ {4} (s)} + 4 \mathrm {H_ {2} (g)}$. Metals such as lead, copper, silver, and gold do not react with water at all.

## Chemical Properties: Nature of Oxides

The chemical behaviour of metals and non-metals also differs in the nature of the oxides they form. Most non-metals produce acidic oxides when dissolved in water. On the other hand, most metals give rise to basic oxides. For example, when magnesium is burnt, the ashes formed are dissolved in water and tested with litmus paper, showing that the product is basic. Similarly, when sulphur powder is burnt, the fumes produced are dissolved in water and tested with litmus paper, showing that the product is acidic.

Most metal oxides are insoluble in water, but some dissolve in water to form alkalis. Sodium oxide and potassium oxide dissolve in water to produce alkalis as follows: $\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{O}(\mathrm{s}) + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}(\mathrm{l}) \rightarrow 2\mathrm{NaOH}(\mathrm{aq})$ and $\mathrm{K}_2\mathrm{O}(\mathrm{s}) \quad + \quad \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}(\mathrm{l}) \to 2\mathrm{KOH}(\mathrm{aq})$.

Some metal oxides show both basic and acidic behaviour. Aluminium oxide and zinc oxide react with acids as well as with bases to give salts and water. Such oxides are called amphoteric oxides. For example, aluminium oxide reacts with hydrochloric acid as a base: $\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3 + 6\mathrm{HCl} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{AlCl}_3 + 3\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$. It reacts with sodium hydroxide as an acid: $\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3 + 2\mathrm{NaOH} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{NaAlO}_2 + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$.

## Worked Example

A student is designing a new type of cooking vessel and needs to choose a material that can be shaped easily into a thin sheet, will not melt when placed on a hot stove, and will distribute heat evenly. 

First, the material must be malleable to allow it to be beaten into a thin sheet. Gold and silver are the most malleable metals, but they are expensive. A more practical choice is aluminium, which is known to be malleable.

Second, the material must have a high melting point so it does not melt when heated. Metals have high melting points, and the best conductors of heat are silver and copper. Aluminium is also a good conductor of heat and has a high melting point, making it suitable for cooking vessels.

Third, the material must conduct heat well to ensure even cooking. Metals are good conductors of heat. Therefore, the student should choose aluminium or copper for the cooking vessel, as they are malleable, have high melting points, and conduct heat efficiently.

## Key points
*   Metals have a shining surface called metallic lustre, but their hardness varies; some like sodium are very soft.
*   Malleability is the property of being beaten into thin sheets, while ductility is the ability to be drawn into thin wires.
*   Metals are good conductors of heat and electricity, which is why they are used in wires and cooking vessels.
*   Metals that produce a sound when struck are said to be sonorous, which is why school bells are made of metals.
*   When burnt in air, most metals react with oxygen to form metal oxides.
*   Metals react with water to produce a metal hydroxide or metal oxide and hydrogen gas, with the reactivity varying from violent to no reaction.
*   Most metal oxides are basic in nature, while most non-metal oxides are acidic.
*   Some metal oxides, such as aluminium oxide and zinc oxide, are amphoteric and react with both acids and bases.
