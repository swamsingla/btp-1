# Reactivity Series

The reactivity series is a list of metals arranged in the order of their decreasing activities. It helps us understand how easily metals react with other substances. This ordering is crucial because it allows us to predict whether a metal will displace another from a compound. It also determines how we extract metals from their natural ores and how they behave in everyday chemical reactions.

## The Order of Reactivity

The reactivity series arranges metals from the most reactive to the least reactive. The most reactive metal at the top of the list is potassium (K). The least reactive metal at the bottom is gold (Au). Between these extremes are sodium (Na), calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), aluminium (Al), zinc (Zn), iron (Fe), lead (Pb), hydrogen ([H]), copper (Cu), mercury (Hg), and silver (Ag). As you move down the list, the reactivity of the metals decreases. This means a metal higher up in the list will displace a metal lower down from its salt solution. For example, a metal like zinc will displace iron from iron(II) sulphate, but copper cannot displace zinc from zinc sulphate.

## Displacement Reactions

A displacement reaction occurs when a more reactive metal replaces a less reactive metal in a compound. These reactions are a direct consequence of the reactivity series. If a metal is more reactive, it will push a less reactive metal out of its salt solution. Consider the experiment where samples of four metals, A, B, C, and D, are added to different solutions. If metal A causes a displacement in copper(II) sulphate but not in iron(II) sulphate, it is less reactive than iron but more reactive than copper. Conversely, if metal C displaces silver from silver nitrate but does not react with iron(II) sulphate, it is less reactive than iron but more reactive than silver. Using these observations, we can arrange the metals in order of decreasing reactivity.

## Extracting Highly Reactive Metals

Metals high up in the reactivity series, such as sodium, magnesium, calcium, and aluminium, are extremely reactive. Because they have a stronger affinity for oxygen than carbon, they cannot be extracted from their oxides by simply heating them with carbon. Instead, we must use a more powerful reducing method called electrolytic reduction. In this process, the metal compound is melted and an electric current is passed through it. The metal ions gain electrons at the cathode (the negatively charged electrode) and are deposited as pure metal. Meanwhile, the non-metal ions lose electrons at the anode (the positively charged electrode) and form a gas. For example, in the electrolysis of molten sodium chloride, sodium ions gain electrons to form sodium metal at the cathode, while chloride ions lose electrons to form chlorine gas at the anode.

## Extracting Moderately Reactive Metals

Metals in the middle of the activity series, such as iron, zinc, lead, and copper, are moderately reactive. They are commonly found in nature as sulphides or carbonates, but it is much easier to extract a metal from its oxide. Therefore, before the actual extraction, the ores must be converted into oxides. Sulphide ores are converted into oxides by heating them strongly in the presence of excess air. This process is known as roasting. For instance, when zinc sulphide is roasted, it reacts with oxygen to form zinc oxide and sulphur dioxide gas. Carbonate ores are changed into oxides by heating them strongly in limited air. This process is called calcination. When zinc carbonate is calcined, it decomposes into zinc oxide and carbon dioxide gas.

Once the ores are converted to oxides, the metal oxides are reduced to the corresponding metals using a suitable reducing agent, typically carbon. When zinc oxide is heated with carbon, it is reduced to metallic zinc and carbon monoxide gas. Besides using carbon, displacement reactions can also be used to extract these metals. Highly reactive metals like aluminium can displace less reactive metals from their compounds. This reaction is highly exothermic, meaning it releases a large amount of heat. The amount of heat evolved is so large that the metals are produced in the molten state. This principle is used in the thermit reaction, where aluminium powder is heated with iron(III) oxide to produce molten iron.

## Extracting Unreactive Metals

Metals low in the activity series are very unreactive. The oxides of these metals can be reduced to metals by simply heating them alone, without the need for any reducing agent. For example, cinnabar is an ore of mercury. When cinnabar is heated in air, it is first converted into mercuric oxide. Mercuric oxide is then reduced to mercury on further heating. Similarly, copper is found as copper(I) sulphide in nature and can be obtained from its ore by just heating in air.

## Worked example

Let us apply the rule of displacement reactions to a practical scenario. Suppose you have four unknown metals, A, B, C, and D, and you add each of them to solutions of iron(II) sulphate, copper(II) sulphate, and silver nitrate. You make these observations.

- Metal A displaces iron from iron(II) sulphate, so A is more reactive than iron.
- Metal B displaces copper from copper(II) sulphate but does not react with iron(II) sulphate, so B is less reactive than iron but more reactive than copper.
- Metal C displaces silver from silver nitrate but does not react with copper(II) sulphate, so C is less reactive than copper but more reactive than silver.
- Metal D does not react with any of the three solutions, so it is less reactive than silver.

By combining these observations, we can arrange the metals in order of decreasing reactivity: A, B, C, D.

## Key points
- The reactivity series is a list of metals arranged in the order of their decreasing activities.
- The most reactive metal is potassium (K) and the least reactive is gold (Au).
- A more reactive metal can displace a less reactive metal from its salt solution.
- Highly reactive metals like sodium and aluminium are extracted by electrolytic reduction of their molten compounds.
- Moderately reactive metals like zinc and iron are extracted from their oxides using carbon after roasting or calcination.
- Unreactive metals like mercury are obtained from their ores by simple heating; copper can also be obtained from its sulphide ore by heating in air.
