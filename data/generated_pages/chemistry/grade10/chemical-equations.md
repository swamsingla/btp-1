# Chemical Equations

A chemical equation is a short, symbolic way to show what happens during a chemical reaction. It tells us which substances are the reactants that start the reaction and which are the products that form at the end. Understanding how to write and balance these equations is a fundamental skill in chemistry because it allows us to predict the amounts of products formed and the quantities of reactants needed.

## The Law of Conservation of Mass

The foundation of writing chemical equations comes from the law of conservation of mass. This law states that mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. In other words, the total mass of the elements present in the products of a chemical reaction has to be equal to the total mass of the elements present in the reactants. Consequently, the number of atoms of each element remains the same, before and after a chemical reaction.

## Symbols and Notation

To write chemical equations concisely, we use chemical formulae instead of words. The gaseous, liquid, aqueous, and solid states of reactants and products are represented by the notations (g), (l), (aq), and (s), respectively. The word aqueous (aq) is written if the reactant or product is present as a solution in water. For example, the burning of magnesium in air can be represented by the skeletal chemical equation $\mathrm{Mg} + \mathrm{O} _ {2} \rightarrow \mathrm{MgO}$.

## Balancing Chemical Equations

A skeletal chemical equation is written without coefficients, and it is often unbalanced. To balance a chemical equation, we must ensure that the number of atoms of each element is the same on both sides of the arrow. This is done using the hit-and-trial method, where we use the smallest whole number coefficient.

### The Step-by-Step Method

To balance a chemical equation, first draw boxes around each formula. Do not change anything inside the boxes while balancing the equation. Next, list the number of atoms of different elements present in the unbalanced equation. It is often convenient to start balancing with the compound that contains the maximum number of atoms. In that compound, select the element which has the maximum number of atoms.

Using these criteria, we select the compound with the most atoms and the element with the most atoms within it. For example, to balance the equation $\mathrm{Fe} + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \rightarrow \mathrm{Fe}_3\mathrm{O}_4 + \mathrm{H}_2$, we select $\mathrm{Fe}_3\mathrm{O}_4$ and the element oxygen in it. There are four oxygen atoms on the RHS and only one on the LHS. To balance the oxygen atoms, we put a coefficient of 4 in front of $\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$, making it $4\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$. We cannot alter the formulae of the compounds or elements involved in the reactions.

Next, we balance the hydrogen atoms. To equalise the number of H atoms, we make the number of molecules of hydrogen as four on the RHS. Finally, we balance the remaining element, which is iron. To equalise Fe, we take three atoms of Fe on the LHS. The balanced equation is $3\mathrm{Fe} + 4\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \rightarrow \mathrm{Fe}_3\mathrm{O}_4 + 4\mathrm{H}_2$.

### Checking the Balance

Finally, to check the correctness of the balanced equation, we count atoms of each element on both sides of the equation. If the numbers of atoms of elements on both sides are equal, the equation is now balanced.

## Writing Symbols of Physical States

To make a chemical equation more informative, the physical states of the reactants and products are mentioned along with their chemical formulae. Usually physical states are not included in a chemical equation unless it is necessary to specify them. Sometimes the reaction conditions, such as temperature, pressure, catalyst, etc., for the reaction are indicated above and/or below the arrow in the equation.

## Worked Example

Consider the reaction between zinc and sulphuric acid. The word-equation is -

$$
\text{Zinc + Sulphuric acid} \rightarrow \text{Zinc sulphate + Hydrogen}
$$

The above word-equation may be represented by the following chemical equation –

$$
\mathrm{Zn} + \mathrm{H} _ {2} \mathrm{SO} _ {4} \rightarrow \mathrm{ZnSO} _ {4} + \mathrm{H} _ {2}
$$

Let us examine the number of atoms of different elements on both sides of the arrow.

| Element | Number of atoms in reactants (LHS) | Number of atoms in products (RHS) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zn | 1 | 1 |
| H | 2 | 2 |
| S | 1 | 1 |
| O | 4 | 4 |

As the number of atoms of each element is the same on both sides of the arrow, this equation is already a balanced chemical equation, so no coefficients are needed.

## Key points

- A chemical equation represents a chemical reaction using chemical formulae.
- The law of conservation of mass states that the total mass of the elements present in the products of a chemical reaction has to be equal to the total mass of the elements present in the reactants.
- The number of atoms of each element remains the same, before and after a chemical reaction.
- To balance a chemical equation, use the hit-and-trial method with the smallest whole number coefficient.
- The gaseous, liquid, aqueous, and solid states of reactants and products are represented by the notations (g), (l), (aq), and (s), respectively.
- Physical states are usually not included in a chemical equation unless it is necessary to specify them.
