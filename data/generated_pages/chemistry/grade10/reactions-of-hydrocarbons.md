# Reactions of Hydrocarbons

Hydrocarbons are compounds made entirely of hydrogen and carbon atoms. They are the main components of fuels like coal, petroleum, and natural gas. Understanding how these compounds react is essential because most of the energy we use every day comes from burning them. This page explores the chemical properties of these carbon compounds, focusing on how they react with oxygen, add new atoms, and undergo substitution.

## Combustion
Combustion is an oxidation reaction where a substance reacts rapidly with oxygen, releasing heat and light. Since most fuels we use are carbon or its compounds, studying combustion is the first step in understanding their chemical properties. 

In a complete combustion reaction, a carbon compound reacts with oxygen to produce carbon dioxide and water, along with a large amount of heat and light. For example, methane, a simple hydrocarbon, burns in oxygen according to the following equation:
$$
\mathrm{CH}_4 + 2\mathrm{O}_2\rightarrow \mathrm{CO}_2 + 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} + \text{heat and light}
$$
Here one carbon atom, four hydrogen atoms and four oxygen atoms are present on each side, so the equation is balanced.

When you observe the flame during combustion, the colour and presence of smoke tell you a lot about the fuel and the reaction. Saturated hydrocarbons generally burn with a clean, blue flame. Unsaturated carbon compounds, on the other hand, burn with a yellow, sooty flame that produces a lot of black smoke. This yellow colour is caused by glowing carbon particles, which are a result of incomplete combustion. If you place a metal plate above the flame, this soot will deposit a black layer on the plate.

Incomplete combustion happens when there is a limiting supply of air. Even saturated hydrocarbons will produce a sooty flame if they do not have enough oxygen. This is why gas stoves and kerosene stoves at home have inlets for air. These inlets ensure a sufficiently oxygen-rich mixture is burnt to give a clean blue flame. If you ever observe the bottoms of cooking vessels getting blackened, it means the air holes are blocked and the fuel is getting wasted.

Sometimes, substances burn without a flame. If you observe a coal or wood fire in an *angithi*, you will notice that the charcoal sometimes just glows red and gives out heat without a visible flame. A flame is only produced when gaseous substances burn. When wood or charcoal is ignited, the volatile substances present inside it vapourise and burn with a flame in the beginning.

Fuels such as coal and petroleum contain some amount of nitrogen and sulphur. The combustion of these fuels results in the formation of oxides of sulphur and nitrogen. These oxides are major pollutants in the environment.

## Oxidation
Oxidation is another important chemical property of carbon compounds. You have already learned about oxidation reactions in earlier lessons. Carbon compounds can be easily oxidised on combustion. However, there are other reactions where alcohols are converted to carboxylic acids in addition to complete oxidation.

You can test for oxidation by taking about 3 mL of ethanol in a test tube and warming it gently in a water bath. Then, add a 5% solution of alkaline potassium permanganate drop by drop to this solution. You will notice that the purple colour of the potassium permanganate disappears as it reacts with the ethanol. However, if you continue to add excess potassium permanganate, the colour will persist.

The reason the colour of potassium permanganate does not disappear when excess is added is that the ethanol has been completely converted to the carboxylic acid, ethanoic acid (acetic acid). At this point, there is no more ethanol left to react with the oxidising agent.

Substances that are capable of adding oxygen to other substances are known as oxidising agents. Alkaline potassium permanganate and acidified potassium dichromate are common examples of oxidising agents. They oxidise alcohols to acids by adding oxygen to the starting material.

## Addition Reactions
Addition reactions occur in unsaturated hydrocarbons. In these reactions, atoms or groups of atoms are added to a molecule to form a new product. Unsaturated hydrocarbons add hydrogen in the presence of catalysts such as palladium or nickel to give saturated hydrocarbons.

Catalysts are substances that cause a reaction to occur or proceed at a different rate without the reaction itself being affected. This reaction is commonly used in the hydrogenation of vegetable oils using a nickel catalyst. Vegetable oils generally have long unsaturated carbon chains, while animal fats have saturated carbon chains.

For example, ethene adds hydrogen to give ethane:
$$
\mathrm{CH}_2 = \mathrm{CH}_2 + \mathrm{H}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Nickel catalyst}} \mathrm{CH}_3 - \mathrm{CH}_3
$$
The double bond of ethene becomes a single bond, and each carbon atom gains one hydrogen atom.

You must have seen advertisements stating that some vegetable oils are 'healthy'. Animal fats generally contain saturated fatty acids, which are said to be harmful for health. Oils containing unsaturated fatty acids should be chosen for cooking.

## Substitution Reactions
Saturated hydrocarbons are fairly unreactive and are inert in the presence of most reagents. However, in the presence of sunlight, chlorine is added to hydrocarbons in a very fast reaction. In this type of reaction, chlorine can replace the hydrogen atoms one by one. It is called a substitution reaction because one type of atom or a group of atoms takes the place of another.

A number of products are usually formed with the higher homologues of alkanes. For example, methane reacts with chlorine in the presence of sunlight to form chloromethane and hydrogen chloride:
$$
\mathrm{CH}_4 + \mathrm{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{Cl} + \mathrm{HCl} \quad \text{(in the presence of sunlight)}
$$

## Worked Example
Let us apply the rule of combustion to a specific hydrocarbon. Consider the complete combustion of ethane ($\mathrm{C}_2\mathrm{H}_6$) in oxygen. According to the rules of combustion, a hydrocarbon reacts with oxygen to produce carbon dioxide and water, along with heat and light.

The unbalanced equation is:
$$
\mathrm{C}_2\mathrm{H}_6 + \mathrm{O}_2 \rightarrow \mathrm{CO}_2 + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} + \text{heat and light}
$$

To balance this equation, we count the number of atoms of each element on both sides of the equation. On the left side, we have 2 carbon atoms and 6 hydrogen atoms. On the right side, we have 1 carbon atom in $\mathrm{CO}_2$ and 2 hydrogen atoms in $\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$. To balance the carbon atoms, we place a coefficient of 2 in front of $\mathrm{CO}_2$. To balance the hydrogen atoms, we place a coefficient of 3 in front of $\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$.

The equation becomes:
$$
\mathrm{C}_2\mathrm{H}_6 + \mathrm{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\mathrm{CO}_2 + 3\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} + \text{heat and light}
$$

Now, we count the oxygen atoms on the right side. We have 4 oxygen atoms in $2\mathrm{CO}_2$ and 3 oxygen atoms in $3\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$, for a total of 7 oxygen atoms. To balance the oxygen atoms, we place a coefficient of $\frac{7}{2}$ in front of $\mathrm{O}_2$.

The final balanced equation is:
$$
\mathrm{C}_2\mathrm{H}_6 + \frac{7}{2}\mathrm{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\mathrm{CO}_2 + 3\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} + \text{heat and light}
$$

Since a fraction is not usually written as a coefficient, multiply every term by 2 to get whole numbers:
$$
2\mathrm{C}_2\mathrm{H}_6 + 7\mathrm{O}_2 \rightarrow 4\mathrm{CO}_2 + 6\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} + \text{heat and light}
$$
Check: the left side has 4 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 14 oxygen atoms, and the right side has 4 carbon, 12 hydrogen and $8 + 6 = 14$ oxygen atoms.

## Key points
*   Combustion is an oxidation reaction where a substance reacts with oxygen, releasing heat and light.
*   Saturated hydrocarbons burn with a clean blue flame, while unsaturated carbon compounds burn with a yellow, sooty flame.
*   Oxidising agents, such as alkaline potassium permanganate, add oxygen to substances like alcohols, converting them to carboxylic acids.
*   Addition reactions occur in unsaturated hydrocarbons, where hydrogen is added in the presence of a catalyst like nickel.
*   Substitution reactions occur in saturated hydrocarbons in the presence of sunlight, where chlorine replaces hydrogen atoms.
