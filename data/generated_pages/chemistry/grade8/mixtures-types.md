# Mixtures — Types and Properties

Mixtures are everywhere in our daily lives, from the food we eat to the air we breathe. A mixture is formed when two or more substances are combined, and each substance keeps its original properties. The individual substances that make up a mixture are called its components, and these components do not react chemically with each other. Understanding mixtures is important because it helps us separate useful substances and understand the world around us.

## Uniform and Non-Uniform Mixtures

Mixtures can be divided into two main categories based on how evenly their components are spread. Uniform mixtures are those where the components are evenly distributed and cannot be distinguished from each other, even with a microscope. A great example of a uniform mixture is sugar dissolved in water. In this case, the sugar particles are completely mixed into the water and cannot be seen separately.

On the other hand, non-uniform mixtures have components that are generally visible with the naked eye or with a magnifying device. The components in these mixtures are not evenly spread out. A sprout salad made with green gram, chickpeas, onion, and tomato is a perfect example of a non-uniform mixture. You can easily see the individual pieces of vegetables and legumes in the salad.

## Air as a Mixture

Air is a mixture that you encounter every single day, yet it is often overlooked. Air is actually a uniform mixture of mainly nitrogen, oxygen, argon, carbon dioxide, and water vapour. Out of these, oxygen is required by most living beings to stay alive, and it also helps in combustion. Nitrogen, which makes up about 78% of the air, does not take part in combustion. 

Air also contains water vapour. When warm air touches a cool surface, the water vapour in the air turns into liquid water, forming tiny droplets. You may have seen this as droplets on the outside of a cold glass of water.

## Suspensions in Air

Air can also contain suspended particles that are not part of the main mixture. Dust particles are often suspended in the air. These dust particles are not an integral part of the air and are considered pollutants. The nature and the number of dust particles in the air may vary from time to time and from place to place.

You can easily observe this by leaving a clean black sheet of paper undisturbed near an open window or in the garden for a few hours. After a while, you will notice tiny particles settled on the surface of the paper. You can use a magnifying glass to examine these particles more closely.

## Types of Mixtures by State

Mixtures can also be classified based on the physical state of their components. In everyday speech, the components of a mixture may themselves be mixtures, as in poha and sprout salad, or pure substances like sugar dissolved in water. In science, however, all the components of a mixture must be pure substances only. For instance, a mixture of gas and gas is air, which is uniform. A mixture of gas and liquid can be seen in aerated water, also known as soda water. 

Other examples include carbon particles in air, which is a solid and gas mixture. Liquid and liquid mixtures include acetic acid in water, which is vinegar. Sand in water and seawater are examples of solid and liquid mixtures. Finally, solid and solid mixtures include baking powder, which is a mixture of baking soda and tartaric acid, and alloys.

## Separation of Mixtures

Separation of mixtures is a process done to obtain the pure substances that make up the mixture. In everyday life, we often separate mixtures to obtain the component of interest and discard the rest. However, in science, the purpose of separating a mixture is to obtain pure substances.

## Worked Example

Let us consider a mixture of sugar and water. This is a uniform mixture because the sugar particles are evenly distributed and cannot be seen separately. If we were to separate this mixture, the goal would be to obtain pure sugar and pure water. Sugar and water are both pure substances, so this mixture fits the scientific meaning of a mixture.

## Key points

- A mixture is formed when two or more substances are combined; each substance retains its properties and the components do not react chemically with each other.
- In a uniform mixture the components are evenly distributed and cannot be distinguished; in a non-uniform mixture they are visible with the naked eye or a magnifying device.
- Air is a uniform mixture of nitrogen, oxygen, argon, carbon dioxide, and water vapour; dust particles suspended in it are not part of the air and are considered pollutants.
- Mixtures can be classified by the physical state of their components, for example gas and gas (air) or solid and solid (alloys).
- In science all the components of a mixture are pure substances, and the purpose of separating a mixture is to obtain them.
