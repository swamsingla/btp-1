# Acids, Bases and Indicators

Have you ever wondered how we know if a lemon is sour enough or if the milk has turned? In our daily lives, we constantly encounter different types of substances that can be sharp, soapy, or completely harmless. Knowing whether a substance is acidic, basic, or neutral is very important because it helps us understand how things react, why an ant bite stings, and how we can safely mix chemicals to make them harmless.

## The Three Types of Substances

Substances can be grouped into three main categories: acidic, basic, and neutral.

An acidic substance is one that contains acids. When you add a drop of blue litmus solution to an acidic substance like lemon juice, the colour of the solution turns red. This is a clear sign that the substance is acidic. On the other hand, a basic substance contains bases. If you add a drop of blue litmus solution to a base, the colour stays blue, or if you add red litmus solution to a base, it turns blue. A neutral substance is neither acidic nor basic. It is completely balanced. When the solution of an acid is mixed with the solution of a base in sufficient quantity, we find that the resulting solution is neither acidic nor basic. Such reactions are called neutralisation reactions. 

## Indicators

How do we know the colour changes? We use special chemicals called indicators. Indicators are substances that change colour when they are added to an acid or a base. The most common indicator is litmus. Litmus solution can be either red or blue. When you add a drop of blue litmus solution to lemon juice, the colour of the solution turns red. This shows that the lemon juice is acidic.

Another common indicator is phenolphthalein. Phenolphthalein is colourless in acidic substances. When you add it to a basic substance, it turns pink or magenta. This makes it very easy to see if a substance is basic. A third indicator is methyl orange. Methyl orange is red in acidic substances and yellow in basic substances. Using these indicators helps us identify the nature of unknown liquids safely.

## Neutralisation Reactions

A neutralisation reaction happens when an acid and a base are mixed together. In a neutralisation reaction, salt and water are formed with the evolution (i.e., release) of heat. The general formula for this reaction is: Acid + Base → Salt + Water + Heat. This means that the acid and the base cancel each other out, leaving behind a safe, neutral salt and water. 

There are many examples in everyday life where you can observe the use of neutralisation processes. For example, when an ant bites you, it injects a small amount of formic acid into your skin. This acid causes a stinging sensation. If you rub a mild base, like baking soda paste, on the bite, the base neutralises the acid. The stinging stops because the acid has been cancelled out by the base.

## Worked example

Let us look at a simple worked example to understand how indicators work in a neutralisation reaction. Imagine you have a test tube containing lemon juice mixed with water. You add a drop of blue litmus solution to the test tube, and the colour turns red. This tells you that the solution is acidic. 

Next, you slowly add drops of lime water to this test tube with the help of a dropper and swirl it well. As you add more lime water, the acid in the lemon juice reacts with the base in the lime water. Eventually, the colour of the solution changes from red to blue. This colour change shows that the solution in the test tube is no longer acidic. Lime water has neutralised the effect of the acid. At this point, the solution has become neutral.

## Key points

- Acidic substances turn blue litmus solution red.
- Basic substances turn red litmus solution blue, or turn phenolphthalein pink.
- Methyl orange is red in acids and yellow in bases.
- A neutral substance is neither acidic nor basic.
- Mixing an acid and a base in the right amounts creates a neutral solution.
- This process is called a neutralisation reaction, and it produces salt, water, and heat.
