# Particle Nature of Matter

Matter is the substance that makes up everything around you, from the chair you sit on to the water you drink. For a long time, scientists debated whether matter is continuous like a block of wood or made up of tiny particles like sand. Today, we know that matter is made of tiny particles. This particle nature of matter helps us understand why things dissolve, why smells travel, and why particles move around.

## Particles are Extremely Small
The particles that make up matter are incredibly small. We cannot see them with our eyes, and they are small beyond our imagination. To understand how small they are, scientists use a method of dilution. Take a few crystals of potassium permanganate and dissolve them in $100 \text{ mL}$ of water. Then, take $10 \text{ mL}$ of that solution and put it into $90 \text{ mL}$ of clear water. If you keep doing this five to eight times, the water will still have a faint colour. This experiment shows that just a few crystals can colour a huge volume of water, about $1000 \text{ L}$. Therefore, there must be millions of tiny particles in just one crystal, and they spread through the water as it is diluted. The same idea works with smells. If you use $2 \text{ mL}$ of Dettol, you can still smell it even after you dilute it many times.

## There is Space Between Particles
If particles were packed tightly together with no gaps, you could not dissolve anything in water. However, we see that particles of sugar, salt, Dettol, or potassium permanganate get evenly distributed in water. This happens because there is enough space between the particles of matter. When you make tea, coffee, or lemonade, the particles of the ingredients get into the spaces between the particles of the water. This shows that matter is not a solid, continuous block.

## Particles are Continuously Moving
Particles of matter are always moving. They possess what we call kinetic energy, which is the energy of motion. You can see this in everyday life. If you put an unlit incense stick in a corner of your class, you have to go very close to smell it. But if you light the incense stick, the smell reaches you even when you are sitting at a distance. This happens because the particles of the incense are moving continuously and spreading out.

You can also watch particles move in water. Take two glasses of water. Put a drop of blue or red ink slowly along the side of the first glass, and a drop of honey in the same way in the second glass. Leave them undisturbed. The ink spreads evenly throughout the water in a few hours or days. The honey also spreads, but it takes much longer than the ink does. If you drop a crystal of copper sulphate into hot water and another into cold water, you will see that the particles move faster in the hot water. This shows that as the temperature rises, the particles move faster and their kinetic energy increases.

## Particles Intermix on Their Own
When particles of two different types of matter mix on their own, it is called diffusion. In the ink and honey experiments, the particles get into the spaces between the water particles to spread out. The rate of mixing changes with temperature. When the water is hot, diffusion becomes faster because the particles are moving faster.

## Particles Attract Each Other
Particles of matter also attract each other. The strength of this force of attraction varies from one kind of matter to another. Imagine a game where four groups of students form human chains. The first group holds each other from the back and locks arms like Idu-Mishmi dancers. The second group holds hands. The third group touches each other with only their fingertips. If another group of students runs around and tries to break the chains, the chain holding with fingertips is the easiest to break. This shows that the particles held each other with the maximum force when they were locked together.

You can see this force in everyday objects. Take an iron nail, a piece of chalk, and a rubber band. If you try to break them by hammering, cutting, or stretching, the iron nail is the hardest to break. This means the particles in the iron nail are held together with the greatest force. If you take some water in a container and try to cut the surface with your fingers, you will not be able to cut it. The surface remains together because there is a force of attraction between the water particles.

## Worked example
Imagine you have $500 \text{ mL}$ of water and add $10 \text{ mL}$ of blue ink to it. The ink particles move through the water and spread out until the $510 \text{ mL}$ of liquid is a uniform blue colour. The ink is now about $10/510$, or roughly $2\%$, of the liquid. If you then take $10 \text{ mL}$ of this solution, it holds only about $0.2 \text{ mL}$ of ink. Add it to $490 \text{ mL}$ of clear water and the $500 \text{ mL}$ now contains about $0.2/500 = 0.04\%$ ink, which is about $50$ times weaker. The colour is much lighter, but it is still visible. A colour can only be seen if some ink particles are present, so even this tiny amount of ink must contain a very large number of particles. This shows that the particles of ink are extremely small.

## Key points
- Matter is made of tiny particles, and there is space between them.
- Particles of matter are continuously moving and possess kinetic energy; they move faster as temperature rises.
- The intermixing of particles of two different types of matter on their own is called diffusion, and it becomes faster at higher temperatures.
- Particles of matter attract each other, and the strength of this force of attraction varies from one kind of matter to another.
- Particles are so small that just a few crystals can colour a large volume of water, and smells can be detected even after many dilutions.
