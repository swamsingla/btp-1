# Law of Conservation of Mass

Have you ever torn a piece of paper into smaller pieces? The pieces look different, but the total amount of paper stays exactly the same. Chemical reactions in a test tube work in a very similar way. When substances react to form new products, the total mass of the reactants before the reaction is exactly equal to the total mass of the products after the reaction. This fundamental rule of nature is called the Law of Conservation of Mass. It matters because it tells us that matter is not created or destroyed when it changes form. The atoms simply rearrange themselves into new combinations.

## What the Law States

The Law of Conservation of Mass is a basic rule in chemistry. It states that mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. This means that if you weigh the chemicals before you mix them, the total mass will be exactly the same after the reaction is complete. No new matter appears out of nowhere, and none of the old matter vanishes into thin air. The atoms might form completely new substances, but the total number and type of atoms remain unchanged. This law was established by Antoine Lavoisier after careful experiments in which he weighed the substances before and after reactions. Joseph L. Proust's experiments led to a different law, the law of definite proportions.

## Testing the Law in the Lab

You can see this law in action through a simple classroom activity. Imagine you have two solutions prepared in water. One solution contains copper sulphate, and the other contains sodium carbonate. Each solution is prepared separately in 10 mL of water to make a 5% solution. You place a little amount of the second solution in a conical flask. Then, you hang an ignition tube containing the first solution inside the flask. It is very important to see that the two solutions do not get mixed yet.

Next, you carefully weigh the flask with all its contents. Then, you tilt and swirl the flask so that the two solutions mix together. A chemical reaction takes place inside the flask. You might notice bubbles forming or a new solid settling at the bottom. After the reaction finishes, you weigh the flask and its contents again.

Does the mass of the flask and its contents change? No, it does not. The total mass remains exactly the same. This is why scientists put a cork on the mouth of the flask during the experiment. The cork traps any gases that might be produced during the reaction. If the cork were missing, the gases would float away into the air, and the mass would appear to decrease.

## Explaining the Law with Atoms

The reason the mass stays the same is explained by John Dalton's atomic theory. Dalton provided the basic theory about the nature of matter. He stated that the smallest particles of matter are atoms. According to Dalton's atomic theory, atoms are indivisible particles, which cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction.

When a chemical reaction occurs, the atoms from the reactants simply rearrange themselves to form new compounds. For example, if you combine atoms of two different elements, they might form a compound. However, the total mass of the atoms does not change. The number of atoms of each element remains constant in a given compound. This explains why the total mass is conserved. Dalton's atomic theory provided an explanation for the law of conservation of mass and the law of definite proportions.

## Worked Example

Let us look at a practical example to understand how to apply this law. Imagine a student is performing a reaction between barium chloride and sodium sulphate. The student prepares a 5% solution of each substance in 10 mL of water. The student weighs the conical flask and the ignition tube. The initial mass of the flask and its contents is $120$ grams.

The student then mixes the solutions. A chemical reaction takes place, and a white precipitate of barium sulphate forms. The student weighs the flask again. The final mass of the flask and its contents is $120$ grams. According to the Law of Conservation of Mass, the mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. Therefore, the mass of the reactants is equal to the mass of the products. The mass remains $120$ grams throughout the reaction.

## Key points
- Mass is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.
- The Law of Conservation of Mass was established by Lavoisier through careful weighing before and after reactions.
- Atoms are indivisible particles, which cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction.
- Dalton's atomic theory provided an explanation for the law of conservation of mass.
- The total mass of the reactants is equal to the total mass of the products.
