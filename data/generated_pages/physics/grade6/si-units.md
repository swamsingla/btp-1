# SI Units

Long ago, people in different parts of the world used their own ways to measure things, which caused a lot of confusion. To fix this, countries came together and agreed on a single set of standard units. This agreed-upon system is called the International System of Units, or SI units. Using these standard units makes it easy for scientists and students all over the world to share their measurements without any misunderstandings.

## The Base Units of Measurement

The SI system gives us seven special base units. These base units are the building blocks for everything else we measure in physics. The seven base units are the metre for length, the kilogram for mass, the second for time, the ampere for electric current, the kelvin for temperature, the mole for amount of substance, and the candela for luminous intensity. When you are measuring the length of your desk, the mass of a heavy book, or the time it takes to run a race, you are using these fundamental SI units.

## The Metre: Measuring Length

The SI unit of length is the metre, and its symbol is m. A metre is a convenient unit for measuring a room, but it is far too small for the distance between two cities, which would come out as a very large number of metres. For such distances we use the kilometre. A kilometre, written as km, is equal to 1000 metres. This makes it perfect for measuring the length of a railway track between two cities. On the other hand, if you want to measure the thickness of a page in your textbook, you would use smaller units like the centimetre (cm) or the millimetre (mm).

To understand how these smaller units work, imagine a 15-cm scale. One metre is divided into 100 equal divisions, and each division is a centimetre. If you look at the markings between two consecutive big marks, such as between 1 and 2, the length of that section is 1 cm. These sections of 1 cm length are further divided into 10 equal parts. The length of one of these smaller parts is called a millimetre (mm). In fact, 1 mm is equal to one-tenth of a centimetre, written as $1 \mathrm{~mm} = 0.1 \mathrm{cm}$. 

## Choosing the Right Unit

It is important to pick the right unit for the job. Would it be convenient to use the unit metre to measure the length of a railway track? No, because the numbers would be very large and hard to read. Similarly, using a kilometre to measure the thickness of a page of a book would be silly because the result would be a tiny fraction. For larger lengths, we use a larger unit called a kilometre, which is equal to 1000 metres. For measuring smaller lengths, we use units such as centimetre or millimetre.

In the past, people used units like the inch and the foot to measure length. You might still see these units on some old scales. For example, 1 inch is equal to 2.54 cm. However, the SI system is the standard that most of the world uses today.

## Worked Example

Let us look at a worked example to see how these units relate to each other. Imagine you have a long ribbon that is exactly 2.5 metres long. You want to know how many millimetres that ribbon is. First, you know that 1 metre is equal to 100 centimetres. So, 2.5 metres is equal to 250 centimetres. Next, you know that 1 centimetre is equal to 10 millimetres. To find the total number of millimetres, you multiply the number of centimetres by 10. This gives you 2500 millimetres.

## Key points

- The International System of Units, or SI units, is a standard set of measurements used worldwide to avoid confusion.
- The seven base units are the metre, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole, and candela.
- The metre (m) is the SI unit of length, and it can be divided into 100 centimetres (cm).
- Each centimetre is divided into 10 millimetres (mm), meaning 1 mm is equal to $0.1 \mathrm{cm}$.
- A kilometre (km) is equal to 1000 metres and is used for measuring very large distances.
