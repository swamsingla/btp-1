# Echo and Reverberation

Sound is a mechanical wave that travels through a medium by making the particles of that medium vibrate. When a sound wave reaches a boundary between two different media, such as air and a solid wall, it bounces back. This phenomenon, where the sound wave returns to its source after hitting a surface, is known as reflection of sound. Understanding this reflection is essential for explaining everyday phenomena like hearing your own voice back in a large empty hall, or how ships and submarines navigate deep underwater.

## What is an Echo?

An echo is the distinct repetition of a sound that a person hears after the original sound is produced. When you shout or clap near a suitable reflecting object such as a tall building or a mountain, the sound waves travel to the obstacle, reflect off it, and return to your ear. For a person to hear a distinct echo, the time interval between the original sound and the reflected one must be at least 0.1 s. This requirement exists because the sensation of sound persists in our brain for about 0.1 s. If the reflected sound arrives too quickly, our brain blends it with the original sound, and we do not perceive it as a separate echo.

## Minimum Distance for a Distinct Echo

Because the sound must travel to the obstacle and back to the listener, the total distance covered by the sound must be at least (344 m/s) × 0.1 s = 34.4 m, taking the speed of sound in air as 344 m/s at about 22 °C. Therefore, for hearing distinct echoes, the obstacle must be at least half of this total distance away from the source of sound, that is, at least 17.2 m. This minimum distance will change with the temperature of air, as the speed of sound varies with temperature. If a person stands closer than 17.2 m to a wall, the reflected sound will arrive in less than 0.1 s, and the brain will not register it as a separate echo.

## Multiple Reflections and Thunder

Echoes may be heard more than once due to successive or multiple reflections. When a sound wave reflects off one surface and then travels to another surface to reflect again, it creates a second, fainter echo. A common example of this in nature is the rolling of thunder. The rolling of thunder is due to the successive reflections of the sound from a number of reflecting surfaces, such as the clouds and the land. Each reflection arrives at the listener's ear slightly after the previous one, creating the prolonged rumble.

## What is Reverberation?

Reverberation is the persistence of sound in a large space due to repeated reflection from the walls, floor, and ceiling. A sound created in a big hall will persist by repeated reflection from the walls until it is reduced to a value where it is no longer audible. In an auditorium or big hall, excessive reverberation is highly undesirable because it makes speech and music sound muddy and unclear. The reflected sound waves overlap with the direct sound waves, causing a confusing mix that obscures the original audio.

## Reducing Reverberation

To reduce reverberation, the roof and walls of the auditorium are generally covered with sound-absorbent materials like compressed fibreboard, rough plaster or draperies. These materials absorb sound energy rather than reflecting it, which helps to dampen the sound much faster. The seat materials are also selected on the basis of their sound absorbing properties. By choosing specific fabrics and materials for the furniture, architects can significantly improve the clarity of sound in a large room.

## Using Sound to Measure Distance

The principle of sound reflection is not just used for hearing echoes; it is also used in technology to measure distances. A system called SONAR uses sound waves to locate objects underwater. In this method, a sound pulse is sent out from a ship. The sound travels through the water, hits an object like an iceberg or a submarine, and reflects back. By measuring the time it takes for the echo to return and knowing the speed of sound in water, the distance to the object can be calculated.

## Worked example

A student standing near a cliff claps her hands and hears the echo after 3 s. If the speed of sound in the air at that moment is $342\mathrm{ms}^{-1}$, we can calculate the distance of the cliff from the student. The sound travels to the cliff and back to the student, so the total distance covered is $342\mathrm{ms}^{-1}$ multiplied by $3\mathrm{s}$, which equals $1026\mathrm{m}$. Since this is a round trip, the distance from the student to the cliff is half of this total distance. Dividing $1026\mathrm{m}$ by $2$ gives $513\mathrm{m}$. Therefore, the cliff is $513\mathrm{m}$ away from the student.

## Key points
- An echo is a distinct repetition of a sound heard after the original sound is produced.
- The sensation of sound persists in our brain for about 0.1 s, so the time interval between the original and reflected sound must be at least 0.1 s for a distinct echo.
- The obstacle must be at least about 17.2 m from the source of sound (in air at about 22 °C) to hear a distinct echo.
- Reverberation is the persistence of sound in a big hall due to repeated reflection from the walls until it is no longer audible.
- Excessive reverberation in an auditorium is highly undesirable and can be reduced using sound-absorbent materials.
- The principle of sound reflection is used in SONAR to locate objects underwater.
