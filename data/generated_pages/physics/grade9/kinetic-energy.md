# Kinetic Energy

Kinetic energy is the energy that an object possesses because it is in motion. This means that anything that is moving, whether it is a rolling stone, flowing water, or a speeding car, has kinetic energy. The faster an object moves, the more kinetic energy it has. This concept is important because a moving object can do work on other things. For example, a moving trolley can hit a wooden block and push it, doing work and transferring energy in the process.

## Everyday Examples of Kinetic Energy

You can observe kinetic energy in many everyday situations. A falling coconut, a rolling stone, a flying aircraft, blowing wind, and a running athlete all possess kinetic energy because they are moving. A bullet can pierce a target because it has kinetic energy. The wind can move the blades of a windmill for the same reason. Objects in motion possess energy, and we call this energy kinetic energy. The kinetic energy of an object increases with its speed, which is why a fast-moving object can do more work than an identical object moving relatively slow.

## The Work-Energy Theorem

The work-energy theorem connects the work done on an object to its kinetic energy. By definition, the kinetic energy of a body moving with a certain velocity is equal to the work done on it to make it acquire that velocity. This means that if you want to make an object move, you must do work on it to give it kinetic energy. The work done on an object causes a change in its kinetic energy. If an object starts from a stationary position, the work done is equal to its kinetic energy.

## The Formula for Kinetic Energy

We can express the kinetic energy of an object in the form of an equation. Consider an object of mass, $m$, moving with an initial velocity, $u$. Let a constant force, $F$, act on it in the direction of its displacement through a distance $s$. The work done by the force is $W = Fs$. The work done on the object will cause a change in its velocity from $u$ to $v$. Using the equations of motion, we can show that the work done is $W = \frac{1}{2} m(v^2 - u^2)$. If the object is starting from its stationary position, that is, $u = 0$, then the work done is $W = \frac{1}{2} mv^2$. Thus, the kinetic energy possessed by an object of mass, $m$, and moving with a uniform velocity, $v$, is $E_k = \frac{1}{2} mv^2$.

## Using the Kinetic Energy Formula

Let us look at an example to see how to use this formula. Consider an object of mass 15 kg moving with a uniform velocity of 4 m s$^{-1}$. The kinetic energy is $E_k = \frac{1}{2} \times 15 \, \text{kg} \times 4 \, \text{m s}^{-1} \times 4 \, \text{m s}^{-1} = 120 \, \text{J}$. The kinetic energy of the object is 120 J.

## Calculating Work Done

We can also use the work-energy theorem to calculate the work done to change an object's speed. Consider a car with a mass of 1500 kg. We want to find the work to be done to increase its velocity from $30 \, \text{km h}^{-1}$ to $60 \, \text{km h}^{-1}$. First, we convert the velocities to m s$^{-1}$. The initial velocity is $u = \frac{25}{3} \, \text{m s}^{-1}$ and the final velocity is $v = \frac{50}{3} \, \text{m s}^{-1}$. The initial kinetic energy is $E_{ki} = \frac{1}{2} \times 1500 \, \text{kg} \times (\frac{25}{3} \, \text{m s}^{-1})^2 = 156250/3 \, \text{J}$. The final kinetic energy is $E_{kf} = \frac{1}{2} \times 1500 \, \text{kg} \times (\frac{50}{3} \, \text{m s}^{-1})^2 = 625000/3 \, \text{J}$. The work done is equal to the change in kinetic energy, which is $E_{kf} - E_{ki} = 156250 \, \text{J}$.

## How Mass and Speed Change Kinetic Energy

The formula $E_k = \frac{1}{2} mv^2$ shows that kinetic energy depends on both mass and speed, but not in the same way. If the mass is doubled, the kinetic energy doubles. If the speed is doubled, the kinetic energy becomes four times as large, because the speed is squared. Consider a car of mass 1000 kg. At $10 \, \text{m s}^{-1}$ its kinetic energy is $\frac{1}{2} \times 1000 \times 10 \times 10 = 50000 \, \text{J}$. At $20 \, \text{m s}^{-1}$ it is $\frac{1}{2} \times 1000 \times 20 \times 20 = 200000 \, \text{J}$, which is four times as much.

This is why speed matters so much on the road. To stop a moving vehicle, the brakes must do work equal to its kinetic energy. For the same braking force $F$, the work done is $F \times s$, where $s$ is the stopping distance, so a car with four times the kinetic energy needs four times the distance to stop.

## Two More Worked Examples

A cricket ball of mass 0.16 kg moves at $25 \, \text{m s}^{-1}$. Its kinetic energy is $E_k = \frac{1}{2} \times 0.16 \times 25 \times 25 = 50 \, \text{J}$. A fielder who catches it and brings it to rest must take away this 50 J of energy, which is why the hands are pulled back while catching.

A body of mass 2 kg has a kinetic energy of 100 J. To find its speed, we write $100 = \frac{1}{2} \times 2 \times v^2$, so $v^2 = 100$ and $v = 10 \, \text{m s}^{-1}$.

Kinetic energy is a scalar quantity. Its SI unit is the joule (J), where $1 \, \text{J} = 1 \, \text{kg m}^2 \text{s}^{-2}$.

## Key points
*   Kinetic energy is the energy possessed by an object due to its motion.
*   The kinetic energy of an object increases with its speed.
*   The kinetic energy of an object of mass, $m$, and moving with a uniform velocity, $v$, is $E_k = \frac{1}{2} mv^2$.
*   The work done on an object is equal to the change in its kinetic energy.
*   A moving object can do work on other objects.
*   Doubling the mass doubles the kinetic energy, while doubling the speed makes it four times as large.
