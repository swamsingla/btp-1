# Atomic Models (Physics)

Atoms are the tiny building blocks of all matter. In the past, scientists thought these particles were solid and could not be broken apart. However, later discoveries showed that atoms are made of even smaller parts. Scientists had to build new pictures, called models, to explain how the electrons and protons are arranged inside an atom. This page explains the three most important models of the atom.

## Thomson's Model

The first picture of the atom was proposed by J.J. Thomson. He suggested that an atom is like a Christmas pudding. The pudding is a big, round ball of positive charge. The electrons are like the small, dry fruits mixed inside the pudding. You can also think of a watermelon. The red, edible part of the watermelon is like the positive charge spread all over the atom. The seeds inside the watermelon are like the electrons studded in the positive sphere.

J.J. Thomson proposed two main ideas based on this model. First, an atom is a sphere of positive charge, and the electrons are embedded in it. Second, the negative charge of the electrons and the positive charge of the atom are equal in magnitude. Because of this balance, the atom as a whole is electrically neutral. 

However, later experiments showed that this model could not explain everything. Scientists found that atoms have a very dense center. Thomson's model showed a soft, spread-out positive charge, so it failed to explain this new data.

## Alpha-Particle Scattering

To find out how electrons are arranged, Ernest Rutherford designed a famous experiment. He wanted to see what happens when fast-moving alpha ($\alpha$) particles hit a thin piece of metal. He chose a gold foil because he could make it extremely thin, only about 1000 atoms thick.

Alpha particles are doubly-charged helium ions. They have a mass of 4 atomic units and carry a lot of energy. Rutherford expected the alpha particles to be slightly deflected by the sub-atomic particles in the gold atoms. He did not expect large deflections because the alpha particles were much heavier than the protons.

The results were totally unexpected. Most of the alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil. Some were deflected by small angles. Surprisingly, one out of every 12,000 particles rebounded. Rutherford said this result was almost as incredible as firing a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and having it come back and hit you.

From these results, Rutherford drew three conclusions. First, most of the space inside the atom is empty. Second, the positive charge of the atom occupies very little space. Third, a very small fraction of particles were deflected by $180^\circ$. This meant that all the positive charge and mass of the gold atom were concentrated in a very small volume. He calculated that the radius of the nucleus is about $10^{5}$ times less than the radius of the atom.

## Rutherford's Model

Based on the alpha-particle scattering experiment, Rutherford proposed the nuclear model of the atom. In this model, there is a positively charged center called the nucleus. Nearly all the mass of the atom resides in this nucleus. The electrons revolve around the nucleus in circular paths. The size of the nucleus is very small compared to the size of the atom.

However, this model had a major drawback. A charged particle moving in a circle is constantly accelerating. When charged particles accelerate, they radiate energy. This meant the revolving electron would lose energy and finally fall into the nucleus. If this were true, the atom would be highly unstable. Since we know atoms are quite stable, this model could not be the complete truth.

## Bohr's Model

To fix the problems of Rutherford's model, Niels Bohr proposed a new set of rules. He suggested that atoms have only certain special orbits for their electrons. These are called discrete orbits or energy levels. The orbits are represented by the letters K, L, M, N, or by numbers $n=1, 2, 3, 4, ...$.

Bohr's main postulate was that while electrons revolve in these discrete orbits, they do not radiate energy. This solved Rutherford's problem. Because the electrons do not lose energy, they do not fall into the nucleus, and the atom remains stable.

## Worked Example

Rutherford calculated that the radius of the nucleus is about $10^{5}$ times less than the radius of the atom. Suppose an atom were enlarged until its radius was 100 m, about the size of a large stadium. The nucleus would then have a radius of $\frac{100\ \text{m}}{10^{5}} = 0.001\ \text{m}$, which is 1 mm, about the size of a tiny grain of sand. This shows just how much empty space exists inside an atom.

## Key points

* Atoms were once thought to be indivisible, but later discoveries found electrons and protons inside them.
* Thomson's model pictured the atom as a sphere of positive charge with embedded electrons, like a Christmas pudding or a watermelon.
* Rutherford's alpha-particle scattering experiment showed that atoms have a small, dense, positively charged nucleus.
* Rutherford's model failed because revolving electrons should radiate energy and collapse into the nucleus.
* Bohr fixed this by proposing that electrons move in discrete, stable energy levels without radiating energy.
