# Conservation of Energy

Energy is the capacity to do work, and it exists in many different forms around us. You may already know that energy can change from one form into another. For example, when you ride a bicycle, the chemical energy stored in the food you ate is transformed into the mechanical energy that moves your legs and the bicycle forward. The law of conservation of energy explains what happens to the total energy during these transformations. According to this law, energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be converted from one form to another. Whenever energy gets transformed, the total energy of the system remains unchanged. This means the total energy before and after the transformation stays exactly the same. The law of conservation of energy is valid in all situations and for all kinds of transformations.

## Forms of Energy

The world we live in provides energy in many different forms. The various forms include mechanical energy, heat energy, chemical energy, electrical energy, and light energy. Mechanical energy itself is a combination of potential energy and kinetic energy. You may already know these two forms of mechanical energy. Potential energy is the energy stored in an object due to its position or height, while kinetic energy is the energy of motion. Understanding these different forms helps us see how energy moves and changes in the world around us.

## Interconversion of Energy

Nature constantly shows us examples of energy changing from one form to another. This process is called interconversion. You can observe this in many everyday situations. For instance, green plants produce their food by converting light energy into chemical energy through a process you may have studied before. The air around us moves from place to place because of energy transformations that occur in the atmosphere. Even the water cycle that brings us rain is sustained by various kinds of energy conversions. Fuels such as coal and petroleum are formed over millions of years through natural processes that involve the storage and transformation of chemical energy. These examples show that energy is always present and always changing form, but the total amount of energy in the universe remains constant.

## Free Fall and Mechanical Energy

A simple example of the law of conservation of energy is an object falling freely from a height. Let an object of mass, $m$, be made to fall freely from a height, $h$. At the start, the object is at rest, so its velocity is zero. Because its velocity is zero, its kinetic energy is zero. At this starting point, all the energy of the object is in the form of gravitational potential energy, which is given by $mgh$. Therefore, the total energy of the object at the start is $mgh$.

As the object falls, its height decreases, and its velocity increases. This means its potential energy decreases while its kinetic energy increases. If $v$ is the velocity of the object at a given instant during the fall, the kinetic energy would be $\frac{1}{2}mv^{2}$. As the fall of the object continues, the potential energy becomes less, and the kinetic energy becomes more. When the object is about to reach the ground, the height $h$ becomes zero, and the velocity $v$ will be the highest. Therefore, the kinetic energy would be the largest, and the potential energy the least. However, the sum of the potential energy and kinetic energy of the object would be the same at all points during the fall. That is,

$$
m g h + \frac {1}{2} m v ^ {2} = \text{constant}.
$$

The sum of kinetic energy and potential energy of an object is its total mechanical energy. During the free fall of the object, the decrease in potential energy at any point in its path appears as an equal amount of increase in kinetic energy. (Here the effect of air resistance on the motion of the object has been ignored.) There is thus a continual transformation of gravitational potential energy into kinetic energy.

## Worked Example

Consider a stone of mass $2$ kg dropped from a height of $20$ meters. We want to find its total mechanical energy at the start and just before it hits the ground, ignoring air resistance.

At the start, the stone is at rest, so its velocity is zero. Its kinetic energy is therefore zero. Taking $g = 10 \, \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, its potential energy is $mgh = 2 \times 10 \times 20 = 400$ joules. The total mechanical energy is $400 + 0 = 400$ joules.

Just before it hits the ground, the height $h = 0$, so the potential energy is zero. According to the law of conservation of energy, the total mechanical energy must remain the same, so it is still $400$ joules. This means all the initial potential energy has been converted into kinetic energy. The kinetic energy just before impact is $400$ joules.

## Key points

- Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be converted from one form to another.
- The total energy before and after a transformation remains the same.
- The law of conservation of energy is valid in all situations and for all kinds of transformations.
- The various forms of energy include mechanical, heat, chemical, electrical, and light energy.
- During a free fall, the decrease in potential energy equals the increase in kinetic energy, keeping the total mechanical energy constant.
