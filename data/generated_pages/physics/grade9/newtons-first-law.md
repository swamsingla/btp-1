# Newton's First Law of Motion

An object remains in a state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line unless compelled to change that state by an applied force. In other words, all objects resist a change in their state of motion. In a qualitative way, the tendency of undisturbed objects to stay at rest or to keep moving with the same velocity is called inertia. This is why the first law of motion is also known as the law of inertia. This law tells us that a force is not required to keep an object moving, but rather to change its motion. Understanding this law helps us predict how objects will behave when we push, pull, or stop them.

## Galileo's Inclined Plane

Long before Isaac Newton, Galileo Galilei studied how objects move. He used an inclined plane to slow down the motion of falling objects so he could study them more easily. He observed that when a marble rolls down an inclined plane, its velocity increases. When the marble climbs up the opposite side, its velocity decreases. Galileo then imagined a special setup where a plane is inclined on both sides with no friction. In this ideal case, the marble would roll down one side and climb the other side to the exact same height from which it was released. If the angle of the right-side plane were gradually decreased, the marble would travel further distances until it reached the original height. If the right-side plane were made completely horizontal, the slope would be zero, and the marble would continue to travel forever. In this specific case, the unbalanced forces on the marble are zero. It thus suggests that an unbalanced external force is required to change the motion of the marble but no net force is needed to sustain the uniform motion of the marble. In practical situations, it is difficult to achieve a zero unbalanced force because of the presence of the frictional force acting opposite to the direction of motion. Thus, in practice, the marble stops after travelling some distance. The effect of the frictional force may be minimised by using a smooth marble and a smooth plane and providing a lubricant on top of the planes.

## Inertia in a Motorcar

We can see the law of inertia in action every time we travel in a vehicle. When a motorcar is moving, we tend to remain at rest with respect to the seat until the driver applies a braking force to stop the motorcar. With the application of brakes, the car slows down, but our body tends to continue in the same state of motion because of its inertia. A sudden application of brakes may thus cause injury to us by impact or collision with the panels in front. Safety belts are worn to prevent such accidents. Safety belts exert a force on our body to make the forward motion slower.

## Inertia on a Bus

An opposite experience is encountered when we are standing in a bus and the bus begins to move suddenly. Now we tend to fall backwards. This is because the sudden start of the bus brings motion to the bus as well as to our feet in contact with the floor of the bus. But the rest of our body opposes this motion because of its inertia.

## Inertia During a Sharp Turn

When a motorcar makes a sharp turn at a high speed, we tend to get thrown to one side. This can again be explained on the basis of the law of inertia. We tend to continue in our straight-line motion. When an unbalanced force (the friction of the road on the tyres) changes the direction of motion of the motorcar, we slip to one side of the seat due to the inertia of our body.

## Worked Example

Imagine a hockey puck sliding across a perfectly smooth, level ice rink with absolutely no friction. According to the law of inertia, the puck will keep sliding in a straight line at a constant speed forever. If a player wants to stop the puck, they must apply an unbalanced external force, such as hitting it with their hockey stick. If they want to change the puck's direction, they must apply a force at an angle to the puck's current path. Without that applied force, the puck's state of uniform motion will never change.

## Key points

- An object remains in a state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line unless compelled to change that state by an applied force.
- The tendency of undisturbed objects to stay at rest or to keep moving with the same velocity is called inertia.
- An unbalanced external force is required to change the motion of an object, but no net force is needed to sustain uniform motion.
- In practical situations, objects stop moving because of the presence of frictional force acting opposite to the direction of motion.
- The effect of frictional force may be minimised by using smooth surfaces and providing a lubricant.
