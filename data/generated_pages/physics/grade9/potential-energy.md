# Potential Energy

When you lift a heavy school bag off the floor and place it on a high shelf, you have to push it upward against the pull of the Earth. The energy you put into the bag is not lost; it is stored inside the bag as potential energy. Potential energy is the energy an object possesses because of its position or its condition. This stored energy can be released later to do work. Understanding potential energy is a crucial step in learning how energy moves and changes form in the physical world.

## Gravitational Potential Energy

The most common type of potential energy you will encounter is gravitational potential energy. This is the energy an object has because of its height above the ground. When you raise an object, you are doing work on it against the force of gravity. The energy present in such an object is the gravitational potential energy, and it depends on how high the object is lifted.

To find the gravitational potential energy of an object at a point above the ground, we look at the work done in raising it from the ground to that point against gravity. Let us consider an object of mass $m$. If we raise it through a height $h$ from the ground, we must apply a force. The minimum force required to raise the object is equal to the weight of the object, which is $mg$. The work done on the object against gravity is the force multiplied by the displacement. Therefore, the work done is $mg \times h$, which equals $mgh$.

Since the work done on the object is equal to $mgh$, an energy equal to $mgh$ units is gained by the object. This stored energy is the potential energy $(E_p)$ of the object. The rule for calculating this is given by the formula:

$$
E _ {p} = m g h
$$

Here, $m$ is the mass of the object in kilograms, $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity, and $h$ is the height in meters. For example, if you have a 10 kg object resting 6 meters above the ground, and the acceleration due to gravity is $9.8 \, m \, s^{-2}$, you can find its potential energy. Multiplying $10$ kg by $9.8$ and by $6$ m gives you $588$ J. The potential energy is 588 J.

## The Zero Reference

You might wonder if an object always has the same potential energy. The answer is that it depends on the ground level, or the zero level, you choose. An object in a given position can have a certain potential energy with respect to one level and a different value of potential energy with respect to another level. 

Imagine a book resting on a table. If you choose the floor as your zero level, the book has a specific amount of gravitational potential energy. However, if you choose the table surface as your zero level, the book has zero potential energy because its height above that specific reference point is zero. The choice of zero reference is completely up to you, but once you choose it, you must stick to it for all your calculations.

It is also useful to know that the work done by gravity depends on the difference in vertical heights of the initial and final positions of the object, and not on the path along which the object is moved. This means that whether you lift a block straight up or carry it up a winding staircase to the same height, the work done against gravity is exactly the same.

## Potential Energy Changing into Kinetic Energy

Potential energy can change into kinetic energy. Suppose a $2 \, \text{kg}$ stone is held $5 \, \text{m}$ above the ground. Taking $g = 10 \, \text{m s}^{-2}$, its potential energy is $2 \times 10 \times 5 = 100 \, \text{J}$. When it is released, the potential energy decreases as it falls, and the kinetic energy increases by the same amount. Just before it reaches the ground, all of the $100 \, \text{J}$ has become kinetic energy (ignoring air resistance).

## Elastic Potential Energy

While gravitational potential energy comes from height, there is another common type called elastic potential energy. This is the energy stored in an object when it is stretched or compressed. A simple example is a spring or a rubber band. When you pull a rubber band back, you are doing work on it. This work is stored as elastic potential energy inside the stretched material. When you let go, this stored energy is released, and the object snaps back to its original shape.

Unlike the formula for gravitational potential energy, the amount of elastic potential energy depends on how much the object is deformed and the stiffness of the material. In later grades, you will learn more about the exact rules for calculating elastic potential energy. For now, it is enough to understand that any object that is stretched or squashed has stored energy waiting to be released.

## Worked example

Let us look at a practical application of the gravitational potential energy formula. Imagine a 12 kg object resting at a certain height above the ground. You measure its potential energy and find that it is 480 J. To find out how high the object is, you can rearrange the formula $E_p = mgh$.

Given that the mass of the object, $m = 12\mathrm{kg}$, and the potential energy, $E_{p} = 480\mathrm{J}$, and taking $g = 10 \, \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, you can set up the equation:

$$
4 8 0 \mathrm{J} = 1 2 \mathrm{kg} \times 1 0 \mathrm{ms} ^ {- 2} \times h
$$

To solve for $h$, you divide $480$ by $120$, which gives you $4$. The object is at a height of $4\mathrm{m}$.

## Key points

*   Gravitational potential energy is the energy an object possesses because of its height above a reference level.
*   The formula for gravitational potential energy is $E_p = mgh$, where $m$ is mass, $g$ is acceleration due to gravity, and $h$ is height.
*   The value of potential energy depends on the zero reference level you choose, such as the floor or a tabletop.
*   The work done by gravity depends only on the difference in vertical heights, not on the path taken between the two points.
*   Elastic potential energy is the energy stored in an object when it is stretched or compressed.
