# Acceleration

Imagine you are riding in a car on a busy road in Bengaluru. If the car is moving at a steady 60 km/h on the Outer Ring Road, the speed does not change. However, if the driver steps on the accelerator to merge into fast-moving traffic, or hits the brakes to avoid a sudden obstacle, the car's speed changes. In physics, whenever the speed or direction of an object changes, we say the object is accelerating. Acceleration is simply a measure of how quickly the velocity of an object changes over time. It is a crucial concept because it tells us how an object's motion is evolving, whether it is speeding up, slowing down, or turning.

## What is Acceleration?

To understand acceleration, we must first look at uniform and non-uniform motion. During uniform motion along a straight line, an object's velocity remains constant, meaning the change in velocity over any time interval is zero. However, in non-uniform motion, the velocity varies with time. It has different values at different instants, and the change in velocity during any time interval is not zero. 

To quantify this change, we introduce the physical quantity called acceleration. It is defined as the change in velocity of an object per unit time. If the velocity of an object changes from an initial value $u$ to a final value $v$ in time $t$, the acceleration $a$ is given by the formula:

$$
a = \frac {v - u}{t}$$

This kind of motion is known as accelerated motion. The acceleration is taken to be positive if it is in the direction of the velocity, and negative when it is opposite to the direction of the velocity. The SI unit of acceleration is $m s^{-2}$.

## Uniform and Non-Uniform Acceleration

Acceleration can be uniform or non-uniform depending on how the velocity changes over time. If an object travels in a straight line and its velocity increases or decreases by equal amounts in equal intervals of time, then the acceleration is said to be uniform. The motion of a freely falling body is a classic example of uniformly accelerated motion. 

On the other hand, an object can travel with non-uniform acceleration if its velocity changes at a non-uniform rate. For example, if a car travelling along a straight road increases its speed by unequal amounts in equal intervals of time, then the car is said to be moving with non-uniform acceleration.

## Equations of Motion

When an object moves along a straight line with uniform acceleration, it is possible to relate its velocity, acceleration during motion, and the distance covered by it in a certain time interval by a set of equations known as the equations of motion. For convenience, a set of three such equations are given below:

$$
v = u + a t$$

$$
s = u t + \frac {1}{2} a t ^ {2}
$$

$$
2 a s = v ^ {2} - u ^ {2}$$

where $u$ is the initial velocity of the object which moves with uniform acceleration $a$ for time $t$, $v$ is the final velocity, and $s$ is the distance travelled by the object in time $t$. The first equation gives the velocity-time relation and the second gives the position-time relation. The third, which relates position and velocity, can be obtained from the first two by eliminating $t$. All three equations can also be derived by a graphical method.

## Deceleration

Deceleration is a specific type of acceleration where the velocity of an object decreases over time. By definition, acceleration is negative when it is opposite to the direction of the velocity. This means that when a driver applies the brakes to a car, the acceleration produced is in the opposite direction to the motion of the car. This negative acceleration causes the vehicle to slow down until it eventually stops.

## Worked Example

Consider a car that accelerates uniformly from $18 \, km \, h^{-1}$ to $36 \, km \, h^{-1}$ in 5 seconds. We can calculate the acceleration and the distance covered by the car in that time. 

First, we convert the initial and final velocities to meters per second to match the standard SI units. We are given that $u = 18 \, km \, h^{-1} = 5 \, m \, s^{-1}$ and $v = 36 \, km \, h^{-1} = 10 \, m \, s^{-1}$, and the time $t = 5 \, s$.

To find the acceleration, we use the formula $a = \frac{v - u}{t}$. Substituting the values, we get:

$$
a = \frac{10 \, m \, s^{-1} - 5 \, m \, s^{-1}}{5 \, s} = 1 \, m \, s^{-2}
$$

To find the distance covered, we use the equation $s = u t + \frac{1}{2} a t^{2}$. Substituting the values, we get:

$$
s = 5 \, m \, s^{-1} \times 5 \, s + \frac{1}{2} \times 1 \, m \, s^{-2} \times (5 \, s)^{2} = 25 \, m + 12.5 \, m = 37.5 \, m
$$

Therefore, the acceleration of the car is $1 \, m \, s^{-2}$ and the distance covered is $37.5 \, m$.

## Uniform Circular Motion

It is also possible for an object to accelerate without changing its speed, but by changing its direction of motion. When an object moves in a circular path with uniform speed, its motion is called uniform circular motion. In this type of motion, the magnitude of the velocity remains constant, but the direction of the motion continuously changes. Since velocity is a vector quantity that includes both magnitude and direction, any change in direction results in a change in velocity. Therefore, the motion of an athlete moving along a circular path is an example of accelerated motion.

## Key points

*   Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity of an object per unit time.
*   The formula for acceleration is $a = \frac{v - u}{t}$, and its SI unit is $m s^{-2}$.
*   Acceleration is positive when it is in the direction of velocity and negative when it is opposite to the direction of velocity.
*   Uniform acceleration occurs when velocity changes by equal amounts in equal intervals of time, while non-uniform acceleration occurs when the rate of change is unequal.
*   The equations of motion for uniformly accelerated motion are $v = u + at$, $s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$, and $2as = v^2 - u^2$.
*   Uniform circular motion is an example of accelerated motion where the direction of motion changes, even if the speed remains constant.
*   Deceleration is simply negative acceleration, occurring when the velocity of an object decreases.
