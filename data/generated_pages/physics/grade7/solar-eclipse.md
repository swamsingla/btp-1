# Solar Eclipse

A solar eclipse is an exciting astronomical event where the Moon passes between the Sun and the Earth, blocking the light from the Sun from reaching us. This happens because the Moon's shadow falls on a small area on the surface of the Earth. The observers in this area witness a total solar eclipse, which is a moment of total darkness during the day. In other areas where the Moon only partially blocks the Sun, people see a partial solar eclipse. During a total solar eclipse, for a few minutes it turns dark during the day as no sunlight reaches the Earth.

## Apparent Size

You might wonder how the Moon, which is much smaller than the Sun, can block the light coming from the Sun. The answer lies in the concept of apparent size. The apparent size of any object as seen by your eye depends upon both its actual physical size and its distance from you. For example, if you ask your friend to stand 5 metres away and consider his head to be the Sun, you can cover his entire head with your thumb. Even though your thumb is much smaller than your friend's head, you can cover it because your thumb is much closer to you. The thumb being much closer to you as compared to your friend, the apparent sizes of your thumb and your friend's head are similar. The apparent sizes of the Moon and the Sun in the sky are similar when viewed from the Earth. This is so because though the Moon is much smaller in physical size than the Sun, the Moon is much closer to us compared to the Sun. Therefore, the Moon can appear to cover the entire Sun as viewed from the Earth.

## Total and Partial Eclipses

During a total solar eclipse, for a few minutes it turns dark during the day as no sunlight reaches the Earth. The shadow of the Moon falls on a small area on the surface of the Earth, and this area is in total darkness, with no part of the Sun visible. The Moon's shadow moves across the surface of the Earth due to the Earth's rotation and the motion of the Moon in its orbit. Thus, the total solar eclipse is visible only for a few minutes in any given location. As the Moon begins to move away from the front of the Sun, we see a partial solar eclipse and daylight begins to return.

## Safe Viewing

Caution—during a solar eclipse, one might be tempted to look at the Sun, thinking that it wouldn't be strong enough to cause harm to our eyes. However, even during the eclipse, the Sun is intense enough to damage the eyes and cause blindness. Thus, directly viewing a solar eclipse must be strictly avoided. Also, do not view it through sunglasses, binoculars, or telescopes. Usually, astronomy organisations, such as planetaria and astronomy clubs, hold eclipse viewing events during a solar eclipse. Participating in such events is the best way to observe a solar eclipse since the organisers not only provide specialised eye protection for solar viewing, but also offer scientific explanations.

## Ancient Beliefs

People have observed eclipses and maintained records since ancient times. When the reasons for eclipses were not known, they feared the occurrence of eclipses. As you can imagine, something blocking the Sun, the main source of heat and light on the Earth, even for a brief period of time, would have been extremely concerning. Many superstitions were attached to solar eclipses in various parts of the world related to the activities that could not be carried out during eclipses—such as eating, cooking, or going out of home. But now that we know the reason why a solar eclipse occurs, we need not fear these events as long as we do not look at the Sun directly. In fact, scientists go around the world to observe the eclipses from wherever these are observable. The eclipses provide them an opportunity to study phenomena that cannot be observed otherwise.

## Worked example

Consider the apparent size of two objects. Imagine a small coin held at arm's length. If you move the coin very close to your eye, it appears to cover a large portion of your vision, even though the coin is physically tiny. Now, imagine a large building far away. Even though the building is much bigger than the coin, it appears very small in your field of view because it is so far away. This is exactly how the Moon and the Sun appear to be the same size in our sky, allowing the Moon to block the Sun during an eclipse.

## Key points
- A solar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes between the Sun and the Earth and its shadow falls on the Earth.
- The apparent size of an object depends on its actual physical size and its distance from the observer.
- The Moon appears to cover the Sun because it is much closer to the Earth, even though it is much smaller in actual physical size.
- A total solar eclipse is visible only in a small area where the Moon's shadow falls, and it lasts for only a few minutes.
- Directly viewing a solar eclipse can cause blindness, so it must be viewed using specialised eye protection or during organised events.
