# Electrical Conductors and Insulators

When we build a simple circuit to light a bulb, we need a material that allows electricity to flow through it. This material acts like a pipe for water, carrying the electric charge from the battery to the lamp. Other materials block this flow entirely, protecting us from shocks. Understanding which materials let electricity pass and which block it is essential for safely using electrical appliances in our daily lives.

## Testing Materials for Conductivity

To figure out which materials allow electricity to flow, we can build a simple tester using a battery, a bulb, and two loose wires. When we connect the wires to the battery and the bulb, the circuit is complete. If we touch the two free ends of the wires together, the bulb glows. This means our tester is working. We can use this setup to test different objects by touching the free ends of the wires to both ends of each object.

Suppose we test a metal spoon, a piece of wood, a plastic scale, and a key. When we touch the tester to the metal spoon and the key, the bulb glows. This tells us that electric current can pass through these objects easily. However, when we test the wood and the plastic, the bulb does not glow at all. This means the electric current cannot pass through them. We can record these outcomes in a table to keep track of our results.

## Defining Conductors and Insulators

Based on our testing, we can group materials into two main categories. The materials through which electric current can flow easily are called good conductors, or simply conductors of electricity. The materials through which current cannot pass through are called insulators, or poor conductors of electricity.

Metals such as silver, copper, and gold are the best electrical conductors. However, for making electrical wires, mainly copper is used due to its comparatively lower cost and abundant supply. Different types of electrical wires are used for different uses.

## Why Wires Are Covered

You might wonder why electric wires are covered with plastic or rubber. From our tests, we would have also realised that plastic, rubber, and ceramics are electrical insulators. Insulators like rubber, plastics, and ceramics are used to cover wires, plug tops, and switches to protect people from electric shocks.

It is important to remember that our body is a conductor of electricity. Electric current passing through our body may cause severe injury or even death. Therefore, always handle electrical appliances with care. Never touch switches or plugs with wet hands, or use electrical devices in wet areas, or handle equipment with damaged insulation or broken plugs.

## Direct and Alternating Current

Have you ever wondered how the electricity from a cell or battery is different from the electricity coming from a wall socket? Electricity from batteries usually powers small devices and is of a type called Direct Current (DC). In contrast, the electricity from power plants that come to the wall socket is known as Alternating Current (AC) and can run larger appliances.

## Worked Example

Imagine you are setting up a small science project and you have a copper wire and a cotton string. You connect the copper wire to your battery and bulb setup, and the bulb glows brightly. This is because copper is a conductor, allowing the current to flow. Now, you replace the copper wire with the cotton string. The bulb does not glow. This happens because cotton acts as an insulator, blocking the current completely.

## Key points

- Materials through which electric current can flow easily are called conductors, while materials that block the current are called insulators.
- Metals like copper and silver are excellent conductors used for making wires, whereas plastics and rubber are common insulators.
- Insulators are used to cover electrical wires and plugs to prevent electric shocks.
- Our bodies conduct electricity, so it is crucial to handle electrical devices carefully, especially with wet hands.
- Electricity from batteries is Direct Current (DC), while electricity from wall sockets is Alternating Current (AC).
