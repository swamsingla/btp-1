# Spherical Mirrors

Have you ever looked closely at the shiny inside of a metallic spoon? If you hold it near your face, you can see your own reflection in it. This simple observation is the perfect way to start learning about spherical mirrors. Unlike the flat mirrors you use every day to check your appearance, these mirrors have curved surfaces. Because of this curve, they reflect light differently, creating images that can be larger, smaller, or upside-down. Understanding how these mirrors work is important because they are used in many everyday tools, from car headlights to dental clinics.

## Concave and Convex Mirrors

Spherical mirrors are special mirrors that are shaped like a piece of a hollow glass sphere. They come in two main types based on the direction their reflecting surface curves. 

A spherical mirror with a reflecting surface that curves inwards is called a concave mirror. Imagine the inside of a spoon; it curves inwards to hold the soup. This inward curve is exactly how a concave mirror looks. 

On the other hand, a spherical mirror with a reflecting surface that curves outwards is called a convex mirror. This is like the outside of a spoon, which bulges outwards. The outer surface of a spoon acts like a convex mirror. 

You can easily tell the difference between them just by looking at them from the side. If the reflecting surface curves inwards, it is concave. If it bulges outwards, it is convex. 

## Characteristics of Images

One of the most interesting things about spherical mirrors is how they form images. The size and orientation of the image depend on the distance between the object and the mirror, and on the type of mirror you are using. 

Let us look at what happens when you place an object very close to a concave mirror. The image formed is upright, but it is larger than the actual object. However, as you slowly move the object farther away from the mirror, the image starts to shrink. Eventually, if you move the object far enough away, the image will flip upside down and become inverted. 

Now, let us consider a convex mirror. If you place an object in front of a convex mirror, the image is always upright. However, the image is always smaller than the object. As you move the object farther away from a convex mirror, the image becomes slightly smaller, but it always remains upright and diminished. 

This behaviour is very different from a plane mirror, which always forms an upright image of the exact same size as the object. However, just like a plane mirror, spherical mirrors also show lateral inversion, which means your left side appears on the right side of the image. 

## Everyday Uses

You might wonder where you can find concave and convex mirrors in real life. They are actually used in many common items around us. 

For example, the reflectors found inside torches and the headlights of cars and scooters are shaped like concave mirrors. Have you ever visited a dentist? A dentist uses a special small mirror to look inside your mouth at your teeth. This mirror is a concave mirror. When the dentist holds it close to your teeth, it provides an enlarged view, making it easier to see what is happening. 

## Worked example

Here is a simple example to show how the size of an image changes with distance in a concave mirror. 

Imagine you are holding a small toy car and placing it 3–4 cm in front of a concave mirror. According to the rules of spherical mirrors, the image of the toy car will be upright and larger than the actual car. 

Now, imagine you slowly move the same toy car farther away from the same concave mirror. At first the image keeps getting smaller. If you move the toy car far enough away, the image becomes inverted. The image is smaller than the actual toy car because the object is now much farther away from the mirror. 

## Key points

*   Spherical mirrors are curved mirrors shaped like a part of a hollow sphere; one that curves inwards is a concave mirror and one that bulges outwards is a convex mirror.
*   A concave mirror forms an upright and enlarged image when the object is close, but an inverted image when the object is far away.
*   A convex mirror always forms an upright and diminished image, regardless of the object's distance.
*   All spherical mirrors, like plane mirrors, show lateral inversion in the image.
*   Concave mirrors are used as reflectors in torches and vehicle headlights, and by dentists to get an enlarged view of teeth.
