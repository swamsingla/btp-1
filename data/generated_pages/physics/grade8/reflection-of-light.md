# Reflection of Light

Reflection of light is the process by which light rays bounce off a surface. This happens when a light ray meets a surface. At the surface, the light changes direction and travels back into the medium it came from. Reflection is a fundamental behaviour of light that allows us to see ourselves in mirrors, read text on a page, and navigate our surroundings.

## Regular Reflection

Regular reflection occurs when light strikes a very smooth surface, such as a polished plane mirror. When the surface is perfectly smooth, all the incoming parallel light rays bounce off in a single, organized direction. As a result, the light rays maintain their parallel paths after bouncing off. 

A simple everyday example is a calm, flat lake on a sunny day. The surface of the water acts like a large mirror, creating a clear reflection of the sky and surrounding trees. This is because the water's surface is smooth enough to cause regular reflection.

## The Laws of Reflection

When light undergoes regular reflection, it follows two strict rules, known as the laws of reflection. The first law states that the angle of incidence is always equal to the angle of reflection. Both of these angles are measured from the normal, an imaginary line drawn perpendicular to the reflecting surface at the exact point where the light hits. The second law states that the incident ray (the incoming ray), the reflected ray (the ray that bounces away) and the normal all lie in the same plane.

To understand how to use these laws, consider a scenario where a light ray strikes a plane mirror. Let us say the angle of incidence is $30^\circ$. According to the first law, the angle of reflection must also be $30^\circ$. Both angles are measured from the normal, which is a line drawn straight out from the mirror's surface at the point of contact, and the reflected ray stays in the same plane as the incident ray and the normal.

## Diffuse Reflection

Not all surfaces are perfectly smooth like a mirror. Rough surfaces, such as a sheet of paper or a wall, cause diffuse reflection. When light hits a rough surface, the microscopic unevenness causes the light rays to scatter in many different directions. Instead of bouncing off in a single organized path, the light spreads out.

Despite scattering, the laws of reflection still apply to each individual ray. However, because the surface is uneven, the normal points in a different direction at each point where a ray hits the surface. This is why you can see a book or a wall from almost any angle you stand in, whereas you must stand in a very specific spot to see your reflection in a mirror. Rough surfaces reflect light, and so does the Moon. The Moon does not make light of its own; its rough, cratered surface scatters sunlight in many directions, which is why we can see it in the night sky.

## Worked Example

Imagine you are shining a laser pointer at a large, flat bathroom mirror. You hold the laser so that it hits the mirror at an angle of $45^\circ$ from the normal. 

According to the laws of reflection, the angle of reflection must be exactly the same as the angle of incidence. Therefore, the reflected laser beam will bounce off the mirror at an angle of $45^\circ$ on the other side of the normal. 

If you were to measure the angle between the incoming laser beam and the reflected beam, you would find it to be $90^\circ$. This is because the total angle is the sum of the angle of incidence and the angle of reflection ($45^\circ + 45^\circ = 90^\circ$). This simple calculation shows how predictable and organized regular reflection is when the surface is smooth.

## Key points
- Reflection is the bouncing back of light rays when they meet a surface.
- Regular reflection happens on smooth surfaces, causing parallel light rays to stay parallel after bouncing.
- The laws of reflection state that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, both are measured from the normal, and the incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal lie in the same plane.
- Diffuse reflection occurs on rough surfaces, scattering light in many directions so objects can be seen from multiple viewpoints.
- The Moon does not make its own light; it scatters sunlight from its rough, uneven surface, which is how we see it from Earth.
