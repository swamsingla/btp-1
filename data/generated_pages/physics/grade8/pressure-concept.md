# Pressure

You may already know that a force is a push or a pull on an object. When you carry a heavy school bag or press a sharp pencil against a paper, you are applying a force. But you may have noticed that the effect of the same force can feel very different depending on how it is applied. A sharp pencil point hurts your skin, but a flat eraser does not. This difference in feeling happens because of a concept called pressure. Pressure tells us how much force is acting on a specific area. It is a measure of how concentrated a force is. Understanding pressure is important because it helps us design safer objects, comfortable tools, and explains how the air around us behaves.

## What is Pressure?

Pressure is defined as the force per unit area. The formula to calculate pressure is very straightforward.

$$
\text{Pressure} = \frac {\text{Force}}{\text{Area}}
$$

This means that if you have a certain amount of force, spreading that force over a larger area will lower the pressure. Conversely, if you keep the same force but apply it to a smaller area, the pressure increases. At this stage, we will consider only those forces which act perpendicular to the surface on which the pressure is to be computed.

Let us look at a practical example. Imagine two buckets, one with a broad handle and one with a narrow handle. Both buckets are completely filled with water and have the exact same weight. If you try to lift them, the bucket with the narrow handle will feel much harder to hold. This happens because the weight of the bucket acts on a smaller area of your hand with the narrow handle. The bucket with the broad handle spreads the same weight over a larger area of your hand. Therefore, the broad handle reduces the pressure exerted by the bucket on your hand, making it feel easier to lift.

## The SI Unit of Pressure

Like all physical quantities, pressure has a standard unit of measurement. The SI unit of force is the newton, and the SI unit of area is the square metre. Therefore, the SI unit of pressure is newton per square metre.

$$
\text{SI unit of pressure} = \frac{\text{newton}}{\text{metre}^{2}} \, (\text{N/m}^{2})
$$

This unit is also called a pascal, and it is denoted by the symbol Pa.

To understand how to use this formula, let us work through a calculation. If a force of 100 N is applied on a cardboard of area $2 \, \text{m}^{2}$, then the pressure applied on the cardboard will be:

$$
\text{Pressure} = \frac {100 \mathrm{N}}{2 \mathrm{m} ^ {2}} = 50 \mathrm{N/m} ^ {2}
$$

## Everyday Applications of Pressure

Pressure is not just a concept for the classroom; it is a part of our daily lives. We can see its effects in many common activities.

When people carry heavy loads like pots or vegetable baskets on their heads, they often place a round piece of cloth under the loads. The round piece of cloth increases the area over which the weight acts. By increasing the area, the cloth reduces the pressure on the person's head. This makes carrying the heavy load much more comfortable.

Similarly, you can see why it feels easier to lift a water-filled bucket with a broad handle than with a narrow handle. The broad handle increases the contact area between your hand and the bucket. This reduces the pressure on your palm, allowing you to hold the bucket for a longer time without pain or discomfort.

## Atmospheric Pressure

The force of pressure is not limited to solid objects like bags and buckets. The air around us also exerts pressure. You already know that air is all around us. The envelope of air surrounding the Earth is called the atmosphere. The atmospheric air contains nitrogen, oxygen, argon, carbon dioxide, and other gases in small quantities. The atmosphere extends up to many kilometres above the surface of the Earth.

Because the atmosphere has mass and is pulled down by gravity, it presses down on everything at the surface of the Earth. This downward push is called atmospheric pressure. You do not usually feel this pressure because the fluids inside your body push outward with an equal amount of pressure, balancing the air around you. However, atmospheric pressure is always there, pressing on every surface.

## Effect of Pressure on Density

Pressure also affects the density of different states of matter. Pressure affects density differently depending on the state of matter. For gases, increasing pressure causes the particles to move closer together. As a result, the volume of the gas decreases and its density increases.

In the case of liquids, pressure has a small effect because they are nearly incompressible. The particles in solids are already very close to each other. So solids are even less affected by pressure than liquids, and changes in their density are usually negligible.

## Worked Example

Let us apply the formula for pressure to a new situation. A wooden box presses on the floor with a force of $600 \, \mathrm{N}$, which is its weight. The box can rest on its wide face, which has an area of $2 \, \mathrm{m}^{2}$, or on its narrow face, which has an area of $0.5 \, \mathrm{m}^{2}$. The force is the same in both cases, and it acts perpendicular to the floor. We can calculate the pressure for both positions using the formula $\text{Pressure} = \frac {\text{Force}}{\text{Area}}$.

On the wide face:
$$
\text{Pressure} = \frac{600 \, \mathrm{N}}{2 \, \mathrm{m}^{2}} = 300 \, \mathrm{N/m}^{2} = 300 \, \mathrm{Pa}
$$

On the narrow face:
$$
\text{Pressure} = \frac{600 \, \mathrm{N}}{0.5 \, \mathrm{m}^{2}} = 1200 \, \mathrm{N/m}^{2} = 1200 \, \mathrm{Pa}
$$

The box exerts four times as much pressure on the floor when it rests on its narrow face, because the same force acts on an area that is four times smaller. This is the same idea as the bucket with the narrow handle: a smaller area means a larger pressure for the same force.

## Key points

*   Pressure is the force per unit area, calculated using the formula $\text{Pressure} = \frac {\text{Force}}{\text{Area}}$.
*   The SI unit of pressure is newton per square metre (N/m$^{2}$), which is also called a pascal (Pa).
*   For the same force, increasing the area over which it acts decreases the pressure.
*   For the same force, decreasing the area over which it acts increases the pressure.
*   The atmosphere is the envelope of air surrounding the Earth, and it exerts pressure on the surface.
*   Increasing pressure on a gas causes its particles to move closer together, decreasing its volume and increasing its density.
*   Liquids are nearly incompressible, so pressure has only a small effect on their density.
*   Solids are even less affected by pressure than liquids, and changes in their density are usually negligible.
