# Lenses

Have you ever watched a watchmaker use a small magnifying glass to inspect tiny parts? If you look closely at the glass, you will notice that it is curved rather than flat. This curved piece of transparent material is called a lens, and it bends light rays to make small objects appear larger. A lens is a transparent material bound by two surfaces, of which one or both surfaces are spherical, forming a lens bound by at least one spherical surface.

## Convex and Concave Lenses

There are two main types of lenses: convex and concave. A double convex lens bulges outwards and is thicker at the middle compared to the edges. This shape causes the lens to converge light rays, which is why convex lenses are also called converging lenses. On the other hand, a double concave lens curves inwards and is thicker at the edges than at the middle. This shape causes the lens to diverge light rays, making it a diverging lens.

## Optical Centre and Principal Axis

A lens has two surfaces, and at least one of them is spherical. The centres of the spheres that these surfaces are part of are called the centres of curvature, usually represented by $C_1$ and $C_2$. An imaginary straight line passing through these two centres of curvature is called the principal axis. The central point of the lens is its optical centre, usually represented by the letter $O$. A ray of light passing through the optical centre of a lens will emerge without suffering any deviation.

## Focus and Focal Length

When parallel rays of light are incident on a convex lens, they refract and converge at a specific point on the principal axis on the other side of the lens. This point is called the principal focus. For a concave lens, the parallel rays appear to diverge from the principal focus located on the same side of the lens. The distance between the optical centre of the lens and its principal focus is called the focal length. According to the sign convention for spherical lenses, the focal length of a convex lens is positive, and that of a concave lens is negative.

## Image Formation Using Ray Diagrams

We can represent image formation by lenses using ray diagrams. To draw these diagrams, we consider specific rules for tracing light rays. A ray of light from the object, parallel to the principal axis, after refraction from a convex lens, passes through the principal focus on the other side of the lens. In case of a concave lens, the ray appears to diverge from the principal focus located on the same side of the lens. Additionally, a ray of light passing through the optical centre of a lens will emerge without any deviation. A ray passing through the principal focus on the object's side of a convex lens emerges parallel to the principal axis.

## Where the Image Forms

The position and nature of the image formed by a lens depend on where the object is placed. Here $F_1$ and $F_2$ are the two principal foci and $2F_1$ and $2F_2$ are the points at twice the focal length from the optical centre $O$.

For a convex lens:

| Position of the object | Position of the image | Size of the image | Nature of the image |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| At infinity | At focus $F_2$ | Highly diminished, point-sized | Real and inverted |
| Beyond $2F_1$ | Between $F_2$ and $2F_2$ | Diminished | Real and inverted |
| At $2F_1$ | At $2F_2$ | Same size | Real and inverted |
| Between $F_1$ and $2F_1$ | Beyond $2F_2$ | Enlarged | Real and inverted |
| At focus $F_1$ | At infinity | Infinitely large or highly enlarged | Real and inverted |
| Between focus $F_1$ and optical centre $O$ | On the same side of the lens as the object | Enlarged | Virtual and erect |

For a concave lens:

| Position of the object | Position of the image | Size of the image | Nature of the image |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| At infinity | At focus $F_1$ | Highly diminished, point-sized | Virtual and erect |
| Between infinity and optical centre $O$ | Between focus $F_1$ and optical centre $O$ | Diminished | Virtual and erect |

A convex lens is the only one of the two that can form a real image, and it forms a virtual, enlarged image when the object is held between the lens and its principal focus. This is how a magnifying glass works.

## Worked example

Consider a convex lens with a focal length of $20$ cm. If an object is placed $30$ cm from the lens, we can use the lens formula to find the image distance $v$. According to the sign convention, the object distance $u$ is $-30$ cm and the focal length $f$ is $+20$ cm. Using the lens formula $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u}$, we can solve for $v$. Substituting the values, we get $\frac{1}{20} = \frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{-30}$, which simplifies to $\frac{1}{20} = \frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{30}$. Solving this equation, we find that $v = +60$ cm. This means the image is formed $60$ cm on the other side of the lens. The object is between $F_1$ and $2F_1$ (between 20 cm and 40 cm), so the table above tells us that the image is beyond $2F_2$, enlarged, real and inverted. The magnification is $m = \frac{v}{u} = \frac{+60}{-30} = -2$, so the image is twice the size of the object, and the negative sign shows that it is inverted.

## Key points

- A lens is a transparent material bound by two surfaces, of which one or both surfaces are spherical.
- A convex lens is thicker at the middle than at the edges and converges light rays, while a concave lens is thicker at the edges than at the middle and diverges light rays.
- The optical centre of a lens is the central point where a ray of light passes without suffering any deviation.
- The focal length of a convex lens is positive, and that of a concave lens is negative.
- A ray of light parallel to the principal axis passes through the principal focus after refraction from a convex lens.
- A convex lens forms a real, inverted image for an object beyond its principal focus, and a virtual, erect, enlarged image when the object is between the focus and the optical centre; a concave lens always forms a virtual, erect, diminished image.
