# Dispersion of Light

Light is a fundamental part of our daily lives, but when it passes through certain materials, it reveals a hidden complexity. White light, which appears uniform to the naked eye, is actually a mixture of many colours. When this light travels through a special shape like a glass prism, it splits apart. This process, known as dispersion, is not just a laboratory curiosity; it explains the beautiful rainbows we see in the sky after a rain shower. Understanding how light behaves in these situations helps us explore the nature of sunlight and the atmosphere around us.

## Refraction Through a Triangular Prism

Before we can understand how white light splits, we must first look at how a single ray of light bends when it passes through a triangular glass prism. A prism is a transparent object that has two triangular bases and three rectangular lateral surfaces. The angle between its two lateral faces is called the angle of the prism.

Imagine shining a beam of light into the prism from the air. When the light ray enters the glass from the air at the first surface, it bends towards a line drawn perpendicular to the surface, which is called the normal. This bending is called refraction. Inside the glass, the ray travels straight to the second surface. When the light exits the glass and enters the air again, it bends away from the normal. 

Because the two surfaces of the prism are inclined to each other, the emergent ray does not travel in the same direction as the incident ray. Instead, it is bent at an angle. This change in direction is called the angle of deviation. In a simple glass slab, the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray, but only slightly displaced sideways. The peculiar shape of the prism causes the emergent ray to bend at a noticeable angle to the original path.

## Splitting White Light

White light from the Sun is not a single colour. When a beam of this white light passes through a glass prism, something exciting happens. The inclined refracting surfaces of the prism cause the light to split into a beautiful band of colours.

To see this, you can use a narrow slit to create a thin beam of sunlight and direct it onto a prism. As the light passes through the prism, it separates into its individual colours. The sequence of colours you will see is Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange, and Red. A common way to remember this sequence is the acronym VIBGYOR. This band of coloured components of a light beam is called its spectrum.

The reason this splitting happens is that different colours of light bend through different angles as they pass through the prism. Red light bends the least, while violet light bends the most. Because each colour emerges along a slightly different path, they become distinct and form a spectrum.

## Formation of Rainbows

The splitting of light by a prism is not just limited to glass objects. You can observe a natural version of this phenomenon in the sky. A rainbow is a natural spectrum that appears after a rain shower. 

Rainbows are caused by the dispersion of sunlight by tiny water droplets present in the atmosphere. These water droplets act exactly like small prisms. When sunlight enters a raindrop, it refracts and disperses into its component colours. The light then reflects internally inside the raindrop and refracts again as it exits. Due to this combination of dispersion and internal reflection, different colours reach the observer's eye.

A rainbow always forms in a direction opposite to that of the Sun. You can also see a rainbow on a sunny day when you look at the sky through a waterfall or a water fountain, provided the Sun is behind you.

## Recombination of Light

Isaac Newton was the first scientist to use a glass prism to study the spectrum of sunlight. He wondered if the colours in the spectrum could be split further. He tried using a second prism to split the colours, but he could not get any more colours. He then placed a second identical prism in an inverted position with respect to the first prism. This allowed all the colours of the spectrum to pass through the second prism. He found that a beam of white light emerged from the other side of the second prism.

This observation gave Newton the crucial idea that sunlight is made up of seven colours. It also showed that the spectrum produced by a prism can be recombined back into white light if the light is passed through an inverted prism.

## Worked Example

To apply the rule that different colours bend through different angles in a prism, consider a simple scenario. Imagine a ray of white light entering a glass prism. Because the red light bends the least, it will emerge from the prism at a smaller angle of deviation compared to the violet light. If the angle of deviation for red light is $5$ degrees, and the angle of deviation for violet light is $8$ degrees, the violet ray will be bent $3$ degrees more than the red ray. This difference in bending is exactly what causes the spectrum to form on a screen placed behind the prism.

## Key points

*   A triangular glass prism has two lateral faces inclined to each other, and the angle between them is called the angle of the prism.
*   When light enters a prism from air to glass, it bends towards the normal, and when it exits from glass to air, it bends away from the normal.
*   The angle between the direction of the incident ray and the emergent ray is called the angle of deviation.
*   White light is dispersed into a band of colours known as a spectrum when it passes through a prism.
*   The sequence of colours in the spectrum is VIBGYOR, where red bends the least and violet bends the most.
*   A rainbow is a natural spectrum formed by the dispersion and internal reflection of sunlight in tiny water droplets in the atmosphere.
*   Isaac Newton used a second inverted prism to show that the spectrum can be recombined to form white light, proving sunlight is made of seven colours.
