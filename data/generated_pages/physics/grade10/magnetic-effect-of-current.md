# Magnetic Effect of Electric Current

An electric current flowing through a metallic conductor produces a magnetic field around it. This magnetic field can be detected using a magnetic compass, which acts as a tiny magnet. The direction and strength of this field depend on the direction of the current and the distance from the wire. Understanding this magnetic effect is essential for building many electrical devices, such as electric bells, motors, fans, loudspeakers, and electromagnets.

## Oersted's Experiment

In 1820, Hans Christian Oersted, a professor at a university in Denmark, noticed that whenever he closed or opened an electrical circuit, the needle of a nearby magnetic compass would deflect. This meant that the electric current in the wire was creating a magnetic field that affected the compass. Oersted investigated this further and published his findings. His work proved that electricity and magnetism are linked, and it inspired other scientists to explore this connection.

## Field Due to a Straight Conductor

When an electric current flows through a straight wire, it creates a magnetic field around the wire. You can observe this effect by setting up a simple circuit with a cell, a switch, and a magnetic compass placed beneath the wire. When you turn the switch to the 'ON' position, the current flows through the wire. The compass needle will deflect from its original direction. When you turn the switch to the 'OFF' position, the current stops, and the magnetic field disappears. The compass needle then returns to its original direction. This experiment clearly shows that the region around a current-carrying wire has a magnetic field.

## Pattern and Direction of the Magnetic Field

The pattern of the magnetic field around a straight conductor carrying current can be investigated using a cardboard and iron filings. Take a long straight thick copper wire and insert it vertically through the centre, normal to the plane of a rectangular cardboard. Connect the copper wire in series with a battery, a plug and key. Sprinkle some iron filings uniformly on the cardboard and close the key so that a current flows through the wire. Gently tap the cardboard a few times. You would find that the iron filings align themselves showing a pattern of concentric circles around the copper wire. These concentric circles represent the magnetic field lines.

To find the direction of the magnetic field, place a compass at a point over one of the circles. The direction of the north pole of the compass needle would give the direction of the field lines. If the current through the straight copper wire is reversed, the direction of the magnetic field lines also gets reversed.

The magnitude of the magnetic field produced at a given point increases as the current through the wire increases. If the current is increased, the deflection in the compass needle also increases. The magnetic field produced by a given current in the conductor decreases as the distance from it increases. The concentric circles representing the magnetic field around a current-carrying straight wire become larger and larger as we move away from it.

## Right-Hand Thumb Rule

A convenient way of finding the direction of magnetic field associated with a current-carrying conductor is given by the right-hand thumb rule. Imagine that you are holding a current-carrying straight conductor in your right hand such that the thumb points towards the direction of current. Then your fingers will wrap around the conductor in the direction of the field lines of the magnetic field. This is known as the right-hand thumb rule.

For example, a current through a horizontal power line flows in the east to west direction. Applying the right-hand thumb rule, we get that the magnetic field at any point below or above the wire turns clockwise in a plane perpendicular to the wire, when viewed from the east end, and anti-clockwise, when viewed from the west end.

## Worked Example

Consider a current-carrying straight conductor. The current in the wire is $3 \mathrm{A}$ and it flows from south to north. You want to find the direction of the magnetic field at a point directly to the east of the wire.

First, imagine that you are holding the conductor in your right hand. Point your thumb in the direction of the current, which is from south to north. Your fingers will wrap around the conductor in the direction of the field lines. With the thumb pointing north, the fingers curl so that, on the east side of the wire, they point downwards into the ground (and on the west side they point upwards).

Therefore, the direction of the magnetic field at a point directly to the east of the wire is vertically downwards.

## Key points

- An electric current produces a magnetic field around the conductor.
- The magnetic field can be detected with a magnetic compass, which deflects when current flows; Hans Christian Oersted discovered this link between electricity and magnetism in 1820.
- The pattern of the magnetic field around a straight conductor is a set of concentric circles.
- The direction of the magnetic field lines is reversed if the direction of current through the straight copper wire is reversed.
- The magnitude of the magnetic field produced at a given point increases as the current through the wire increases.
- The magnetic field produced by a given current in the conductor decreases as the distance from it increases.
- The right-hand thumb rule is used to find the direction of magnetic field associated with a current-carrying conductor.
