# Line Symmetry and Reflection

Line symmetry is a special property of shapes and objects. It occurs when a single straight line divides a figure into two matching halves. These halves are mirror images of each other, meaning if you fold the shape along this line, the two parts will perfectly overlap. Understanding line symmetry helps us recognize patterns in everyday objects, from nature to architecture, and it forms the foundation for the mathematical concept of reflection.

## Defining the Line of Symmetry

A line of symmetry is a straight line that cuts a figure into two parts that exactly overlap when folded along that line. Take a triangle with two equal sides, and fold it along the line that runs from its tip down to the middle of its base. One half of the triangle covers the other half completely. These perfectly matching halves are called mirror halves.

Not every shape has such a line, and not every line drawn through the middle of a shape is one. A line can pass through the exact centre of a figure and still leave the two sides failing to match when it is folded, and in that case it is not a line of symmetry. The test is always the fold: the two parts must land exactly on top of each other.

## Everyday Examples and Symmetry

You can find symmetrical objects all around you: a butterfly, a flower, a traditional rangoli, a leaf. A butterfly's two wings are mirror halves of each other, so the line running down its body is a line of symmetry. Built structures show it too — the Taj Mahal and a temple gopuram are both designed so that the left and right halves mirror each other across a central line. A picture of clouds, by contrast, has no repeating pattern and no line you could fold it along, so it has no line of symmetry.

## Reflection and Mirror Halves

Reflection is closely connected to line symmetry. When a figure has a line of symmetry, it means that one half of the figure is the mirror image of the other half. If you place a mirror on the line of symmetry, the reflection in the mirror will perfectly match the other half of the shape. This is exactly what happens when you fold a shape along its line of symmetry: the two halves reflect each other perfectly.

## Worked Example

Let us apply the rule of line symmetry to a simple shape. A square has four lines of symmetry, and the fold test finds all four. A vertical line down the exact centre makes the left and right halves overlap when folded. A horizontal line across the centre makes the top and bottom halves overlap. Each of the two diagonals, joining one corner to the opposite corner, works as well: folding along either one makes the two triangular halves land exactly on top of each other. Two lines through the centre plus two diagonals makes four in all, which is why a square is such a highly symmetrical shape.

A rectangle that is not a square is a useful contrast. Its vertical and horizontal centre lines are both lines of symmetry, so it has two. Its diagonals are not: fold a long rectangle along a diagonal and the two halves point in different directions instead of matching. So having four equal sides, not just four right angles, is what gives the square its extra two lines.

## Key points

- A line of symmetry divides a figure into two parts that exactly overlap when folded along that line.
- The two matching parts created by a line of symmetry are called mirror halves.
- Not all figures have a line of symmetry, and a line through the middle of a figure is not automatically one: the two sides must land exactly on each other when folded.
- Symmetry is found in nature, such as in butterflies and flowers, as well as in man-made structures like the Taj Mahal and rangoli designs.
- A square has four lines of symmetry — two centre lines and two diagonals — while a rectangle that is not a square has only the two centre lines.
