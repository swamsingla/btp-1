# Measuring Angles with Protractor

Angles are everywhere in our daily lives, from the corners of a book to the hands of a clock. You may already know how to compare two angles to see which one is larger or smaller. However, comparing angles only tells us the relative size. To know exactly how big an angle is, we need to measure it using a number. A protractor is the tool we use to do this. It helps us find the exact measure of an angle in degrees.

## The Degree: A Standard Unit of Angle Measure

To measure an angle precisely, mathematicians decided to divide a full circle into 360 equal parts. Each of these equal parts is called 1 degree, which is written as $1^{\circ}$. The measure of any angle is simply the number of these $1^{\circ}$ unit parts it contains inside it. For example, if an angle contains 30 units of $1^{\circ}$ angle, then its angle measure is $30^{\circ}$. 

This system allows us to assign a precise number to any angle without having to compare it to another angle. A full turn, which is a complete circle, contains 360 of these degrees, so its measure is $360^{\circ}$. A straight angle, which is exactly half of a full turn, measures $180^{\circ}$. A right angle, which is exactly one-quarter of a full turn, measures $90^{\circ}$.

## How to Measure an Angle Using a Protractor

To measure an angle, we place the centre point of the protractor exactly on the vertex of the angle. The vertex is the point where the two arms of the angle meet. We then align one arm of the angle with the straight base line of the protractor, making sure it lines up perfectly with the 0 mark. Once the base is aligned, we look at the curved scale of the protractor. We start from the 0 mark and count the degrees up to where the second arm of the angle crosses the curved edge. The number we land on is the measure of the angle in degrees.

## Worked Example

Let us say you have an angle named $\angle ABC$. You want to find out exactly how many degrees it measures. First, place the centre point of your protractor right on the vertex, point B. Next, rotate the protractor so that one arm, say $\overrightarrow{BA}$, lines up perfectly with the 0 line on the base. Now, look at the curved edge of the protractor. The second arm, $\overrightarrow{BC}$, crosses the curved edge at the number 75. This means that the angle $\angle ABC$ contains 75 unit parts of $1^{\circ}$. Therefore, the measure of $\angle ABC$ is $75^{\circ}$.

## Estimating by Eye

Estimating an angle by eye, before you measure it, is worth practising. You will not always have a protractor to hand, and an estimate catches mistakes. Estimating works by comparing the angle against sizes you already know well: a right angle is $90^{\circ}$, half a right angle is $45^{\circ}$, and a third of a right angle is $30^{\circ}$.

With those three in your eye, an angle that looks a little narrower than the corner of a book is somewhere near $80^{\circ}$. An angle of $49^{\circ}$ looks just over half a right angle. An angle of $34^{\circ}$ looks just over a third of one. Anything clearly wider than a square corner but well short of a straight line is obtuse.

Estimating also guards against the commonest slip. A protractor carries two rows of numbers running in opposite directions, and the two rows always add to $180^{\circ}$. So if an angle plainly looks acute to the eye but the figure you read off is $141^{\circ}$, you have used the wrong row, and the measure you want is $180^{\circ} - 141^{\circ} = 39^{\circ}$. Estimating first tells you which row to trust.

## Key points
* An angle is measured in degrees, where one degree is written as $1^{\circ}$.
* A full turn measures $360^{\circ}$, a straight angle measures $180^{\circ}$, and a right angle measures $90^{\circ}$.
* The measure of an angle is the number of $1^{\circ}$ unit parts it contains inside it.
* To measure an angle, place the centre of the protractor on the vertex and align one arm to the 0 line.
* Count the degrees on the curved scale up to where the second arm crosses to find the angle's measure.
* A protractor has two rows of numbers running opposite ways and they add to $180^{\circ}$, so estimate the angle by eye first to be sure you read the right row.
