# Data Collection and Organisation

Data is all around us, and it helps us understand the world better. If you ask your classmates about their favourite fruits, you will get a list of different answers. This list is an example of data. Similarly, if you measure the weight of each student in your class, you would get a collection of numbers—again, this is data. Any collection of facts, numbers, measures, observations or other descriptions of things that convey information about those things is called data. We live in an age of information. We constantly see large amounts of data presented to us, and learning how to gather and organise it is a very useful skill.

## What is Raw Data?

When you first gather facts or numbers, they are in their most basic form. This is called raw data. Imagine you ask everyone in your class how many pets they own. You might get answers like 1, 3, 0, 2, 1, and 4. This unorganized list is raw data. It is just a collection of facts, but it is not easy to read or understand yet. To make sense of it, we need to organise it.

## Organising with Tally Marks

One of the easiest ways to organise raw data is by using tally marks. Tally marks are simple lines that we use to count things quickly. To make a tally mark, you draw a single vertical line. Every time you count one more item, you draw another line. When you have counted four items, you draw a fifth line diagonally across the first four lines. This group of five looks like a little gate or a bundle.

## Understanding Frequencies

When we organise data, we often want to know how many times a specific value appears. This count is called the frequency. For example, if three students say they have 2 pets, the frequency of students with 2 pets is 3. Tally marks help us find these frequencies very easily. By grouping our tallies in fives, we can count large numbers without losing track.

## Worked Example

Let us see how this works in an everyday situation. Imagine you are collecting data for a school project about the favourite modes of transport for your classmates. You ask 20 students and write down their answers. Your raw data looks like this: bus, car, bus, walk, bus, bike, car, bus, walk, car, bus, walk, car, bus, walk, bike, bus, car, walk, bus. 

To organise this, you can make a table using tally marks. Write down each mode of transport, then go through the raw data one answer at a time and add a mark beside the matching mode.

Counting through the list gives 8 answers of "bus", 5 of "car", 5 of "walk" and 2 of "bike". In tally marks, "bus" is one bundle of five followed by three single marks, "car" is one bundle of five, "walk" is one bundle of five, and "bike" is two single marks.

Now you can see the frequencies clearly: bus 8, car 5, walk 5 and bike 2. The most popular mode of transport is the bus, with a frequency of 8. It is worth checking your work here: the frequencies must add up to the number of students you asked, and $8 + 5 + 5 + 2 = 20$, which matches the 20 students. If they had not added up to 20, you would know a mark had been missed or counted twice.

## Why Organise Data?

Organising data helps us analyse and interpret it. When data is messy, it is hard to find patterns or make decisions. By using tally marks and tables, we turn raw data into useful information. This makes it easier to answer questions about the data. For example, we can quickly see which transport is most popular, or which option is the least popular. This is the first step in data handling, and it sets the stage for making pictographs and bar graphs later on.

## Key points

*   Data is any collection of facts, numbers, measures, observations or descriptions that convey information.
*   Raw data is the unorganized list of facts or numbers collected directly from a source.
*   Tally marks are groups of lines used to count and organise data quickly.
*   A group of four vertical lines with a fifth line drawn diagonally across them represents a frequency of 5.
*   Frequencies are the counts of how many times a specific value or observation occurs.
*   Organising raw data into tables makes it easier to analyse and interpret.
