# Parallel Lines and Transversals

Imagine you are looking at the lines painted on a straight railway track. The two metal rails are perfectly parallel, meaning they never meet, no matter how far they stretch. Now, imagine a strong cross-tie or a piece of wood laid across them at an angle. This cross-piece is exactly what mathematicians call a transversal. Understanding what happens when a transversal cuts through parallel lines is a crucial skill in geometry. It allows you to figure out the exact size of hidden angles just by knowing the size of one.

## What is a Transversal?

You already know what parallel lines are—they are lines in a plane that never intersect. Now, consider what happens when a third line crosses these two parallel lines. This third line is called a transversal. When one line crosses a pair of lines, it forms eight distinct angles. You might wonder if all eight of these angles can have completely different measurements. The answer is no. Because of the rules of vertical angles, there can be a maximum of only four different angle measures. 

To understand this, look at the four angles formed where the transversal meets the first line. Among these, there are two pairs of vertically opposite angles. Vertically opposite angles are always equal to each other. The same logic applies to the four angles formed where the transversal meets the second line. Therefore, when a transversal intersects two lines, it creates eight angles, but these angles can only take on a maximum of four distinct measurements.

## Corresponding Angles

When a transversal crosses parallel lines, special angle pairs are created. One of the most important pairs is the corresponding angles. Corresponding angles are located in the same relative position at each intersection. For example, if you look at the top-left angle where the transversal meets the first line and the top-left angle where it meets the second line, those two are corresponding angles.

Here is the most important rule to remember: if a transversal cuts two parallel lines, the corresponding angles are equal. This means that if one corresponding angle measures $90^\circ$, the angle in the exact same position on the other line will also measure $90^\circ$. 

How can you be absolutely sure that two lines are parallel? You can use this rule in reverse. If a transversal crosses two lines and you find that the corresponding angles are equal, you can confidently say that the two lines are parallel to each other. This rule is both necessary and sufficient for proving that lines are parallel.

## The Role of Right Angles

You may already know that when two lines intersect and all four angles are equal, each angle must be a right angle measuring $90^\circ$. When two lines intersect at right angles, they are called perpendicular lines. 

Let us apply this to parallel lines. Imagine you have a line, which we will call line $l$. You use a set square to draw a second line perpendicular to $l$. This means the angle between them is exactly $90^\circ$. If you slide your set square and draw a third line that is also perpendicular to $l$, you now have two new lines. 

Because both new lines make a $90^\circ$ angle with line $l$, they are parallel to each other. If line $l$ is viewed as a transversal cutting through these two new lines, the corresponding angles are both $90^\circ$. Since the corresponding angles are equal, we can be sure that the two new lines are parallel.

## Worked Example

Let us apply the rule of corresponding angles to a real-world scenario. Imagine you are building a wooden fence. You have two long, straight wooden planks that are perfectly parallel to each other, representing the rails of the fence. You want to attach a horizontal support beam across them to hold them steady. This support beam acts as a transversal.

You measure the angle where the support beam meets the first plank and find that it is exactly $40^\circ$. Because the support beam is a transversal cutting through parallel lines, the corresponding angle on the second plank must also be $40^\circ$. 

Furthermore, because the corresponding angles are equal, you know that the support beam is holding the two planks perfectly parallel and will not cause them to twist or lean. If you were to measure the vertically opposite angle where the beam meets the first plank, it would also be $40^\circ$. The other angles would measure $140^\circ$, but the key takeaway is that the $40^\circ$ corresponding angles prove the planks are parallel.

## Key points

*   A **transversal** is a line that intersects two or more other lines in the same plane.
*   When a transversal crosses a pair of lines, it forms eight angles, which can have a maximum of four distinct angle measures due to vertical angles.
*   If a transversal cuts two parallel lines, the **corresponding angles** are equal.
*   If a transversal cuts two lines and the corresponding angles are equal, the two lines are parallel.
*   A pair of lines that intersect at right angles ($90^\circ$) are called **perpendicular lines**.
