# Algebraic Expressions

Algebraic expressions are mathematical phrases built from variables, constants, and arithmetic operations like addition and multiplication. They help us represent unknown quantities and solve real-world problems. Knowing how to read and evaluate these expressions is important because, without clear rules, a single expression can be interpreted in multiple ways, leading to different answers.

## The Problem of Multiple Interpretations

Sometimes, an expression without a specific context can be evaluated in more than one way. Just like a sentence in language can have different meanings depending on its punctuation, a mathematical expression can also be interpreted differently without clear rules.

Consider the sentence: “Shalini sat next to a friend with toys”. This could mean that the friend has toys, and Shalini sat next to her. Alternatively, it could mean that Shalini has toys, and she sat with them next to her friend. The use of a comma changes the meaning entirely.

A similar confusion happens in mathematics. For example, Mallesh brought 30 marbles to the playground. Arun brought 5 bags of marbles with 4 marbles in each bag. The total number of marbles brought to the playground can be written as the expression $30 + 5 \times 4$.

Without knowing the context, Purna evaluated this expression by adding 30 and 5 first, getting 35, and then multiplying 35 by 4 to get 140. However, Mallesh correctly multiplied 5 and 4 first to get 20, and then added 20 to 30 to get 50. Mallesh is right because the context clearly states that Arun brought 5 bags with 4 marbles in *each* bag.

Just as punctuation marks are used to resolve confusions in language, brackets and the notion of terms are used in mathematics to resolve confusions in evaluating expressions.

## Using Brackets to Specify Order

Brackets are essential tools in expressions. They tell us exactly which operation to perform first. When evaluating an expression having brackets, we need to first find the values of the expressions inside the brackets before performing other operations.

In the marble example, the correct order of operations is clarified by using brackets as follows: $30 + (5 \times 4)$. When evaluating this expression, we first find the value of $5 \times 4$, which is 20, and then do the addition. Thus, this expression describes the number of marbles: $30 + (5 \times 4) = 30 + 20 = 50$.

Consider another example. Irfan bought a pack of biscuits for ₹15 and a packet of toor dal for ₹56. He gave the shopkeeper ₹100. We can write an expression to calculate the change Irfan will get back: $100 - (15 + 56)$.

Can we first subtract 15 from 100 and then add 56 to the result? We would get 141, which is absurd because he would get more money than he paid the shopkeeper! By using brackets, we ensure that we first find the total cost inside the brackets, which is 71, and then subtract it from 100 to get 29. So, Irfan will get back ₹29.

## Terms in Expressions

What if an expression has multiple operations, but the order of operations is not specified by brackets? We use the notion of terms to determine the order.

Terms are the parts of an expression separated by a ‘+’ sign. For example, in $12 + 7$, the terms are 12 and 7.

Now, what are the terms in $83 - 14$? We know that subtracting a number is the same as adding the inverse of the number. The inverse of a given number has the sign opposite to it. For example, the inverse of 14 is -14, and the inverse of -14 is 14. Thus, subtracting 14 from 83 is the same as adding -14 to 83. That is, $83 - 14 = 83 + (-14)$.

Thus, the terms of the expression $83 - 14$ are 83 and -14. All subtractions in an expression are converted to additions in this manner to identify the terms.

Here are some more examples of expressions and their terms:
- $-18 - 3 = (-18) + (-3)$, so the terms are -18 and -3.
- $6 \times 5 + 3 = (6 \times 5) + 3$, so the terms are $6 \times 5$ and 3.
- $2 - 10 + 4 \times 6 = 2 + (-10) + (4 \times 6)$, so the terms are 2, -10, and $4 \times 6$.

Note that $6 \times 5$ and $4 \times 6$ are single terms as they do not have any ‘+’ sign.

## Worked Example

Let's apply these rules to a new situation. Imagine you are at a stationery shop. You buy 3 notebooks, each costing ₹25, and a pencil box for ₹40. You pay the shopkeeper ₹200. We want to find out how much change you will get back.

First, we write an expression for the total cost of the items. The cost of the notebooks is $3 \times 25$, which is ₹75. Adding the cost of the pencil box, the total cost is $75 + 40$, or ₹115.

Next, we write an expression for the change. You paid ₹200, so the change is $200 - (75 + 40)$. Using the rule that we must evaluate the expression inside the brackets first, we get $200 - 115 = 85$.

So, you will get back ₹85.

## Key points

- Expressions built from variables and constants using arithmetic operations can be evaluated in more than one way without clear rules.
- Brackets are used to specify the exact order of operations, and we must evaluate the expressions inside the brackets first.
- Terms are the parts of an expression separated by a ‘+’ sign, and we convert all subtractions to additions to identify the terms.
- The inverse of a given number has the sign opposite to it.
- Expressions having multiple operations without brackets use the notion of terms to determine the order of evaluation.
