# Properties of Triangles

Triangles are three-sided polygons that form the basic building blocks of geometry. Understanding their properties helps us solve everyday problems, from designing buildings to measuring land. In this section, we will explore the fundamental properties of triangles, focusing on how their sides and angles relate to each other, and how we can use these relationships to understand their shapes.

## Classifying Triangles by Their Sides

Triangles can be grouped based on the lengths of their sides. If all three sides of a triangle have the same length, it is called an equilateral triangle. If exactly two sides are equal, the triangle is an isosceles triangle. If all three sides have different lengths, the triangle is called a scalene triangle. Knowing these categories helps us identify the specific properties a triangle might have.

## The Angle-Side Relationship

A very important property of triangles connects the lengths of their sides to the sizes of their angles. If a triangle has two equal sides, then the angles opposite to those equal sides are also equal. We can prove this by drawing an altitude from the vertex between the equal sides down to the base. This altitude splits the triangle into two smaller, congruent triangles. Because these two smaller triangles are congruent, their corresponding angles are equal.

This property also tells us something about equilateral triangles. Since all three sides are equal, all three angles must be equal. We know that the sum of the angles in any triangle is $180^{\circ}$. Therefore, if we divide $180^{\circ}$ by three, we find that each angle in an equilateral triangle measures exactly $60^{\circ}$.

## The Triangle Inequality

Not every set of three numbers can be the lengths of the sides of a triangle. For three line segments to form a triangle, the sum of the lengths of any two sides must be greater than the length of the third side. This rule is called the triangle inequality.

For example, if we try to build a triangle using side lengths of 4 cm, 6 cm, and 8 cm, we can check if it works. The sum of the two shorter sides is $4 + 6 = 10$ cm, which is greater than 8 cm. The sum of the other pairs is also greater than the remaining side. Because this condition is met, a triangle with these measurements can exist.

## Constructing Congruent Triangles

If we know the exact lengths of all three sides of a triangle, we can use that information to construct an identical triangle. This is known as the Side-Side-Side (SSS) condition for congruence. If two triangles have the same three side lengths, they are congruent, meaning they have the same size and shape.

Imagine you need to make a cardboard cutout identical to a triangular frame. You can measure the three sides of the frame. If you measure 40 cm, 60 cm, and 80 cm, you have enough information to draw a new triangle with those exact dimensions. You do not need to measure the angles at all. You can draw a base line, and then use a compass to draw arcs from the endpoints to find the third vertex, ensuring the new shape is perfectly congruent to the original.

## Worked Example

Let us apply the angle-side relationship to find the missing angles in a triangle. Suppose we have an isosceles triangle where the two equal sides are 5 cm, and the angle between them is $80^{\circ}$. We want to find the measure of the other two angles.

Because the sides are equal, the angles opposite them must be equal. Let us call each of these angles $x$. The sum of the angles in a triangle must be $180^{\circ}$. So, we can write the equation: $x + x + 80^{\circ} = 180^{\circ}$. Combining the $x$ terms gives $2x + 80^{\circ} = 180^{\circ}$. Subtracting $80^{\circ}$ from both sides gives $2x = 100^{\circ}$. Dividing by two, we find that $x = 50^{\circ}$. Therefore, each of the other two angles in the triangle measures $50^{\circ}$.

## Key points

*   If a triangle has two equal sides, the angles opposite those sides are equal.
*   In an equilateral triangle, all three sides are equal, so all three angles are equal to $60^{\circ}$.
*   The sum of the lengths of any two sides of a triangle must be greater than the length of the third side.
*   If two triangles have the same three side lengths, they are congruent by the SSS condition.
*   Knowing the three side lengths is sufficient to construct a triangle; measuring the angles is not required.
