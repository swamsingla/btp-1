# Profit, Loss and Discount

Understanding how money changes hands is a very useful skill. When you buy or sell things, the prices are often given as percentages of the original cost. This helps us compare different deals and see if we are getting a good value. In this section, we will learn how to calculate profit, loss, and discount using simple percentage formulas.

## The Price of Things

When a shopkeeper buys an item from a manufacturer or wholesaler, they pay a specific amount for it. This amount is called the cost price (CP). The shopkeeper then decides to sell the item at a higher price to make a profit. This higher, original price is called the marked price (MP). However, the shopkeeper often offers a discount on this marked price to attract customers. The final price that the customer actually pays is called the selling price (SP).

For example, imagine a retailer buys a sweater for ₹300. He decides to sell it for ₹480. If he manages to sell it for ₹430 after some bargaining, the ₹430 is the selling price. The ₹300 is the cost price. Because the selling price is greater than the cost price, the retailer makes a profit.

## Calculating Profit and Loss

Profit is the extra money you make when the selling price is higher than the cost price. To find the profit percentage, we compare the profit amount to the cost price. The formula for profit percentage is the profit divided by the cost price, multiplied by 100. 

For instance, if a retailer buys a sweater for ₹300 and sells it for ₹430, the profit is ₹130. The profit percentage is $\frac{130}{300} \times 100 = 43.33\%$. 

Sometimes, a shopkeeper has to sell an item for less than what they paid for it. This is called a loss. The loss is the difference between the cost price and the selling price. To find the loss percentage, we divide the loss amount by the cost price and multiply by 100. 

For example, a provision store owner buys 10 kg of rice at ₹35 per kg. He pays a total of ₹350. To clear his old stock, he sells the 10 kg for ₹300. The loss is ₹50. The loss percentage is $\frac{50}{350} \times 100 = 14.29\%$.

## Calculating Discount

Discounts are a common way shops encourage customers to buy items. A discount means the shop reduces the marked price of an item. For example, if a store offers a 30% discount, it means they reduce the price by 30% of the marked price.

To find the selling price after a discount, we can calculate the discount amount and subtract it from the marked price. Alternatively, we can find what percentage of the marked price we actually pay. 

For example, a utensil store offers a 35% discount on a cooker with an MRP of ₹1800. The discount amount is 35% of ₹1800, which is ₹630. The selling price would be the marked price minus the discount, so ₹1800 - ₹630 = ₹1170.

## Worked Example

Let us look at a real-world scenario to see how these calculations work together. Suppose a shopkeeper buys 200 notebooks at ₹36 per book. She wants to sell them with a profit margin of 20%. To find the selling price, we first calculate 20% of the cost price. 20% of ₹36 is $0.20 \times 36 = 7.2$. We add this profit to the cost price to get the selling price. The selling price is ₹36 + ₹7.2 = ₹43.2.

## Key points

*   Cost price (CP) is the amount paid to buy an item, marked price (MP) is the original selling price, and selling price (SP) is the final amount paid by the customer.
*   Profit occurs when the selling price is greater than the cost price. The profit percentage is calculated as $\frac{\text{Profit}}{\text{Cost Price}} \times 100$.
*   Loss occurs when the selling price is less than the cost price. The loss percentage is calculated as $\frac{\text{Loss}}{\text{Cost Price}} \times 100$.
*   A discount is a reduction on the marked price. The selling price is the marked price minus the discount amount.
