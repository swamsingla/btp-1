# Volume of Sphere

A sphere is a perfectly round three-dimensional shape, like a cricket ball or a marble. Just as a cuboid takes up space, a sphere also occupies a certain amount of space inside it. This amount of space is called its volume. Understanding the volume of a sphere is useful in everyday life. For example, knowing the volume helps determine how much water a spherical tank can hold, or how much material is needed to build a round sports ball.

## Finding the Volume by Displacement

We can find the volume of a sphere using a simple water displacement experiment. First, take a container large enough to hold a sphere and fill it completely to the brim with water. Then, gently place a sphere into the container. Because the sphere takes up space, some of the water will overflow into a larger trough. Collect this overflowed water in a measuring cylinder to find its exact volume. You can measure the diameter of the sphere using a ruler and halve it to get the radius. If the radius of the immersed sphere is $r$, then evaluate $\frac{4}{3} \pi r^3$. You will find this value is almost equal to the measure of the volume of the water that overflowed. 

If you repeat this procedure with a different size of sphere and find the radius $R$, you will calculate $\frac{4}{3}\pi R^{3}$. Once again, this value is nearly equal to the measure of the volume of the water displaced by the sphere. This tells us that the volume of the sphere is the same as the measure of the volume of the water displaced by it. By doing this experiment repeatedly with spheres of varying radii, we are getting the same result, namely, the volume of a sphere is equal to $\frac{4}{3}\pi$ times the cube of its radius.

## The Formula for Volume

This experiment gives us the idea that the formula for the volume of a sphere is:

Volume of a Sphere $= \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$

where $r$ is the radius of the sphere.

Notice that the radius is cubed, not squared. This is because volume measures space in three directions. A useful consequence is that a small change in the radius makes a big change in the volume. If the radius is doubled, $r$ becomes $2r$, and the volume becomes $\frac{4}{3}\pi (2r)^3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi \times 8r^3$, which is $8$ times the original volume. Volume is measured in cubic units, such as cm$^3$.

Later, in higher classes, this formula can be proved also. But at this stage, we will just take it as true.

## Volume of a Hemisphere

A hemisphere is exactly half of a sphere. If you have a bowl cut perfectly in half, each half is a hemisphere. Since a hemisphere is half of a sphere, we can easily find its volume by taking half of the sphere's volume formula. 

Yes, it is $\frac{1}{2}$ of $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}$

So, Volume of a Hemisphere = $\frac{2}{3}\pi r^{3}$

where $r$ is the radius of the hemisphere.

## Worked example

Let us take an example to illustrate the use of these formulae. 

Find the volume of a sphere of radius $11.2\mathrm{cm}$.

Solution : Required volume = $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}$

$=\frac{4}{3}\times\frac{22}{7}\times11.2\times11.2\times11.2\ cm^{3}=5887.32\ cm^{3}$.

Now a smaller sphere, such as a marble with radius $3$ cm. Its volume is $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 = \frac{4}{3} \times \pi \times 27 = 36\pi$ cm$^3$. Using $\pi = 3.14$, this is $36 \times 3.14 = 113.04$ cm$^3$.

Next, a hemisphere. Suppose a hemispherical bowl has radius $21$ cm. Its volume is $\frac{2}{3}\pi r^3 = \frac{2}{3} \times \frac{22}{7} \times 21 \times 21 \times 21$ cm$^3$. Since $21 \div 7 = 3$, this becomes $\frac{2}{3} \times 22 \times 3 \times 21 \times 21 = 2 \times 22 \times 21 \times 21 = 19404$ cm$^3$. So the bowl holds $19404$ cm$^3$ when completely full.

Finally, a check that links the two formulas. A sphere of radius $3$ cm has volume $36\pi$ cm$^3$, and a hemisphere of the same radius has volume $\frac{2}{3}\pi \times 27 = 18\pi$ cm$^3$. The hemisphere is exactly half of the sphere, as it should be.

## Key points
* The volume of a sphere is calculated using the formula $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$, where $r$ is the radius.
* The volume of a hemisphere is calculated using the formula $\frac{2}{3}\pi r^3$.
* The volume of a sphere is equal to the measure of the volume of the water displaced by it when fully immersed.
* In Grade 9, we take the sphere volume formula as true, and a formal proof is covered in higher classes.
* Doubling the radius of a sphere makes its volume $8$ times as large, because the radius is cubed.
