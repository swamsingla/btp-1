# Congruence of Triangles

When two triangles are congruent, they are identical in every way, meaning their corresponding sides and angles match perfectly. This concept is highly useful in everyday life, such as when carpenters or engineers need to create identical triangular frames, cutouts, or structural supports for buildings and bridges. You may already know that congruence means two figures have the exact same size and shape. In this section, we will explore the specific criteria used to prove that two triangles are congruent without having to measure every single side and angle.

## The SSS Congruence Rule

Let us start with the Side-Side-Side condition. Construct two triangles with sides 4 cm, 3.5 cm and 4.5 cm. Cut them out and place them on each other. What do you observe? They cover each other completely, if the equal sides are placed on each other. So, the triangles are congruent.

This observation leads to the SSS congruence rule. Theorem 7.4 states: If three sides of one triangle are equal to the three sides of another triangle, then the two triangles are congruent. This rule is quite straightforward to understand because if the lengths of all three sides are fixed, the shape and size of the triangle are uniquely determined. There is no way to bend or twist the sides to form a different triangle.

Let us look at a quick example. Suppose we have a triangle with side lengths 5 cm, 7 cm, and 10 cm. If we construct another triangle with sides 5 cm, 7 cm, and 10 cm, we will find that it is exactly the same as the first one. We do not need to measure the angles to know that the two triangles are congruent. The SSS rule guarantees it. This is a powerful tool in geometry because it allows us to prove congruence using only measurements of length.

## The SAS Congruence Rule

In earlier classes, you have learnt four criteria for congruence of triangles. Let us recall them one by one, starting with the Side-Angle-Side rule.

To understand why the included angle is so important, try to construct two triangles with one side 3 cm. Are these triangles congruent? Observe that they are not congruent. Now, draw two triangles with one side 4 cm and one angle $50^{\circ}$. Are they congruent? See that these two triangles are not congruent.

This shows that equality of one pair of sides or one pair of sides and one pair of angles is not sufficient to give us congruent triangles. What would happen if the other pair of arms (sides) of the equal angles are also equal?

In two triangles, if two sides and the angle between them are equal, the triangles are congruent. This is the SAS congruence rule. Axiom 7.1 states: Two triangles are congruent if two sides and the included angle of one triangle are equal to the two sides and the included angle of the other triangle.

This rule cannot be proved with the help of previously known results and so it is accepted true as an axiom. The included angle is the angle that lies between the two given sides. If the angle is not included, the triangles may not be congruent. For example, if you have two sides and an angle that is not between them, you might be able to form two different triangles. This is why the SAS rule is so specific and important.

## The ASA and AAS Congruence Rules

Next, try to construct the two triangles in which two angles are $60^{\circ}$ and $45^{\circ}$ and the side included between these angles is 4 cm. Cut out these triangles and place one triangle on the other. What do you observe? See that one triangle covers the other completely; that is, the two triangles are congruent.

This is the Angle-Side-Angle criterion for congruence and is written as ASA criterion. You have verified this criterion in earlier classes, but let us state and prove this result. Since this result can be proved, it is called a theorem and to prove it, we use the SAS axiom for congruence.

Theorem 7.1 states: Two triangles are congruent if two angles and the included side of one triangle are equal to two angles and the included side of other triangle.

Suppose, now in two triangles two pairs of angles and one pair of corresponding sides are equal but the side is not included between the corresponding equal pairs of angles. Are the triangles still congruent? You will observe that they are congruent.

You know that the sum of the three angles of a triangle is $180^{\circ}$. So if two pairs of angles are equal, the third pair is also equal ( $180^{\circ}$ – sum of equal angles). So, two triangles are congruent if any two pairs of angles and one pair of corresponding sides are equal. We may call it as the AAS Congruence Rule.

## The RHS Congruence Rule

You have already seen that in the SAS congruence rule, the pair of equal angles has to be the included angle between the pairs of corresponding pair of equal sides and if this is not so, the two triangles may not be congruent.

Perform this activity: Construct two right angled triangles with hypotenuse equal to 5 cm and one side equal to 4 cm each. Cut them out and place one triangle over the other with equal side placed on each other. Turn the triangles, if necessary. What do you observe?

The two triangles cover each other completely and so they are congruent. Repeat this activity with other pairs of right triangles. What do you observe?

You will find that two right triangles are congruent if one pair of sides and the hypotenuse are equal. You have verified this in earlier classes. Note that, the right angle is not the included angle in this case. So, you arrive at the following congruence rule: Theorem 7.5 (RHS congruence rule): If in two right triangles the hypotenuse and one side of one triangle are equal to the hypotenuse and one side of the other triangle, then the two triangles are congruent.

Note that RHS stands for Right angle - Hypotenuse - Side. It has its own name because right triangles come up so often. It applies only to right-angled triangles.

## Worked example

Let us apply the SSS and SAS congruence rules to a real problem. Here is the problem: AB is a line-segment. P and Q are points on opposite sides of AB such that each of them is equidistant from the points A and B. Show that the line PQ is the perpendicular bisector of AB.

Solution: You are given that PA = PB and QA = QB and you are to show that PQ ⊥ AB and PQ bisects AB. Let PQ intersect AB at C.

Can you think of two congruent triangles here? Let us take $\Delta$ PAQ and $\Delta$ PBQ. In these triangles, AP = BP (Given), AQ = BQ (Given), and PQ = PQ (Common).

Therefore, $\Delta$ PAQ $\cong$ $\Delta$ PBQ (SSS rule).

Therefore, $\angle$ APQ = $\angle$ BPQ (CPCT, i.e. corresponding parts of congruent triangles).

Now let us consider $\Delta$ PAC and $\Delta$ PBC. You have: AP = BP (Given), $\angle$ APC = $\angle$ BPC ($\angle$ APQ = $\angle$ BPQ proved above), and PC = PC (Common).

Therefore, $\Delta$ PAC $\cong$ $\Delta$ PBC (SAS rule).

Therefore, AC = BC (CPCT) and $\angle$ ACP = $\angle$ BCP (CPCT).

Also, $\angle$ ACP + $\angle$ BCP = $180^{\circ}$ (Linear pair).

$2 \angle$ ACP = $180^{\circ}$.

or, $\angle$ ACP = $90^{\circ}$.

From the above, you can easily conclude that PQ is the perpendicular bisector of AB.
