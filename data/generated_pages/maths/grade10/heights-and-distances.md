# Heights and Distances

Trigonometry is not just an abstract set of ratios; it is a powerful tool for measuring the world around you. You can use the trigonometric ratios you learned in the previous chapter to find the height of a tower or the distance to the opposite bank of a river without ever touching a measuring tape. This chapter shows you how to apply these mathematical concepts to real-world problems involving heights and distances, using angles of elevation and depression.

## The Line of Sight and Angles

To measure heights and distances, you first need to understand the line of sight and the angles it creates with the horizontal. The line of sight is the straight line drawn from the eye of an observer to the point on the object being viewed. 

When you look up at an object, such as the top of a minar, the angle formed between your line of sight and the horizontal level is called the angle of elevation. This angle is formed when the point being viewed is above the horizontal level, which is the case when you raise your head to look at the object.

Conversely, when you look down at an object, the angle formed between your line of sight and the horizontal is called the angle of depression. This angle is formed when the point is below the horizontal level, which is the case when you lower your head to look at the point being viewed.

## Finding the Height of an Object

To find the height of a tall object, such as a minar or a tower, without actually measuring it, you need three pieces of information. First, you need to know the distance from your eye to the foot of the object. Second, you need to know the angle of elevation to the top of the object. Third, you need to know the height of the observer.

Once you have these values, you can use a right-angled triangle to solve for the unknown height. The total height of the object is the sum of the observer's height and the vertical height from the observer's eye level to the top of the object. To find the vertical height, you use the trigonometric ratios that involve the angle of elevation and the distance to the object.

For example, if you know the angle of elevation and the distance from the object, you can use the tangent or cotangent of the angle to find the vertical height. The tangent of the angle of elevation is equal to the opposite side (the vertical height) divided by the adjacent side (the distance to the object). By rearranging this formula, you can solve for the vertical height. Adding the observer's height to this result gives you the total height of the object.

## Using Trigonometric Ratios

When solving problems involving heights and distances, you must choose the correct trigonometric ratio based on the information you have and the value you need to find. If you know the angle of elevation and the distance to the object, and you need to find the height, you should use the tangent ratio.

The tangent of the angle of elevation is equal to the height of the object divided by the distance from the object. This formula is written as $\tan A = \frac{\text{height}}{\text{distance}}$. If you need to find the distance instead of the height, you can use the cotangent ratio, which is the reciprocal of the tangent ratio.

In some problems, you may need to find the length of the hypotenuse, which is the line of sight itself. In this case, you should use the sine ratio. The sine of the angle of elevation is equal to the opposite side (the height) divided by the hypotenuse (the line of sight).

## Worked Example

Suppose a student is standing 20 m away from a vertical pole. The student's eyes are 1.5 m above the ground, and the angle of elevation to the top of the pole is $30^{\circ}$. To find the total height of the pole, we first find the vertical height from the student's eyes to the top of the pole.

Using the tangent ratio, we have $\tan 30^{\circ} = \frac{\text{height}}{20}$. Since $\tan 30^{\circ} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$, we can write $\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{\text{height}}{20}$. Solving for the height, we get $\text{height} = \frac{20}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 11.55$ m.

To find the total height of the pole, we add the student's eye level to this height. The total height is $11.55 + 1.5 = 13.05$ m. Therefore, the height of the pole is approximately 13.05 m.
