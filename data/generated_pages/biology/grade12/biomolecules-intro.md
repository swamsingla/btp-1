# Biomolecules — Introduction

Living organisms are made of chemicals. The water you drink, the food you eat, and the air you breathe all contain chemical compounds. These compounds are called biomolecules. Biomolecules are the basic building blocks of life. They control every process inside your body, from growing hair to fighting off a cold. Without them, life would not exist. To understand how living things work, we must look at the four main types of biomolecules. These are carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. Each type has a specific job to do. Some provide energy, while others build structures or carry information. In this page, we will explore what these four classes of biomolecules are and what they do.

## The Two Types of Biomolecules

Biomolecules can be grouped into two main categories. The first group contains small molecules. These have molecular weights less than one thousand daltons. They are usually called micromolecules. The second group contains large molecules. These have molecular weights of ten thousand daltons or more. They are called macromolecules or biomacromolecules. 

How do scientists separate these molecules? They use a method involving acids. The acid soluble pool contains the small molecules. This pool represents the normal liquid inside a cell, called the cytoplasm. The acid insoluble fraction contains the large molecules. 

But there is a tricky one. Lipids are small molecules. Their molecular weights do not exceed 800 daltons. So why do they end up in the large, acid insoluble fraction? Lipids are not strictly macromolecules. The reason is their physical behavior. When a scientist grinds a piece of tissue to study it, the cell structures break. The cell membranes break into tiny pieces called vesicles. These vesicles are not water soluble. They get separated along with the acid insoluble pool. 

Together, these two groups of molecules represent the entire chemical composition of living tissues. 

## Proteins: The Workhorses

Proteins are essential for life. They are called polypeptides. A protein is a linear chain of amino acids. 

There are 20 different types of amino acids. Examples include alanine, cysteine, proline, tryptophan, and lysine. Because a protein is made of many different amino acids, it is called a heteropolymer. A homopolymer would have only one type of amino acid repeating over and over. 

Proteins carry out many functions in living organisms. Some transport nutrients across the cell membrane. Some fight infectious organisms. Some act as hormones. Others act as enzymes. 

For example, insulin is a hormone. Antibodies fight infectious agents. GLUT-4 is a protein that enables glucose to move into cells. Collagen is the most abundant protein in the animal world. RuBisCO is the most abundant protein in the whole biosphere. 

## Nucleic Acids: The Information Carriers

The other type of macromolecule in the acid insoluble fraction is the nucleic acid. These are polynucleotides. 

A nucleic acid is built from a building block called a nucleotide. A nucleotide has three chemically distinct components. The first is a heterocyclic compound. The second is a monosaccharide. The third is a phosphoric acid, or phosphate. 

The heterocyclic compounds in nucleic acids are called nitrogenous bases. These bases are named adenine, guanine, uracil, cytosine, and thymine. Adenine and guanine are substituted purines. The rest are substituted pyrimidines. The skeletal heterocyclic ring is called a purine or a pyrimidine. The sugar found in these molecules is either ribose, which is a pentose, or 2' deoxyribose. 

A nucleic acid containing deoxyribose is called deoxyribonucleic acid, or DNA. A nucleic acid containing ribose is called ribonucleic acid, or RNA.

## Carbohydrates and Lipids: Energy and Structure

The acid insoluble fraction also contains carbohydrates and lipids. Together with proteins and nucleic acids, these comprise the true macromolecular fraction of any living tissue or cell. 

Carbohydrates and lipids make up a smaller percentage of the cell compared to proteins and nucleic acids. According to the average composition of cells, water is the most abundant chemical, making up 70 to 90 percent of the total cellular mass. Proteins make up 10 to 15 percent. Carbohydrates make up 3 percent. Lipids make up 2 percent. Nucleic acids make up 5 to 7 percent. Ions make up the remaining 1 percent.

## Worked example

Here is an example to show how we classify molecules based on their size. Imagine a scientist is studying a piece of plant tissue. She measures the molecular weight of a compound and finds it is 950 daltons. Based on the rule from the text, this compound is a micromolecule because its molecular weight is less than one thousand daltons. 

Now, imagine she measures another compound and finds its molecular weight is 12,000 daltons. This compound is a macromolecule because its molecular weight is above ten thousand daltons. 

Finally, imagine she finds a lipid with a molecular weight of 700 daltons. Even though this is a small molecule, it will likely end up in the acid insoluble fraction when the tissue is ground. This happens because the lipid forms cell membranes, which break into water-insoluble vesicles during grinding.

## Key points

* Biomolecules are the chemical compounds found in living organisms and are divided into micromolecules (less than 1000 Da) and macromolecules (10,000 Da and above).
* The acid soluble pool contains micromolecules, while the acid insoluble fraction contains macromolecules.
* Lipids are small molecules (under 800 Da) but appear in the acid insoluble fraction because they form cell membranes that break into water-insoluble vesicles.
* Proteins are polypeptides made of 20 different amino acids and carry out many functions like transport, defense, and hormone action.
* Nucleic acids are polynucleotides built from nucleotides, which contain a nitrogenous base, a sugar (ribose or deoxyribose), and a phosphate group.
