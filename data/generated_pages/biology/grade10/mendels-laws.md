# Mendel's Laws of Inheritance

You may already know that both your father and mother contribute practically equal amounts of genetic material to you. This means that for every trait, like eye colour or height, you receive two versions of the instructions. But what trait will actually show up in you? It could be a mixture of your parents, or it could be exactly like one of them. Over a century ago, a scientist named Gregor Johann Mendel figured out the main rules of how these traits are passed down from parents to children. He used pea plants and carefully counted the offspring to discover the patterns of inheritance.

## The Law of Dominance

When Mendel crossed a tall pea plant with a short pea plant, he noticed something very interesting. In the first generation of offspring, called the F1 generation, there were no plants that were of medium height. Every single plant was tall. This means that only one of the parental traits was seen, and there was no mixture of the two. 

The tall plants in the F1 generation might not have been exactly the same as the tall plants of the original parent generation. Mendel tested this by letting the F1 tall plants reproduce by self-pollination. The second generation, or F2, offspring of these F1 tall plants were not all tall. Instead, one quarter of them were short. This tells us that both the tallness and shortness traits were inherited by the F1 plants, but only the tallness trait was expressed. Mendel proposed that two copies of factors controlling traits are present in sexually reproducing organisms. These two copies may be identical or different, depending on the parentage.

## The Law of Segregation

Because the F1 tall plants carried both the tallness and shortness traits but only showed the tallness, Mendel realized that the two versions of a trait must separate when the plant makes its reproductive cells. This is known as the Law of Segregation. Each parent contributes one copy of a gene to the offspring. This ensures that each sexually reproducing organism has two sets of all genes, one inherited from each parent.

For this to work, each germ cell must have only one gene set. This happens because each gene set is present, not as a single long thread of DNA, but as separate independent pieces, each called a chromosome. Every cell will have two copies of each chromosome, one from the male and one from the female parent. When reproductive cells are formed, they take one chromosome from each pair. When two germ cells combine during fertilization, they restore the normal number of chromosomes in the offspring, so the chromosome number stays the same from generation to generation.

## The Law of Independent Assortment

Mendel also studied how two different traits are inherited at the same time. He looked at the shape and colour of seeds. He found that these two characteristics are inherited independently of each other. This means that the inheritance of one trait does not affect the inheritance of another trait. 

This independent assortment happens when the genes for different traits are located on separate chromosomes. Since every germ cell takes one chromosome from each pair, and these chromosomes are independent pieces, the traits they control are passed down separately.

## How Genes Control Traits

But how does a gene actually control a physical trait? The mechanism of heredity works through cellular DNA. A section of DNA that provides information for one protein is called a gene. Genes control characteristics by determining how much of a certain substance is produced in the body.

For example, plant height can depend on the amount of a particular plant hormone. The amount of hormone produced depends on the efficiency of the process for making it. If a gene codes for an enzyme that is very efficient, a lot of hormone will be made, and the plant will be tall. If the gene has an alteration that makes the enzyme less efficient, less hormone will be made, and the plant will be short.

## Worked Example

Let us look at a simple monohybrid cross. Imagine you cross a pure-breeding tall pea plant with a pure-breeding short pea plant. According to Mendel's findings, the F1 generation will all be tall because the tallness trait is dominant. 

Now, let us cross two of these F1 tall plants with each other. According to the Law of Segregation, each parent will pass on one of its two trait versions to the offspring. Suppose the F2 generation has 400 plants. One quarter of them, about 100, will be short, while the remaining three quarters, about 300, will be tall. So the tall and short plants appear in the ratio 3 : 1. This shows that even though the F1 plants looked exactly like the original tall parent, they still carried the hidden shortness trait.

## Key points

*   Both parents contribute practically equal amounts of genetic material to the child, giving two versions of each trait.
*   The Law of Dominance states that only one of the parental traits is seen in the F1 generation, with no mixture of the two.
*   The Law of Segregation states that two copies of a gene are present, but they separate into reproductive cells so each cell gets only one copy.
*   The Law of Independent Assortment states that different traits are inherited independently because genes are on separate chromosomes.
*   Genes control traits by providing the information to make proteins, which in turn control the amount of substances like hormones in the body.
