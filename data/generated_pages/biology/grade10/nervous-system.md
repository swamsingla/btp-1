# Nervous System

The nervous system is the body's rapid communication and control network, allowing us to react to the world around us. When you accidentally touch a hot pan, you pull your hand away almost instantly. This speed is essential for survival, as it protects us from immediate danger. The nervous system achieves this by transmitting electrical signals through specialized cells called neurons, ensuring that information about your environment is quickly detected and acted upon.

## The Central and Peripheral Nervous Systems

The nervous system is divided into two main parts. The central nervous system (CNS) consists of the brain and the spinal cord. The brain is the main coordinating centre of the body. It receives information from all parts of the body and integrates it. It also allows us to think and take actions based on that thinking, such as moving a chair or clapping at the end of a programme. These are voluntary actions, which are based on deciding what to do next.

The brain has three major regions: the fore-brain, mid-brain, and hind-brain. The fore-brain is the main thinking part. It has regions that receive sensory impulses from various receptors, and separate areas of association where sensory information is interpreted. Based on this, a decision is made about how to respond. The hind-brain controls involuntary actions, including blood pressure, salivation, and vomiting, through the medulla. The cerebellum, another part of the hind-brain, is responsible for the precision of voluntary actions and maintaining the posture and balance of the body.

The second major part is the peripheral nervous system (PNS). This consists of cranial nerves arising from the brain and spinal nerves arising from the spinal cord. The PNS facilitates the communication between the central nervous system and the other parts of the body.

## Neuron Structure and Impulse Transmission

Nervous tissue is made up of an organised network of nerve cells or neurons. These are specialised for conducting information via electrical impulses from one part of the body to another. A neuron has three main parts. Information is acquired at the dendritic tip, which are usually located in our sense organs such as the inner ear, the nose, and the tongue. This information travels from the dendrite to the cell body, and then along the axon to its end. At the end of the axon, the electrical impulse sets off the release of some chemicals.

The simplest notion of movement at the cellular level is that muscle cells will move by changing their shape so that they shorten. Muscle cells have special proteins that change both their shape and their arrangement in the cell in response to nervous electrical impulses. When this happens, new arrangements of these proteins give the muscle cells a shorter form.

## The Synapse and Reflex Arc

A synapse is the gap between the end of one axon and the beginning of the next neuron's dendrite. At the end of the axon, the electrical impulse sets off the release of some chemicals. These chemicals cross the gap, or synapse, and start a similar electrical impulse in a dendrite of the next neuron. This is a general scheme of how nervous impulses travel in the body.

A reflex arc is the neural pathway that controls a reflex action, a quick and involuntary response to a stimulus. For example, when touching a hot object, the information is detected by specialised tips of nerve cells called receptors. This information sets off a chemical reaction that creates an electrical impulse. The impulse travels along a sensory neuron to the spinal cord, where it passes across synapses to a motor neuron. The motor neuron carries the impulse to a muscle, which responds. Because the impulse is handled at the spinal cord and does not have to be decided by the thinking part of the brain, the response is very fast. A similar synapse finally allows delivery of such impulses from neurons to other cells, such as muscle cells or glands.

## Worked Example

Imagine you accidentally touch a hot cup of tea. The receptors in your skin detect the heat. This information is acquired at the dendritic tip of a sensory neuron. The electrical impulse travels from the dendrite to the cell body, and then along the axon to its end. At the end of the axon, the electrical impulse sets off the release of some chemicals. These chemicals cross the synapse and start a similar electrical impulse in a dendrite of the next neuron in the spinal cord. The impulse then passes to a motor neuron, which carries it to your arm muscles, causing you to pull your hand away. This entire process happens in a fraction of a second, protecting you from injury.

## Key points

- The nervous system is made up of an organised network of nerve cells or neurons, and is specialised for conducting information via electrical impulses from one part of the body to another.
- The central nervous system (CNS) consists of the brain and the spinal cord, while the peripheral nervous system (PNS) consists of cranial nerves arising from the brain and spinal nerves arising from the spinal cord.
- The brain is the main coordinating centre of the body. The fore-brain is the main thinking part, the hind-brain controls involuntary actions like blood pressure and salivation, and the cerebellum maintains posture and balance.
- A neuron acquires information at the dendritic tip, travels as an electrical impulse through the cell body and axon, and is converted into a chemical signal at the end of the axon to cross the synapse.
- Reflex actions, like touching a hot object, involve a rapid sequence of impulse transmission from receptor to muscle via the synapse, allowing for immediate response.
