# Human Circulatory System

The human body is a busy place, constantly working to keep you alive and active. To do this, your body needs a reliable way to deliver essential supplies like oxygen to your muscles and brain, and to carry away waste products like carbon dioxide. The system that handles this massive delivery job is the human circulatory system. It acts like a vast, interconnected network of highways and delivery trucks, ensuring that every single cell in your body gets exactly what it needs to function properly.

## Blood: The Transport Fluid

At the heart of this transport system is blood. You might already know that blood is a fluid connective tissue, but it is much more than just a liquid. It is a complex mixture that has two main parts. The first part is a fluid medium called plasma, and the second part consists of the cells suspended within that plasma. The plasma is the liquid base of the blood. Its main job is to transport food, carbon dioxide, and nitrogenous wastes in a dissolved form. It also carries many other substances, such as various salts, throughout the body. Oxygen, however, is carried mainly not by the plasma but by the red blood corpuscles, which are the cells floating inside the plasma. Together, the plasma and the suspended cells form a highly efficient transport fluid that keeps the body running smoothly.

## The Pump: The Human Heart

To push this blood through the vast network of tubes, the body needs a powerful pumping organ. That organ is the heart. The heart is a muscular organ, and it is about as big as your fist. Because the blood has to transport both oxygen and carbon dioxide, the heart is specially designed with different chambers. These chambers are crucial because they prevent the oxygen-rich blood from mixing with the blood that contains carbon dioxide. The carbon dioxide-rich blood needs to reach the lungs so the carbon dioxide can be removed, and the oxygenated blood from the lungs needs to be brought back to the heart. Once the blood is oxygen-rich, the heart pumps it out to the rest of the body.

You can follow this process step by step. Oxygen-rich blood from the lungs enters the thin-walled upper chamber of the heart on the left, which is called the left atrium. When the left atrium is collecting this blood, it relaxes. Then, it contracts, while the next chamber, the left ventricle, relaxes. This allows the blood to be transferred into the left ventricle. When the muscular left ventricle contracts in its turn, it pumps the blood out to the rest of the body. Meanwhile, de-oxygenated blood comes from the body into the upper chamber on the right, the right atrium, as it relaxes. As the right atrium contracts, the corresponding lower chamber, the right ventricle, dilates. This transfers the blood to the right ventricle, which then pumps it to the lungs to get oxygenated. Because the ventricles have to pump blood into various organs, they have much thicker muscular walls than the atria do. To make sure the blood flows in the right direction, valves ensure that blood does not flow backwards when the atria or ventricles contract.

## The Network: Blood Vessels

The heart is the pump, but it needs a network of tubes to reach every part of the body. These tubes are called blood vessels. Arteries are the vessels that carry blood away from the heart to the various organs of the body. Since the blood emerges from the heart under high pressure, the arteries have thick, elastic walls to handle this force. Veins, on the other hand, collect the blood from different organs and bring it back to the heart. They do not need thick walls because the blood is no longer under high pressure by the time it reaches the veins. Instead, veins have valves that ensure the blood flows only in one direction back to the heart.

On reaching an organ or tissue, an artery divides into smaller and smaller vessels to bring the blood in contact with all the individual cells. The smallest of these vessels have walls that are only one cell thick, and they are called capillaries. The exchange of material between the blood and the surrounding cells takes place across this very thin wall. After the exchange, the capillaries join together to form veins, which then convey the blood away from the organ or tissue.

## The Fluid Backup: Lymph

There is another type of fluid also involved in transportation. This is called lymph, or tissue fluid. Through the pores present in the walls of the capillaries, some amount of plasma, proteins, and blood cells escape into the intercellular spaces in the tissues to form the tissue fluid or lymph. It is similar to the plasma of blood but is colourless and contains less protein. Lymph drains into lymphatic capillaries from the intercellular spaces. These capillaries join to form large lymph vessels that finally open into larger veins. The lymph carries digested and absorbed fat from the intestine and drains excess fluid from the extra cellular space back into the blood.

## Maintenance: Blood Clotting and Blood Pressure

What happens if this system of tubes develops a leak? Think about situations when you are injured and start bleeding. Naturally, the loss of blood from the system has to be minimised. In addition, a leakage would lead to a loss of pressure, which would reduce the efficiency of the pumping system. To avoid this, the blood has platelet cells. These platelet cells circulate around the body and plug these leaks by helping to clot the blood at the points of injury.

The force that blood exerts against the wall of a vessel is called blood pressure. This pressure is much greater in arteries than in veins. The pressure of blood inside the artery during ventricular systole (contraction) is called systolic pressure, and the pressure in the artery during ventricular diastole (relaxation) is called diastolic pressure. The normal systolic pressure is about 120 mm of Hg and diastolic pressure is 80 mm of Hg. Blood pressure is measured with an instrument called a sphygmomanometer. High blood pressure is also called hypertension. It is caused by the constriction of arterioles, which results in increased resistance to blood flow. It can lead to the rupture of an artery and internal bleeding.

## Worked example

To understand how blood moves through the heart, let us look at a simple rule: the ventricles have thicker muscular walls than the atria do. This is because the ventricles have to pump blood into various organs, whereas the atria only receive blood from the lungs or the body. 

Imagine a student is studying the heart. They learn that the left ventricle has a very thick wall. Why? Because it has to pump blood out to the entire body, which is a long distance and requires a lot of force. The left atrium, however, only collects blood from the lungs and passes it down to the left ventricle, which is just below it. Therefore, the left atrium only needs a thin wall to relax and collect the blood and then contract gently to move it into the ventricle. A thick, heavy wall like the one on the left ventricle would be of no use there. This shows how the structure of the heart matches its function.

## Key points

*   Blood is a fluid connective tissue consisting of plasma and suspended cells, with plasma carrying dissolved food and waste, and red blood corpuscles carrying oxygen.
*   The heart is a muscular, fist-sized pump with four chambers (left atrium, left ventricle, right atrium, right ventricle) separated to prevent oxygenated and de-oxygenated blood from mixing.
*   Arteries carry blood away from the heart under high pressure and have thick, elastic walls, while veins bring blood back to the heart and have valves to ensure one-way flow.
*   Capillaries are the smallest blood vessels with one-cell-thick walls, allowing the exchange of materials between the blood and surrounding cells.
*   Lymph is a colourless fluid similar to plasma that drains excess fluid from tissues and carries digested fats from the intestine.
*   Platelet cells in the blood help to clot blood and plug leaks in the vessel walls when injuries occur.
*   Blood pressure is the force exerted against vessel walls, measured as 120 mm of Hg systolic and 80 mm of Hg diastolic, and high blood pressure (hypertension) can be caused by the constriction of arterioles.
