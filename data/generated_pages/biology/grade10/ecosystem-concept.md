# Ecosystem — Concept

An ecosystem is the fundamental unit of ecology where all living organisms interact with each other and their physical surroundings to maintain a balance in nature. Understanding this concept is crucial because it explains how energy flows through different organisms and how essential nutrients are recycled to sustain life. When you visit a garden, you are looking at a small-scale ecosystem where living organisms interact with non-living factors like soil and sunlight.

## Biotic and Abiotic Components

An ecosystem consists of two main categories of components: biotic and abiotic. The biotic components comprise all the living organisms in an area, such as plants, animals, and microorganisms. The abiotic components comprise the physical factors like temperature, rainfall, wind, soil, and minerals. All the interacting organisms in an area together with the non-living constituents of the environment form an ecosystem. For example, if you visit a garden, you will find different plants, such as grasses, trees, and flower-bearing plants, as well as animals like frogs, insects, and birds. All these living organisms interact with each other, and their growth, reproduction, and other activities are affected by the abiotic components of the ecosystem.

## Types of Ecosystems

Ecosystems can be broadly classified based on their origin into natural and human-made ecosystems. Natural ecosystems are those that occur in nature without human intervention. Forests, ponds, and lakes are natural ecosystems. On the other hand, human-made ecosystems are created by human activities to serve specific purposes. Gardens and crop-fields are examples of human-made (artificial) ecosystems. 

## Producers, Consumers and Decomposers

In an ecosystem, organisms can be grouped as producers, consumers, and decomposers according to the manner in which they obtain their sustenance from the environment. Producers are organisms that make organic compounds like sugar and starch from inorganic substances using the radiant energy of the Sun in the presence of chlorophyll. All green plants and certain bacteria which can produce food by photosynthesis come under this category and are called the producers. Consumers are organisms which consume the food produced, either directly from producers or indirectly by feeding on other consumers. Consumers can be classed variously as herbivores, carnivores, omnivores, and parasites. Decomposers are microorganisms, comprising bacteria and fungi, that break down the dead remains and waste products of organisms. These microorganisms are the decomposers as they break down complex organic substances into simple inorganic substances that go into the soil and are used up once more by the plants.

The interactions among various components of the environment involve the flow of energy from one component of the system to another. Autotrophs capture the energy present in sunlight and convert it into chemical energy. This energy supports all the activities of the living world. From autotrophs, the energy goes to the heterotrophs and decomposers. However, when one form of energy is changed to another, some energy is lost to the environment in forms which cannot be used again.

## How the Parts Depend on One Another

The parts of an ecosystem cannot be separated from one another. Producers are the only group that captures the energy of sunlight, so every other organism depends on them, either directly or through other consumers. Consumers cannot make their own food, so they must feed on producers or on other consumers. Decomposers feed on dead remains and waste products, and in doing so they turn complex organic substances back into simple inorganic substances. These substances go into the soil and are used up once more by plants, so the materials keep cycling.

The abiotic components set the conditions for all of this. Temperature, rainfall, wind, soil and minerals affect the growth, reproduction and other activities of the living organisms. In a garden, for example, the soil supplies minerals to the plants, the plants are eaten by insects, the insects may be eaten by frogs or birds, and when any of them dies, bacteria and fungi break down its remains.

Notice the difference between how materials and energy behave. Materials such as the simple inorganic substances are used again and again. Energy is different. It flows from the Sun to the autotrophs, then to the heterotrophs and decomposers, and at every change of form some of it is lost to the environment and cannot be used again. This is why an ecosystem needs a constant supply of sunlight to keep running.

## Worked Example

Consider a small pond ecosystem. The producers in this pond are the aquatic plants that perform photosynthesis. The primary consumers are the small fish that feed directly on these plants. The secondary consumers are the larger fish that feed on the smaller fish. The tertiary consumers are the birds that catch and eat the larger fish. If the decomposers, such as bacteria and fungi, were absent, the dead remains of these organisms would not break down into simple inorganic substances. The producers could not take these substances up again, so the materials they need would run out and the whole pond ecosystem would be disrupted.

## Key points

- An ecosystem consists of biotic components comprising living organisms and abiotic components comprising physical factors like temperature, rainfall, wind, soil, and minerals.
- Ecosystems are classified as natural (e.g., forests, ponds, lakes) or human-made (e.g., gardens, crop-fields).
- Organisms in an ecosystem are grouped as producers, consumers, and decomposers according to how they obtain their food.
- Energy flows from autotrophs to heterotrophs and decomposers, but some energy is lost to the environment during this transfer.
- Decomposers are essential for breaking down dead remains and waste products into simple inorganic substances that are used by plants.
