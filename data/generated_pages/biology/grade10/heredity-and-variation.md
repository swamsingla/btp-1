# Heredity and Variation

When living organisms reproduce, they pass on traits to their offspring. This transfer of traits from one generation to the next is called heredity. At the same time, the offspring often show subtle differences from their parents. These differences are called variations. Together, heredity and variation ensure that a species can maintain its basic body design while also adapting to changing environments over time.

## The Source of Heredity: DNA and Genes

The mechanism of heredity is controlled by cellular DNA. DNA is the information source for making proteins in the cell. A section of DNA that provides information for one protein is called a gene. Genes control the characteristics, or traits, that we observe in an organism. For example, plant height can depend on the amount of a particular plant hormone. The amount of this hormone is determined by the efficiency of the enzyme that produces it. If a gene for that enzyme works efficiently, the plant will be tall. If the gene has an alteration that makes the enzyme less efficient, the amount of hormone will be less, and the plant will be short. Thus, genes control characteristics.

## How Traits are Expressed and Inherited

For sexual reproduction to work, both parents must contribute equally to the DNA of the offspring. This means that each parent must contribute a copy of the same gene. Therefore, each offspring must have two sets of all genes, one inherited from each parent. For this mechanism to work, each germ cell must have only one set of genes.

Each set of genes is present, not as a single long thread of DNA, but as separate independent pieces, each called a chromosome. Thus, each cell will have two copies of each chromosome, one each from the male and female parents. Every germ cell will take one chromosome from each pair, and these may be of either maternal or paternal origin. When two germ cells combine during fertilization, they restore the normal number of chromosomes in the offspring, ensuring the stability of the DNA of the species.

## Where Variation Comes From

Variation arises during the process of reproduction. The copying of DNA is not perfectly accurate, and small inaccuracies in the copying produce slightly different copies of the DNA. These differences are passed on to the offspring, and some of them can be inherited by the next generation as well. In sexual reproduction, the two parents contribute different combinations of genes, so the offspring differ from both parents and from one another.

## The Importance of Variation

While the consistency of DNA copying is important for the maintenance of body design features, variations are also useful for the survival of species over time. Populations of organisms fill well-defined places, or niches, in the ecosystem, using their ability to reproduce. However, niches can change because of reasons beyond the control of the organisms. Temperatures on earth can go up or down, water levels can vary, or there could be meteorite hits. If a population of reproducing organisms were suited to a particular niche and if the niche were drastically altered, the population could be wiped out. However, if some variations were to be present in a few individuals in these populations, there would be some chance for them to survive. For example, if there were a population of bacteria living in temperate waters, and if the water temperature were to be increased by global warming, most of these bacteria would die, but the few variants resistant to heat would survive and grow further. Selection of variants by environmental factors forms the basis for evolutionary processes.

## Worked Example: Keeping the Chromosome Number Steady

Suppose, for simplicity, that a species has body cells with 8 chromosomes, which are 4 pairs, one chromosome of each pair from each parent.

1. Each germ cell takes one chromosome from each pair, so each germ cell has 4 chromosomes. This is one set.
2. The male germ cell and the female germ cell each bring 4 chromosomes.
3. When the two germ cells combine during fertilisation, the new cell has $4 + 4 = 8$ chromosomes.

So the offspring has the same number of chromosomes as its parents, with 4 from each parent. If germ cells carried all 8 chromosomes instead, the offspring would have $8 + 8 = 16$, and the number would double in every generation. This is why every germ cell must have only one set of genes.

## Key points

*   Heredity is the process by which traits and characteristics are reliably inherited from parents to offspring.
*   DNA is the information source for making proteins, and genes are sections of DNA that control specific traits.
*   Each cell has two copies of each chromosome, one from each parent, but germ cells have only one copy of each chromosome.
*   Variations arise due to small inaccuracies in DNA copying and are essential for the survival of species when the environment changes.
*   Traits are expressed based on the efficiency of the proteins produced by genes, which can affect the amount of hormones or other substances in the cell.
