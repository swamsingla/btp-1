# Cell — Basic Unit of Life

Every living thing you see around you, from the tallest tree to the smallest ant, is built from tiny building blocks called cells. A cell is the smallest living unit; a single cell contains the components that help an organism perform its life functions. Understanding cells is important because they are the foundation of all biology; they grow, take in nutrients, and reproduce to ensure life continues. Without cells, there would be no plants, animals, or microorganisms to share our planet.

## Cell Theory

The idea that all living things are made of cells is known as cell theory. The theory states that all living beings are made up of one or more cells. It also tells us that the cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life. This means that the work of a living organism is carried out by its cells, whether it has one cell or many. 

The cell theory applies to every living thing. It includes the tiny bacteria in our soil and the massive whales in the ocean. By understanding this theory, we can see that a single-celled organism is a complete living unit, while in larger organisms many cells work together.

## Unicellular and Multicellular Organisms

Organisms can be grouped into two main categories based on how many cells they have. Some organisms, like certain bacteria and protozoa, are made up of just one cell. These are called unicellular organisms. A single cell carries out all the functions necessary for its survival. 

Other organisms, including all plants and animals, are made up of many cells. These are called multicellular organisms. In multicellular organisms, cells carry out specialised functions individually but also cooperate with each other to increase the chance of survival. 

Think of a large factory. In a factory, different workers have different jobs, such as cutting, assembling, and packaging. Similarly, in a multicellular organism, some cells are responsible for movement, while others are responsible for digestion. They all work together to keep the whole organism alive.

## The Building Blocks of Life

Cells have different shapes and structures depending on the job they need to do. The unique shape, size, and structure of cells help them carry out their specific functions. For example, nerve cells are very long and have branches. This elongated shape and branched structure help them reach different parts of the body and pass on messages quickly. Muscle cells, on the other hand, are thin and spindle-shaped. This shape allows them to contract and relax easily. 

Cells are surrounded by a cell membrane, which acts as a protective barrier. Some cells, like those of fungi and plants, also have a cell wall for extra support. Not all cells look the same under a microscope. Bacteria, for example, do not have a well-defined nucleus and a nuclear membrane. Instead, they have a nucleoid. This feature distinguishes them from the cells of yeast, protozoa, algae, fungi, plants, and animals. 

## Observing Cells

To observe the tiny structures inside a cell, scientists use microscopes. A standard light microscope allows us to see the basic shape and size of a cell. However, to see the subcellular components inside a cell, we need microscopes with high magnification. An electron microscope magnifies the cell about 10,00,000 times (1 million times). 

Cells differ in size, shape, and structure. Plant and animal cells also have some differences. Understanding these differences helps us learn how these organisms function differently. For example, plant cells have a cell wall and chloroplasts, which allow them to make their own food through photosynthesis. Animal cells do not have chloroplasts, so they must obtain food from other sources.

## Worked Example

Let us apply the idea of cell specialisation to a real-world situation. Imagine a multicellular organism, like a human, trying to digest a piece of bread. The digestive system is made up of different types of cells. Muscle cells in the food pipe are thin, flexible, and spindle-shaped. They contract and relax in a wave-like manner, pushing the food down to the stomach. Other cells in the stomach produce digestive juices and acid to break down the food. All these cells work together to make digestion possible. If one type of cell stopped working, digestion would be disturbed.

## Key points
- All living beings, including microorganisms, are made up of one or more cells.
- A cell is the basic building block of living things and contains the components that help an organism perform its functions.
- Unicellular organisms, like bacteria, carry out all functions in a single cell.
- Multicellular organisms, like plants and animals, have many cells that specialise and cooperate.
- The shape and structure of a cell relate to its specific function.
- An electron microscope magnifies the cell about 10,00,000 times (1 million times) to see subcellular components.
- Bacteria have a nucleoid instead of a well-defined nucleus and nuclear membrane.
