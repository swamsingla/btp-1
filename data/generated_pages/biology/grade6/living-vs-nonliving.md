# Living and Non-Living Things

Everything around you is either alive or not alive. Understanding the difference between living and non-living things is one of the most important ideas in biology. It helps you make sense of the world and see how humans, animals, and plants are connected to each other.

## The Problem with Movement

You might think that if something moves, it must be alive. Many things move on their own: cars, buses, trains, birds and animals all do. However, movement alone is not a reliable way to tell living from non-living. For example, cars move on the road, but they are not alive. You are alive because you can do many things that a car cannot do. A car does not grow, while you do. When you compare yourself with a car, you can see that you change over time, but the car does not.

Even plants, which do not move from one place to another, are living. They still show movement. For example, the leaves of a touch-me-not plant fold inward when you touch them. Insectivorous plants like Drosera have saucer-shaped leaves that trap insects. When an insect lands on the leaf, the hairs move inward and catch it. Climbers also wind themselves around nearby objects. So, movement does not prove that something is living, because non-living things can move too.

## Growth

Growth is a clear sign of life. Think about yourself four years ago. You probably cannot wear the same clothes now because your body has become larger. This happens because you are growing. Plants and other living beings also grow. They need food, air, and water to grow and develop. 

Imagine a young sapling that is only a few centimetres tall. Over the next year, with water and sunlight, it might become a tall tree. A non-living thing like a rock or a pencil does not grow larger on its own. It might break into pieces, but it does not increase in size through a life process.

## Respiration

Breathing is part of a process called respiration. When you inhale, air moves from outside your body to inside. When you breathe out, air moves from inside to outside. You can count your breaths per minute after a normal walk, a run, or a few dance steps to see this in action. You will notice that you breathe faster after running because your body needs more air. Dogs, cats, cows, and buffaloes also respire. You can see the movement of their abdomen when they are resting. 

Plants also respire. They have tiny pores called stomata on the surface of their leaves. These pores help plants take air in and out. All living beings respire to get the energy they need to stay alive.

## Excretion

Living beings produce waste. For example, when you sweat during the summer, you lose water and salts from your body. This removal of waste products is called excretion. Animals also excrete waste in the form of urine. Plants also excrete. You might see small droplets of excess water and minerals on the leaves of grasses and roses. All living beings excrete waste to keep their bodies clean and healthy.

## Response to Stimuli

A stimulus is anything or any event that prompts a living being to respond. For example, if you accidentally step on a sharp thorn or touch a hot cup of tea, you will instantly pull your foot or hand away. This instant reaction is called a response to stimuli. 

Plants also respond to stimuli. The touch-me-not plant folds its leaves when you touch it. Certain plants also fold their leaves after sunset. The leaves of amla trees tend to come together at night. They do this because they respond to the change in light. All living beings respond to stimuli in their environment.

## Reproduction

Reproduction is the process of producing new ones of one's own kind. You have seen young ones of cats, dogs, and other animals. They grow up and eventually have their own young ones. This is necessary for the continuity of life. If you look for young ones of non-living things, like a pencil, a chair, or an electric bulb, you will not find any. All living beings reproduce.

## The End of Life

When a living being is not able to exhibit all of these characteristics, even when food, air, and water are available, it is said to be dead. All living beings eventually die. A thing that shows none of these features—no growth, no respiration, no excretion, no response to stimuli and no reproduction—is non-living.

## Worked Example

You can use these rules to classify things around you. Let us test a seed. Is a seed living or non-living? A seed sitting in a packet does not appear to do anything at all. But give it water, air and warmth and it germinates: it respires, it takes in nutrients, and it grows into a plant, and that plant will in time produce seeds of its own. Because it carries out these life processes, a seed is a living thing.

## Key points

- Movement is not a reliable test for life because non-living things like cars can move, and plants show movement without changing location.
- Living beings grow in size and change over time with the help of food, air, and water.
- All living beings respire to exchange air, taking it in and letting it out through pores or lungs.
- Living beings excrete waste products like sweat, urine, or water droplets to stay healthy.
- Living beings respond to stimuli, such as touching a hot object or a plant folding its leaves when touched.
- All living beings reproduce to create new life and continue their species.
- The absence of these characteristics means a thing is non-living or dead.
